# The Realist's Creed

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** May 31, 2002 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2002-05-31-the-realists-creed.pdf)

## Summary

A slightly revised version of an article Marks wrote for Trusts & Estates magazine (published April 2002). Rather than give an asset-allocation recipe, he lays out the 'mental arsenal' a fiduciary needs because investing is hard. He contrasts the 'I know' school, which builds portfolios from macro forecasts, with the 'I don't know' school he belongs to, and lists seven principles: a firm belief system (contrarianism, skepticism, modest expectations, humility, defensive investing), distrust of forecasts, the cyclicality of everything, the role of timeframe, the primacy of valuation, avoiding the silver bullet, and awareness of investor psychology. He applies them to common stocks, forecasting single-digit returns after the 1978-99 run, and to hedge funds, which he expects to become the next fad with fund flows, fees and weaker managers eroding returns.

## Key Ideas

1. The 'I know' school chains macro forecasts into stock picks; the 'I don't know' school focuses on knowing more about micro situations and on what can go wrong.
2. Consensus forecasts are already in prices and mostly extrapolate the present; the profitable surprises are by definition hard to foresee.
3. Everything is cyclical: extrapolating today's events is among the most dangerous things for investors; the tech bubble ignored the economic, credit and corporate life cycles.
4. Timeframe matters: the six-foot man who drowned crossing a stream five feet deep on average; markets can stay irrational longer than you can stay solvent.
5. Success comes from 'buying things well', not 'buying good things'; good companies bought too high produce big losses.
6. Mainstream stock markets are relatively efficient, so most active managers add little; think of a coin flip, not a diligent paperboy.
7. Equity returns = profit growth + multiple expansion + dividend yield; after 1978-99, expect single-digit returns.
8. Hedge fund caveats: returns rarely 'absolute', money flows degrade records, the best close and the rest get funded, fees eat skill.

## Market Context

Spring 2002, after the technology/telecom bubble burst: the NASDAQ had fallen since 2000, capital was denied to start-ups in 2000-02, Enron had collapsed, and investors disappointed by equities were migrating into hedge funds marketed as 'absolute return' vehicles.

## Notable Claims

- S&P 500 averaged 17.6% a year from 1978 through 1999.
- Returns were 20.6% for 1991-99 and 28.3% for 1995-99.
- Stocks averaged about 10% a year between 1930 and 1990.
- Steady low-correlation pre-fee returns of 12-15% are rare; 20% is Herculean.
- Through 1999, the S&P 500 had no year with a 5% loss since 1978.

## Quotes

> It's easy for most people to tell the difference between a good company and a bad one, but much harder for them to understand the difference between a cheap stock and an expensive one

> When the best are closed, the rest will be funded

> And there's little that's as dangerous for investor health as insistence on extrapolating today's events into the future.
