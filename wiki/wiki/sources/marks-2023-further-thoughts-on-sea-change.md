# Further Thoughts on Sea Change

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** October 11, 2023 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/further-thoughts-on-sea-change3dda4540-569f-4415-9a5b-004fbabf06d4.pdf)

## Summary

A follow-up to 'Sea Change' (December 2022), first sent to clients on May 30, 2023 and released publicly in October 2023. Marks argues that the end of ultra-low rates is not a normal cyclical swing but a sweeping change that warrants major capital reallocation toward credit. He calls the roughly 2,000-basis-point fall in rates between 1980 and 2020 the most important financial event of recent decades, overlooked like a slowly boiled frog or a moving walkway. He criticises the Fed for keeping rates near zero for nearly seven years after 2008, arguing that the lack of a free market in money distorted behaviour, as seen in Silicon Valley Bank and First Republic. With rates unlikely to return to 2009-21 lows, asset ownership and leverage should help less, while lending now offers contractual, equity-like returns: high yield above 8.5% and leveraged loans near 10% versus the S&P 500's roughly 10% century average. He weighs five downsides of a credit-heavy allocation and concludes that credit could be a substantial portion, perhaps the majority, of portfolios.

## Key Ideas

1. Templeton allowed that 'this time is different' might be true 20% of the time; Marks argues the post-2022 rate regime is such a case.
2. The 40-year interest rate decline drove most investment profits and benefited asset owners and borrowers, yet investors overlooked it.
3. Keeping the fed funds rate near zero for almost seven years after the GFC was a mistake that distorted risk-taking ('the worst of loans are made in the best of times').
4. Levered strategies such as private equity grew almost entirely in a falling-rate era; investors may have confused brains with a bull market.
5. With credit yields at 8-10%, non-investment grade debt now offers returns near equities' historic average, and these returns are contractual.
6. Credit returns depend on the borrower's contract, not on Mr. Market's mood, and money-good bonds are anchored by pull to par.
7. Institutions tend to make token 2% shifts; a true sea change may justify making credit a majority of a portfolio.
8. The transition is incomplete: a tug-of-war between buyers and sellers has left the S&P 500 roughly flat over the year.

## Market Context

Written in May 2023 (market data as of May 30, 2023) and republished October 11, 2023. The Fed had just completed its fastest tightening cycle in four decades after post-Covid inflation, Silicon Valley Bank and First Republic had failed, recession was widely expected, and the S&P 500 was within half a percent of its level a year earlier.

## Notable Claims

- Black Monday 1987 saw the Dow fall 22.6% in one day.
- High yield yielded in the 4% range in early 2022 and more than 8% now.
- Credit indices offer yields near or above equities' historic returns.
- The S&P 500 has returned just over 10% a year for almost a century.
- The Fed held rates near zero for nearly seven years after late 2008.
