# Dare to Be Great

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** September 7, 2006 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2006-09-07-dare-to-be-great.pdf)

## Summary

Written for institutional clients, this memo argues that superior results require unconventional, non-consensus behavior and superior judgment, and that no formula for success can exist. Marks introduces a 2-by-2 matrix: conventional behavior produces average results, good or bad, while only unconventional behavior can produce above- or below-average results. He illustrates the value of being early with Oaktree's start in high yield bonds in 1978 and distressed debt in 1988, and with contrarian buys such as Tumi after 9/11. He then turns to the institutional obstacles: groupthink in committees, agency risk, consultants used as cover, egalitarian portfolio management, over-diversification and peer-mirroring. He closes with the tension between his own preference for cautious managers and the need for boldness in allocating to them.

## Key Ideas

1. You cannot take the same actions as everyone else and expect to outperform; only unconventional behavior with superior judgment yields above-average results.
2. Median manager returns in venture capital, buyouts and real estate are unexciting, so superior results depend on picking superior managers.
3. Market inefficiency often lives in niches that most people do not know, understand or feel comfortable with, so pioneers get the biggest rewards.
4. Committees tend to average their members' insight and avoid idiosyncratic positions, so they rarely produce superior results without real intellectual friction.
5. Agency risk makes hired decision-makers care more about avoiding blame than achieving gains; incentives should reward attempts at nonconformity.
6. Over-diversifying, capping a manager's share of AUM, moderating and mirroring peer allocations all ensure you cannot win as well as cannot lose.
7. Marks prefers cautious managers but argues the allocator must be bold, for example by giving more capital to aggressive asset classes through risk-controlling managers.

## Market Context

September 2006, a period of abundant capital in which, as Marks puts it, everyone was willing to do anything to make money and assumed that the more exotic the concept, the higher the return. He judged that few free lunches remained and that risk-taking was likely to earn sub-par rewards. The prompt was reading Barton Biggs's "Hedgehogging".

## Notable Claims

- Buyout funds 1980-97 did no better than the S&P 500 despite leverage.
- The 1999 growth-value divergence was the largest ever.
- A concentrated pension commitment to a new Oaktree strategy gained 118% after fees in three years.
- Oaktree bought Tumi in 2002 and sold it in 2004.
- Oaktree's high yield portfolios beat high grade bonds for over two decades.

## Quotes

> This just in: you can’t take the same actions as everyone else and expect to outperform.

> I like to say Oaktree buys when people say “no way” and sells when they say “no sweat.”

> Especially in terms of asset allocation, “can’t lose” usually goes hand-in-hand with “can’t win.”
