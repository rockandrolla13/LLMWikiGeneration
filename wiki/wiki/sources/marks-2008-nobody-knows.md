# Nobody Knows

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** September 19, 2008 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2008-09-19-nobody-knows.pdf)

## Summary

Written days after the Lehman failure, this memo argues that no one knows what the crisis means or what comes next, because the excesses and the financial inventions involved were unprecedented. Marks frames the crisis as boom and bust: a decades-long bubble in the financial sector, fed by excess liquidity, low rates, reaching for return, leverage, structured products, rating-agency reliance and slogans such as 'home prices never fall nationally'. He notes environmental changes such as the repeal of Glass-Steagall, the end of the uptick rule, derivatives feeding back into underlying prices, and mark-to-market accounting. He warns that markets were taking their lead from falling prices and CDS spreads, which can become self-fulfilling. His central decision rule is that investors must assume the world will not end, because the meltdown case cannot be planned for, and should hunt for bargains in the third stage of the bear market.

## Key Ideas

1. Opinions about the future are not knowledge, and the novelty of the 2008 excesses made forecasting even less reliable.
2. If you have a boom you will have a bust; the 2003-07 boom was in the financial sector rather than in the real economy or stocks.
3. Price declines can feed on themselves, and rising CDS prices can act as self-fulfilling prophecies against institutions.
4. Treat the meltdown question as binary: since you cannot act usefully on 'the world ends', assume it does not and invest accordingly.
5. Opaque, levered, short-funded financial institutions are vulnerable to a loss of confidence and may be unanalysable (Lehman had about two million swaps).
6. Bull and bear markets each have three stages; the third stage of a bear market lets you buy at prices reflecting little or no optimism.
7. The cause of a recovery cannot be predicted; assets may simply become so cheap they cannot stay down.
8. At extremes, people accept as true things that have never held before, such as the safety of engineered leverage or the non-viability of the whole financial sector.

## Market Context

September 2008, the peak of the global financial crisis: the failure or rescue of Lehman Brothers, Bear Stearns, Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac and AIG; doubts about Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley; T-bill yields near zero; and rising CDS prices on US Treasuries.

## Notable Claims

- Galbraith's two kinds of forecasters.
- Glass-Steagall was repealed in 1999.
- The uptick rule was revoked in July 2007.
- Lehman had about two million interest-rate swaps (quoting WSJ).
- Contrarianism beats trend following now.

## Quotes

> If you have a boom, eventually you’ll have a bust.

> Maybe things will just get so cheap that they can’t stay down.

> But heroes aren’t people who’re unafraid, but rather those who act bravely despite their fears.
