# The Most Important Thing

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** July 1, 2003 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2003-07-01-the-most-important-thing.pdf)

## Summary

Marks collects in one place the precepts that guide Oaktree, each framed as 'the most important thing'. The investment precepts are: price relative to value comes first, intrinsic value must be estimated rigorously and held with conviction, invest defensively and avoid losers, avoid bad years rather than chase the market in good ones, accept the limits of macro forecasting, stay mindful of cycles, behave as a contrarian, wait patiently for opportunities to come to you, work only in inefficient markets, and be wary of leverage. The business precepts are: say what you will do and do it, keep tactical flexibility, refuse to manage too much money, acknowledge uncontrollable factors and the gap between probabilities and outcomes, communicate candidly, keep staff turnover low by being fair, work hard to keep partnerships intact, and stand for something other than assets under management. He counts eighteen points and adds a nineteenth: never think it will be easy. The memo is the seed of his later book of the same name.

## Key Ideas

1. Price relative to value is the starting point: no asset is so good it cannot be a bad investment at too high a price, and few are so bad they cannot be good investments if cheap enough.
2. Conviction in a rigorously derived intrinsic value lets a manager buy more as price falls, but an incorrect opinion held with conviction is very dangerous.
3. Defensive investing ('invest scared'): avoid losers and bad years, insist on a margin of safety, diversify, and avoid relying on macro forecasts or timing; it is good enough to be average in good times and essential to beat the market in bad times.
4. Investors must choose between the 'I know' and 'I don't know' schools about the macro future; Marks belongs to the latter and treats survival as the prerequisite of success.
5. Cycles are inevitable and price swings overstate fundamental swings; extrapolating trends is one of the most dangerous things an investor can do.
6. Only unpopular assets can be truly cheap; distressed debt profits partly because no one loves a distressed company.
7. In inefficient markets, risk and return need not be tightly correlated, so low risk need not mean low return; in efficient markets few beat the benchmark.
8. Leverage raises both prospective return and risk, eroding margin of safety; asset growth beyond a point degrades performance, so turning away money matters.

## Market Context

Mid-2003, after the late-1990s tech bubble had burst. The memo is a statement of philosophy rather than a market call. It references tech stocks bought without regard to valuation in the late 1990s and the shunning of junk bonds in the 1970s and early 1980s as examples of ignoring price.

## Notable Claims

- Oaktree has retained 100% of its senior partners since 1983.
- Sheldon Stone has run the high yield bond portfolios since 1985.
- Oaktree was formed in 1995.
- Marks decided 25 years earlier to work only in markets he believes are inefficient.
- Oaktree has limited strategies and turned away money for twenty years.
