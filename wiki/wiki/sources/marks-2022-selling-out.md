# Selling Out

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** January 13, 2022 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/selling-out.pdf)

## Summary

Marks's first memo devoted entirely to selling, building on 'Liquidity' (2015) and 'Something of Value' (January 2021). He argues that most people sell for the wrong reasons, either because an asset is up (to lock in gains and avoid regret) or because it is down (fearing further loss). The second does more damage, can turn declines into crashes, and is the source of distressed-debt bargains. Evidence of behavioural error includes mutual fund investors underperforming the funds they own and the rise of passive investing, which he attributes to bad active management. Good reasons to sell come from the investment's outlook, not the investor's psyche: a weakened thesis, or a better risk-adjusted alternative under Sidney Cottle's 'discipline of relative selection' and opportunity cost. Selling for market timing adds two or three ways to be wrong, and position-size limits are matters of judgement that optimisation models can't settle. Above all, staying invested and letting compounding work is 'the most important thing'. Reducing exposure through ill-conceived selling is a cardinal sin.

## Key Ideas

1. People mainly sell because assets are up or because they are down, and both are driven by psychology (regret, embarrassment, fear) rather than analysis.
2. Realised gains are not more durable than unrealised ones, since sale proceeds are reinvested and put back at risk.
3. Selling because prices fell can turn declines into crashes and is the mistake that supplies distressed-debt buyers with bargains.
4. Mutual fund investors underperform the funds they own by chasing winners and dumping laggards; passive investing grew because active management did badly.
5. Legitimate selling rests on a weakened thesis or a superior alternative, judged by 'the discipline of relative selection' and opportunity cost.
6. Selling to time a dip gives two or three ways to be wrong (whether the dip comes, when to re-enter, what to hold meanwhile).
7. Investors diversify to guard against what they don't know; position limits are legitimate but can only be set by judgement, because no single number captures risk.
8. 'Time, not timing': returns are concentrated in a few sharp bursts, so staying invested and compounding matters most; Oaktree's founding tenet is never to raise cash for timing.

## Market Context

January 2022, after the strong 2020-21 recovery from the pandemic decline, with highly appreciated growth stocks (e.g., Amazon at $3,304) raising the question of whether to take profits.

## Notable Claims

- Amazon was up 660x from $5 at the start of 1998 to $3,304, with a 93% fall in 2001.
- The S&P 500's estimated 90-year compound return is 10.5% a year, turning $1 into about $8,000.
- In 1999-2018 the S&P 500 returned 5.6% a year, but only 2.0% if one missed the 10 best days.
- The Rindge family bought all 13,330 acres of Malibu in 1892 for $300,000.
- Bill Miller: US stocks rose in about 70% of post-war years.

## Quotes

> But it certainly doesn’t make sense to sell things just because they’re up.

> Superior investing consists largely of taking advantage of mistakes made by others.

> What’s clear to me is that simply being invested is by far “the most important thing.”
