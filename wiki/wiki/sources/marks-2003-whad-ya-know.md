# Whad’Ya Know?

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** March 11, 2003 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2003-03-11-whad-ya-know.pdf)

## Summary

A long 'rant' against the 'I know' school: commentators, strategists, forecasters and analysts who claim knowledge of the future they do not have. Marks debunks the 'common knowledge' that net selling builds cash on the sidelines, since every trade has a buyer and seller. He mocks after-the-fact market explanations, blames media 'experts' and brokerage strategists for feeding the 1998-99 TMT bubble, and notes that no P&I-surveyed manager predicted 2002's decline. He explains that conventional forecasts are usually right but unprofitable, while unconventional ones could pay but are usually wrong, so forecasts rarely help beat the market. Byron Wein's surprise list and Robert Prechter's extreme calls illustrate the problem. He also says credit rating agencies create the inefficiencies Oaktree exploits, warns against weak data such as the January effect, and doubts post-scandal 'independent research' will help retail investors, who are better served by index funds. He ends as a member of the 'I don't know' school: invest only where the worst case is tolerable.

## Key Ideas

1. 'Common knowledge' is often wrong: there is no net selling, and trading cannot create cash on the sidelines.
2. Daily market explanations are mostly useless; markets often move for reasons no one knows.
3. Brokerage strategists failed to call the TMT bubble owing to congenital bullishness, Wall Street's vested interests and real limits on foresight.
4. Conventional forecasts are usually right but already priced in; unconventional forecasts could profit but are usually wrong.
5. Rating agencies create market inefficiencies, for example by labelling B-rated bonds as lacking desirable investment characteristics regardless of price.
6. Small samples and old data (January effect, December-January rule, war rallies) confuse possibility, probability and certainty.
7. If a forecast were correct, it would not be given away for free.
8. Because the future is unknowable, invest only where the worst case is tolerable; consequences must dominate probabilities.

## Market Context

March 2003, at the tail of a three-year bear market after the TMT bubble burst, following 9/11, the 2001-02 corporate scandals (Enron, WorldCom), and the regulatory settlement with Wall Street research analysts. War with Iraq loomed, and star strategists such as Barton Biggs were leaving their posts.

## Notable Claims

- No stock manager in P&I's survey predicted a negative return for 2002.
- A West Coast manager predicted +15% for the S&P 500 in 2002; it fell 22%.
- None of the 100 equity funds most used by DC plans were profitable in 2002.
- Business Week's 'best' small companies underperformed after publication.
- There is no such thing as net selling.
