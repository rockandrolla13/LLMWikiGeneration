# The Value of Predictions, or Where'd All This Rain Come From?

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** February 15, 1993 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/1993-02-15-the-value-of-predictions-or-where-39-d-all-this-rain-come-from.pdf)

## Summary

Marks, then at TCW, sets out every objection he has to investing on the basis of macro and market forecasts. He argues that a forecast only adds value if it is both correct and different from the consensus, because consensus expectations are already in prices. Such non-consensus calls are rare, usually wrong, hardest to make at turning points, and hard to believe and hold through bad timing. Forecasts also cost money to act on, are seldom checked against a track record, and usually rest on a chain of conditional guesses whose joint probability is low. He concludes that his team will not manage portfolios on market forecasts and will instead try to 'know the knowable' in inefficient niches such as convertibles, high yield bonds and distressed debt.

## Key Ideas

1. A forecast's expected value is the payoff from being right times the probability of being right, and most forecasters have similar, average ability.
2. A correct consensus forecast earns only an average return because prices already reflect it. Only accurate non-consensus forecasts produce above-average results (2x2 payoff table).
3. Stock prices react to earnings surprises, not to earnings changes the consensus already anticipated.
4. Forecasters extrapolate the recent past and are most wrong at inflection points. A market bottom is the moment the last would-be seller has sold.
5. Timing matters as much as direction. Market timing is a source of risk, not protection.
6. Chained scenario forecasts compound error: five steps each 67% likely leave about a 13% chance that the whole scenario plays out.
7. Ask 'why me?': a truly valuable forecast would be traded on, not given away.
8. The alternative is to 'know the knowable' through deep work in inefficient markets (convertibles, high yield, distressed debt) without relying on macro forecasts.

## Market Context

Written in February 1993, after the 1989-1990 high yield bond collapse (recession, failed 1980s LBOs, stringent regulation, the fall of Drexel Burnham, Columbia Savings and Executive Life). The trigger was heavy rain in the American West after a five-year drought that newspapers had just suggested might last fifty years.

## Notable Claims

- Missing the best 50 of 744 months from 1926-1987 would have erased the whole equity return.
- Five 67% forecasts compound to a 13% joint probability.
- In 1978 forecasters bracketed rates at 7-9%, while the money went to those who foresaw 15% long bond yields.
- Non-consensus forecasts are both the profitable ones and the hardest to believe.
- High yield bonds took a beating in 1989-1990 from recession, LBO failures, regulation and the collapse of Drexel Burnham, Columbia Savings and Executive Life.

## Quotes

> Extreme predictions are rarely right, but they're the ones that make you big money.

> "being too far ahead of your time is indistinguishable from being wrong."

> attempts at market timing are a source of risk, not protection
