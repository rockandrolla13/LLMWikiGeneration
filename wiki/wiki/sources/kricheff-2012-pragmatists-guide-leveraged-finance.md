# A Pragmatist's Guide to Leveraged Finance: Credit Analysis for Bonds and Bank Debt

**Authors:** [[entities/robert-s-kricheff|Robert S. Kricheff]]

**Year:** 2012

**Publisher:** FT Press

**ISBN:** 978-0-13-285523-5 (ISBN-10: 0-13-285523-2)

**Converted text:** `markdown_output/kricheff-2012-pragmatists-guide-leveraged-finance.md`

## Summary

Kricheff gives a practitioner's guide to credit analysis of high-yield bonds and leveraged loans. He was head of Americas high-yield sector strategy at Credit Suisse. The book skips theory and teaches the working tools analysts use every day.

All analysis rests on two questions. Can the issuer service its debt from cash flow and other liquidity? If not, does asset value cover the debt? The book builds credit ratios on these questions: coverage, leverage, free cash flow to debt, and an equity cushion that compares leverage with peer enterprise value multiples.

A large share of the book covers structure. It explains coupon types, call schedules and clawbacks, ranking, structural subordination and guarantees, the main covenants, and how holders amend or waive them. The author argues that these terms decide how bonds in the same capital structure trade against each other, even far from default.

Later chapters apply the tools to news events, ownership, equity signals, relative value, new issues and bankruptcy recoveries. The book ends with a quick-snapshot checklist and an investment process. Each chapter closes with multiple-choice questions.

## Key Contributions

- A ratio toolkit with explicit construction rules: adjusted EBITDA, cash interest, (EBITDA - capex)/interest, total and net leverage, tranche-level leverage, market-adjusted debt and FCF/debt.
- An equity cushion that divides a peer EV/EBITDA multiple by leverage, so leverage can be compared across industries.
- Spread per turn of leverage using bond-level leverage, read together with total leverage, FCF/debt and the equity cushion.
- Worked mechanics of high-yield covenants: incurrence tests, restricted-payment baskets, change-of-control puts at 101, asset sales, maintenance headroom and restricted subsidiaries.
- How structural subordination and subsidiary guarantees let issuers prime existing notes.
- Event templates for acquisitions, takeovers, IPOs and maturities, including pro forma leverage and a blended takeout price.
- Bankruptcy recovery mechanics: accreted-value claims, voting and blocking stakes, fulcrum securities, and discounting recoveries by case length.

## How the Book Is Organised

- **Market, participants and instruments** — chapters 1-8. Defines the market, ratings, participants, loan features and price, yield and spread conventions.
- **Financial analysis and modeling** — chapters 9-12. Derives EBITDA, free cash flow and debt from statements, builds credit and operating ratios, and projects scenarios.
- **Structure, covenants and amendments** — chapters 13-17. Covers coupons, maturities and calls, ranking and structural subordination, covenants, and amendments, waivers and exchanges.
- **Events, ownership, equity and relative value** — chapters 18-22. Applies the tools to news events, ownership, equity-based valuation and monitoring, relative value sheets and new issues.
- **Distress and the decision process** — chapters 23-26. Covers bankruptcy claims and recoveries, the quick credit snapshot, the investment process and closing lessons.

## Chapter Guide

### Chapter 1. Introduction

**Question.** What is the leveraged finance market, how do issuers enter it, and where does credit analysis start?

**Method.** Two starting points for all credit analysis: liquidity (can operating cash or other sources pay interest and principal) and asset protection (if not, is there enough asset value to repay the debt). The analyst takes a cynical, downside-first stance and keeps the investment horizon in mind.

**Findings.**

- The author sizes the high-yield bond and loan market at over $2.3 trillion in North America, with further markets in Europe and emerging markets.
- Issuers enter the market as fallen angels, as first-time sub-investment-grade issuers, through leveraged buyouts, or as early-stage developmental companies.
- A high-yield bond that simply pays to maturity tends to beat many assets because of its coupon. So the analyst's main job is to avoid the downside.
- The author argues that almost no below-investment-grade company is in equilibrium. Each is improving, transforming, being sold, refinancing or sliding toward default.

**Use for bond signals.** Issuer origin (fallen angel, original-issue high yield, LBO, developmental) is a categorical feature to test. The liquidity and asset-value split maps onto two feature families: coverage and cash-flow ratios versus leverage and enterprise-value cushions.

### Chapter 2. Common Leveraged Finance Terms

**Question.** What vocabulary, yield conventions and trading conventions does the market use?

**Method.** Definitions: spread $= YTM - y_{benchmark}$ at equivalent maturity (Treasuries for US bonds, LIBOR for loans). Spread-to-worst $STW = YTW - y_{benchmark}(T_{worst})$. YTW is the lowest yield over all call dates and maturity. Rules of thumb: at par the yield equals the coupon. At par or a discount YTW equals YTM. Prices are quoted as a percentage of face value. Trades settle at price plus accrued interest, and defaulted bonds trade flat. Covenant types: affirmative, financial/maintenance and negative.

**Findings.**

- Spread-to-worst is usually the best tool to compare the value of bonds and loans with different maturities.
- The practical round-lot trade is $1,000,000 face, which keeps the market institutional.
- Loan agreements and indentures usually give a 30-day grace period to cure a non-principal default before acceleration.
- A buyer halfway between semiannual coupons on a 10% bond pays the price plus half a coupon period of accrued interest.

**Use for bond signals.** Spread features for callable high-yield bonds should be computed to the worst date, not to maturity. Defaulted bonds trading flat need separate price handling in return series.

Concepts: [[concepts/spread|Spread]] · [[concepts/yield-to-maturity|Yield to Maturity]] · [[concepts/yield-to-worst|Yield-to-Worst and Spread-to-Worst]]

### Chapter 3. Defining the Market and the Ratings Agencies

**Question.** How do ratings define the market, and how much do they tell an investor about trading value?

**Method.** Investment grade is BBB-/Baa3 and above. The analyst reads agency write-ups mainly for the stated upgrade and downgrade triggers. Rating crossover points (the investment-grade boundary and CCC+) matter because many funds face rating limits.

**Findings.**

- Ratings add little to trading value. The agencies are backward-looking and react slowly to events.
- Two issuers with the same single-B rating can trade 1,000 bp or more apart in yield. Dispersion in the CCC category can be even larger.
- Crossover points move prices because many buyers face rating limits, for example on CCC+ and below or on sub-investment-grade holdings. Most of the price move happens before the agency acts.
- Investment-grade issuers that barely hold their rating and are on watch for downgrade may be the next high-yield opportunities.

**Use for bond signals.** Within-rating spread dispersion supports residual spread signals that control for rating. Distance to rating crossovers (BBB-/BB+ and CCC+) and the lag between price moves and rating actions are testable features.

### Chapter 4. The Participants

**Question.** Who issues, arranges and buys leveraged debt, and how does each group behave?

**Method.** Descriptive taxonomy: issuers, sell side (arrangers and market makers), buy side (mutual funds, insurers, pension money, hedge and distressed funds, CDO/CLO managers) and private-equity sponsors.

**Findings.**

- Loan arrangers keep part of the loan and usually the revolver. Bond underwriters must fully distribute an issue before they can make markets in it.
- Structured vehicles such as CLOs matter far more in loans than in bonds and lean toward buy-and-hold.
- Sponsor-owned issuers are event-driven because the sponsor plans to monetize its investment. Sponsors sometimes buy back the debt of their companies when it gets cheap.
- Many new issues are Rule 144A and only QIBs can buy them. A typical trade above $1 million keeps individuals out.

**Use for bond signals.** Sponsor ownership and 144A versus registered status are issuer and bond flags to test. Investor-base mix (CLO share in loans) can matter for flow-driven pricing.

### Chapter 5. Why Is Leveraged Finance Analysis Unique?

**Question.** Why does leveraged credit need tools from fixed income, equity and corporate finance at once?

**Method.** Conceptual framing. Analysis combines debt ratios, equity-style asset valuation, corporate-finance views on funding and liquidity, covenant and structural review, and bankruptcy analysis.

**Findings.**

- Higher leverage leaves less margin for error. So prices react more strongly to small changes in results or headlines, closer to equities than to investment-grade bonds.
- Mergers, acquisitions and asset sales are relatively more common than in investment grade. This pushes the analysis toward equity-style valuation.
- In distress the market stops using yield and spread and switches to a total-return basis.
- The author cites studies showing the market is more correlated with equities, especially mid and small caps, than with fixed income.

**Use for bond signals.** Equity returns, especially small and mid cap, are natural explanatory variables for high-yield bond returns. Signal design should switch from spread-based to price or recovery-based measures for distressed bonds.

### Chapter 6. The Major Components of Analysis

**Question.** What are the building blocks of a leveraged credit analysis?

**Method.** Five components: (1) liquidity, (2) asset value and where the security ranks in its claim on it, (3) structural issues: which entity holds which assets and issued which debt, ranking, call features, covenants in this and other instruments, and technicals such as issue size and currency, (4) event scenarios with probabilities, decision trees or timelines, (5) relative value against other bonds, loans, equities or commodities.

**Findings.**

- Each component should be judged both historically and on how it could change next.
- Even a credit with ample liquidity needs structural and asset-protection analysis because the analyst always guards the downside.
- Covenants in other instruments matter too. They may allow much more debt to be placed ahead of the bond being analyzed.

**Use for bond signals.** The component list is a checklist for a bond-level feature set: liquidity ratios, asset-value cushion, structural flags, event probabilities and relative-value spreads.

### Chapter 7. Some Features of Bank Loans

**Question.** How do leveraged loans differ from bonds in structure, trading and covenants?

**Method.** Descriptive comparison: revolvers versus term loans (A and B tranches), floating coupons over LIBOR with floors and pricing grids, amortization, excess cash flow sweeps, callability at low premium, pro rata repayment, security and guarantees, maintenance covenants, assignment versus participation, and public versus private-side access.

**Findings.**

- Borrowers pay a small fee on undrawn revolver commitments, so revolvers sit mainly with commercial banks.
- Loans are usually callable at any time at a low premium and must be repaid pro rata across bank tranches. Bonds carry call protection.
- Loans carry maintenance covenants that bonds usually lack. Amendments and waivers are easier to obtain from loan holders, often for a fee.
- Banks keep granting waivers to troubled borrowers because they rank senior, fear lender liability and earn fees.
- Secured status does not prove priority over all other debt. The analyst must read the security and guarantee terms.

**Use for bond signals.** Loans and bonds of the same issuer differ in call options, covenant tightness and information access. Loan amendment or waiver events are observable credit-stress signals that can lead bond moves.

### Chapter 8. A Primer on Prices, Yields, and Spreads

**Question.** How are prices, yields, spreads, duration and total return used to compare leveraged debt?

**Method.** Price is a percentage of face. Spread uses a benchmark of matching maturity, so STW uses the Treasury that matches the worst call date. Floating coupon $= \max(\text{LIBOR}, \text{floor}) + \text{spread}$ (our notation; the source states it in words). Floaters use discount margin instead of STW. Duration rule: a duration of $D$ means about $D$ points of price change per 100 bp of yield change. Event or bankruptcy total return: an IRR from today's price plus accrued interest to an estimated payoff (tender price or recovery) at an estimated date. Zero-coupon and PIK accretion: claim and balance-sheet debt follow accreted value.

**Findings.**

- Across bonds of different maturities, spreads are more meaningful than yields because of the term structure.
- A LIBOR floor sets a minimum loan rate. With a 3% floor and a 500 bp spread, the loan pays at least 8% even when LIBOR is 2%.
- A duration of four means about four points of price move for a 100 bp yield change. Longer maturities and lower coupons raise duration.
- A five-year zero issued at 61.4 to yield 10% accretes to 67.68 after one year. Buying at accreted value locks in the issue yield.
- Tighter credits are quoted in spread and riskier ones in price or yield. Among similar-quality bonds the lower yielder trades rich and the higher yielder cheap.

**Use for bond signals.** Signals should use spread-to-worst and duration measured to the worst date. Distressed or event-driven bonds need an IRR-to-recovery or IRR-to-tender measure instead of spread.

### Chapter 9. A Primer on Key Points of Financial Statement Analysis

**Question.** How do you derive cash flow, debt and debt-service figures from financial statements?

**Method.** EBITDA $=$ net income $+$ taxes $+$ interest $+$ depreciation $+$ amortization. Adjusted EBITDA adds back non-cash items, most often stock compensation. OIBDA starts from operating income. Free cash flow $= \text{Adj. EBITDA} - \text{Capex} - \text{cash interest} - \text{cash taxes} \pm \Delta WC$ (our notation; the source states it in words). Total debt $=$ current portion $+$ long-term debt $+$ capital leases. Net debt $=$ total debt $-$ cash and equivalents. Cash interest and cash taxes come from the statement of cash flows. Read the debt footnote for the maturity schedule and undrawn facilities.

**Findings.**

- The statement of cash flows matters more than the income statement for leveraged credits. The analyst should reconcile operating cash flow with EBITDA and free cash flow.
- Most high-yield companies pay little or no cash tax because of interest, depreciation and loss carry-forwards. Book tax should not be treated as cash.
- Working capital swings over a quarter or even a year can mislead because of seasonality. In a liquidity squeeze they can force or prevent a default.
- Restricted cash is usually excluded from liquidity. Debt maturities in the footnote are a main source of downside event risk.

**Use for bond signals.** Feature pipelines should build cash interest and cash taxes from the cash-flow statement. Working-capital changes should use windows of at least a year. Maturity schedules give a refinancing-wall feature.

### Chapter 10. Credit Ratios

**Question.** Which ratios measure debt-service capacity and asset protection, and how should they be built and read?

**Method.** Coverage: $\text{Adj. EBITDA}/\text{Total interest}$, $\text{Adj. EBITDA}/\text{Cash interest}$ and $(\text{Adj. EBITDA} - \text{Capex})/\text{Interest}$. Leverage: $\text{Total debt}/\text{Adj. EBITDA}$ and net leverage $(\text{Debt} - \text{Cash})/\text{Adj. EBITDA}$, also run tranche by tranche (bank leverage versus total leverage). Enterprise value $EV = \text{shares} \times \text{price} + \text{total debt} - \text{cash}$. Equity cushion $= (EV/\text{EBITDA})/(\text{Debt}/\text{EBITDA})$ (our notation; the source states it in words). Market-adjusted debt $MAD = \sum_i P_i \times \text{Face}_i$, used as $MAD/\text{EBITDA}$. Reciprocal $\text{EBITDA}/\text{Debt}$ gives the interest rate at which coverage is 1x. $\text{FCF}/\text{Debt}$ gives the share of debt repayable per year.

**Findings.**

- In the worked example total debt of $5.150 billion over adjusted EBITDA of $1.505 billion gives leverage of 3.42x. Against a 5x peer multiple, the book reports an equity cushion of about 47%, from 5 / 3.42.
- Reasonable leverage depends on the industry multiple. A cyclical manufacturer trading at 5x to 6x EBITDA may support 3x to 4x leverage, while a stable satellite company valued near 9x can carry more.
- Market-adjusted debt lowers leverage in the example from 4.25x to 3.75x. The author says this is realistic only for the most senior tranche.
- The reciprocal of 3x leverage shows the company could pay a 17% rate before coverage falls to 1x. Leverage measured to each tranche (4x bank versus 6x total) drives how the tranches trade against each other.
- FCF/debt of 7.4% means the company generated enough free cash flow to retire 7.4% of its debt in a year. The author rates it among the most telling ratios.

**Use for bond signals.** Build leverage, coverage net of capex, FCF/debt and an equity cushion that scales leverage by a sector EV/EBITDA multiple. Tranche-level leverage and the gap between leverage and coverage (same leverage, different coverage implies higher borrowing cost) are direct bond-level features.

### Chapter 11. Business Trend Analysis and Operational Ratios

**Question.** How do you judge the operating health and direction of a leveraged issuer?

**Method.** Trend tables of revenue and EBITDA over three to five years plus year-over-year quarterly comparisons, benchmarked to GDP and peers, adjusted pro forma for acquisitions and broken out by segment and geography. Ratios: EBITDA margin $= \text{EBITDA}/\text{Revenue}$, gross margin $= (\text{Revenue} - \text{COGS})/\text{Revenue}$, inventory turnover $= \text{COGS}/\text{average inventories}$, capex as a share of revenue (ideally maintenance capex). Qualitative risk factors: competition, customer concentration, entry barriers, cyclicality, capital intensity, price-taker versus price-setter.

**Findings.**

- A steady fall in gross margin, even a small one, deserves follow-up.
- High fixed costs create operating leverage. Margins rise fast with revenue and fall fast when revenue falls.
- A company that defers capex can show inflated free cash flow for a while. It may later face a capex spike or lost revenue.
- Industry metrics such as subscriber acquisition cost or reserve values are needed for relative performance. ROI and ROE are seldom used in leveraged finance.

**Use for bond signals.** Margin trends, revenue growth relative to peers, and capex/revenue versus history are fundamental momentum features. Low capex relative to history can flag overstated free cash flow.

### Chapter 12. Expectations, Modeling, and Scenarios

**Question.** How do you build forward projections and scenarios for a leveraged credit?

**Method.** Top-down revenue drivers (GDP or industry data, for example units times price). Costs split into COGS and SG&A with inflation. Interest from average debt per tranche times its rate in a separate capitalization section. Capex as a share of revenue checked against depreciation. Cash taxes and working capital from history. Explicit assumptions for maturities: refinance, show a funding gap, or treat as a default or asset-sale trigger. Scenarios such as an EBITDA haircut. Track headroom against bank maintenance covenants using the covenant definitions.

**Findings.**

- Cutting projected EBITDA by 10% in the tire-company model hits free cash flow and EBITDA/interest hardest.
- Maintenance covenants are often set with about 25% headroom over company projections. So they hint at the issuer's internal plan.
- Refinancing should be tested, not assumed. In the example bank leverage at maturity is 2.5x, about 1.7x net, and the new loan is assumed to cost more.
- Capex well below depreciation for a long time warns of a coming capex spike.
- Earnings versus consensus matters less for bonds than for stocks. It still matters for larger issuers with public equity.

**Use for bond signals.** Covenant headroom, sensitivity of FCF and coverage to an EBITDA shock, and capex/depreciation are candidate features. Earnings surprises of issuers with listed equity can drive short-horizon bond moves.

### Chapter 13. Structural Issues: Coupons

**Question.** What coupon structures exist in leveraged debt, and what sets the coupon?

**Method.** Loan coupon $= \max(\text{LIBOR}, \text{floor}) + \text{spread}$ (our notation; the source states it in words), with pricing grids keyed to debt/EBITDA or senior secured debt/EBITDA. Bond structures: fixed (most common, paid semiannually), floating (rare, almost always with a floor), and deferred pay: zero coupon, zero-step, PIK and PIK toggle. Accretion adds to balance-sheet debt and to non-cash interest expense. Overfunded escrow structures. Coupon drivers: base rates, average high-yield yields, the issuer's existing yields, comparables, structure and supply and demand.

**Findings.**

- With LIBOR + 625 bp and a 2% floor, the loan never pays below 8.25%.
- Deferred-pay bonds need a higher yield and are more rate-sensitive than cash-pay bonds. Startup or transitional companies tend to issue them.
- For a $100 million five-year zero issued at 55.75, one year of accretion adds $6.99 million of non-cash interest expense.
- Interest rate hedges cost about 50 to 75 bp and usually expire after a few years.
- High yield is less correlated with interest rates than other bond sectors. Credit changes dominate, coupons are high, and maturities rarely exceed ten years.

**Use for bond signals.** Coupon type (zero-step, PIK, toggle, floater with floor) is a bond-level risk flag. The low rate sensitivity of high yield argues for modelling credit spread rather than yield in signals.

Concepts: [[concepts/duration-times-spread|Duration Times Spread (DTS)]] · [[concepts/private-credit-leveraged-loans|Private Credit and Leveraged Loans]]

### Chapter 14. Structural Issues: Maturities, Calls, and Puts

**Question.** How do maturities, calls, clawbacks, sweeps and repurchases change a bond's effective life and value?

**Method.** Typical bond call schedule: non-call for half the life, first call at par plus half the coupon, stepping down to par one year before maturity. YTW is taken over the call schedule. Equity clawback: up to 35% redeemable at par plus coupon from equity proceeds, with 65% left outstanding. 10% annual call at 103 on some secured bonds. Excess cash flow sweep of about 50%, prorated if oversubscribed. AHYDO catch-up payments. Make-whole at Treasuries + 50 bp. Quick refinancing test: call price times likely new coupon (current YTW as proxy) compared with the existing coupon: explore refinancing if $P_{call} \times y_{new} \ll c_{old}$ (our notation; the source states it in words).

**Findings.**

- A typical ten-year high-yield bond is non-callable for five years and then callable at par plus half the coupon.
- A standard equity clawback lets the issuer redeem up to 35% of the issue at 110% with equity proceeds in the early years.
- Leveraged loans now carry light call protection, for example 103 in year one and 101.5 in year two.
- Issuers can buy bonds back below par and book a gain, but bank debt must be repaid pro rata. So discount buybacks target bonds.
- Tenders for non-callable bonds are usually priced at 50 to 100 bp over the matching Treasury. Most bonds carry a make-whole call at Treasuries plus 50 bp.

**Use for bond signals.** Call schedule, clawback eligibility and price relative to the next call price cap upside and define refinancing probability. A coupon well above current YTW times the call price flags likely early takeout.

Concepts: [[concepts/yield-to-maturity|Yield to Maturity]] · [[concepts/yield-to-worst|Yield-to-Worst and Spread-to-Worst]]

### Chapter 15. Structural Issues: Ranking of Debt

**Question.** How does ranking work, and how can corporate structure and guarantees override stated ranking?

**Method.** Priority order: senior secured, senior unsecured, senior subordinated, subordinated, preferred stock, common stock. Security covers only the listed collateral. Structural subordination: holding-company debt ranks behind all operating-company debt. Subsidiary guarantees (senior or subordinated) can lift new debt above old notes and avoid a negative pledge. Read for first-out language and convertible terms.

**Findings.**

- When a credit is strong the yield gap between senior and junior debt is small. It should widen as risk rises.
- Senior notes of a holding company rank behind the senior subordinated notes of its operating company because only the operating company owns the assets.
- New notes with subsidiary guarantees can prime old unguaranteed senior notes. The old notes then tend to trade down.
- Convertibles usually let the issuer force conversion once the stock trades at about 150% of the conversion price.
- Debt called pari passu can still carry first-out language, such as the first $25 million of asset sale proceeds going to the revolver.

**Use for bond signals.** Issuing entity (holding company versus operating company), guarantee status and seniority are bond-level features. The senior-junior spread gap within an issuer is a candidate risk indicator. Priming events are negative event signals for existing notes.

### Chapter 16. Key Leveraged Finance Covenants

**Question.** How do the main covenants work, and how do defined terms and carve-outs change them?

**Method.** Debt incurrence: pro forma total debt/EBITDA below a cap, or pro forma fixed-charge coverage above a floor, plus carve-outs (bank line, refinancing, equity-funded debt). Restricted payments: the issuer must be able to incur $1 of debt under the ratio test, and a basket builds from 50% of cumulative net income or from EBITDA above 1.4x interest. Change of control: put at 101 within about 90 days of closing, with permitted holders and ratings or leverage exceptions. Asset sales: size threshold, minimum cash share, offer to senior debt, then reinvest or offer at par within 180 days. Reporting, line-of-business, affiliate and drop-away covenants. Maintenance tests in loans that tighten over time. Restricted versus unrestricted subsidiaries. Watch for 'and' versus 'or' and for 'notwithstanding'.

**Findings.**

- A 5x leverage test lets a 4x issuer with $500 million EBITDA add about $500 million of debt. A 2.0x fixed-charge test allows $800 million at a 7% rate but only $400 million at 13%, so it is rate-sensitive.
- Restricted-payment baskets commonly build from 50% of cumulative net income, or from EBITDA above 1.4x interest.
- Change of control usually requires an offer to buy the bonds at 101 within 90 days of closing. Loans often use par.
- As a rule of thumb, maintenance covenants carry about a 20% to 25% cushion over the company's internal model.
- Asset-sale covenants may require 85% cash consideration and give 180 days to reinvest or offer to repurchase at par.

**Use for bond signals.** Remaining debt capacity under the incurrence test measures releveraging and priming risk. The 101 change-of-control put sets a floor in takeover events. Maintenance-covenant headroom is a loan-stress feature. Covenant strength can enter as a relative-value control.

### Chapter 17. Amendments, Waivers, and Consents

**Question.** How do issuers change covenants, and what do the voting rules and tools imply for holders?

**Method.** Amendment (permanent) versus waiver (one-time). Voting: simple majority for most terms, sometimes a supermajority, and 100% for money terms (principal, rate, maturity, amortization). 30-day grace periods. Equity cures. Tender with consent fee. Exchange offers with exit consents, priming or sweeteners. Amend and extend in loans.

**Findings.**

- Most covenant changes need a simple majority, for example holders of $500.1 million of a $1 billion bond. Some terms need a supermajority such as 67%, and money terms usually need 100%.
- Amendments and waivers are more common in loans because loans have more covenants, fewer holders, senior positions and lender-liability concerns.
- When a stronger buyer acquires an issuer, its bonds may trade above the 101 put. The buyer then weighs bridge-financing costs against paying bondholders to waive change of control.
- An exchange that wins the required share, usually 50.1%, can strip covenants from the notes that do not exchange. The new notes may also prime the holdouts.
- Amend and extend cannot force all lenders to extend. Even with 90% agreement the loan can split into two tranches.

**Use for bond signals.** Consent solicitations, waiver requests and exchange offers are observable events. Covenant stripping in exchanges is a downside risk for non-tendering holders worth flagging.

Concepts: [[concepts/distressed-exchange|Distressed Exchange]] · [[concepts/event-risk|Event Risk]] · [[concepts/high-yield-covenants|High-Yield Covenants]]

### Chapter 18. Making Money or Losing It Off of News Events

**Question.** How should an analyst estimate the effect of acquisitions, takeovers, IPOs and maturities on bond and loan prices?

**Method.** Event checklist: deal facts, pro forma leverage with and without synergies $(D + D_{new})/(\text{EBITDA} + \text{EBITDA}_{target} [+ \text{synergies}])$ (our notation; the source states it in words), funding mix, issuing entity, covenant capacity, sources and uses, deal-completion risk. Takeover cases by buyer type (investment grade, private equity, equal quality). IPO: primary versus secondary shares, use of proceeds, clawback, valuation. Maturity: refinancing cost and covenant room. Blended takeout price $= \sum_i w_i P_i$ across call, clawback, 10% call and tender (our notation; the source states it in words).

**Findings.**

- In the FastFoodCo example an all-debt acquisition at 4.8x EBITDA lifts leverage to 4.4x, or 3.8x with full cost savings. The analyst should never give full credit for planned savings.
- Existing senior subordinated notes get primed when the acquisition is funded with new senior debt. They trade down even if the leverage increase is modest.
- For a target, change of control matters only if bonds trade at or below 101. An investment-grade buyer that assumes the bonds is a big positive, while a private-equity buyer may add holding-company debt.
- An IPO is usually positive for bonds through deleveraging, clawbacks, a market valuation and better liquidity. A pulled IPO pushes bonds down and may lead to a sale or a leveraged recap.
- Maturity concerns start six to 12 months ahead. Refinancing bank debt at almost twice the old rate leaves leverage unchanged but cuts free cash flow and coverage.

**Use for bond signals.** Event features: announced M&A with pro forma leverage change, acquirer type (investment grade versus sponsor), IPO filings, and maturities within 12 months. The blended takeout price gives an upper bound for bonds in takeout scenarios.

### Chapter 19. Management and Ownership

**Question.** Why do ownership and management matter to debt holders, and how can they be assessed?

**Method.** Qualitative review: management track record and goals compared with peers, ownership from proxies or prospectuses, dual-class voting, compensation targets, takeover vulnerability, private-equity holding horizon, management stake, and independent directors.

**Findings.**

- Management and owners serve shareholders first. What helps equity can hurt debt.
- Guidance that is an outlier versus peers deserves doubt. An example is a target of 15% EBITDA margins from 9% when no peer exceeds 12%.
- Private-equity ownership almost ensures a future transaction because the sponsor needs to realize returns within a fund horizon.
- Analysts prefer management with a meaningful stake and sponsors that have not already taken out most of their equity.

**Use for bond signals.** Sponsor ownership, time since buyout and prior dividend recaps are event-risk features. A stock underperforming peers flags takeover or strategy-change risk for issuers with public equity.

Concepts: [[concepts/lbo-risk|LBO Risk]] · [[concepts/leveraged-recapitalization|Leveraged Recapitalization]] · [[concepts/event-risk|Event Risk]]

### Chapter 20. I'm Looking at Debt, So Why Does Equity Matter?

**Question.** How do equity prices inform asset value and monitoring for leveraged debt?

**Method.** Total enterprise value $TEV = \text{market equity} + \text{debt} - \text{cash}$, with careful share counts (classes, in-the-money options and converts, preferred). Use TEV/adjusted EBITDA for the issuer and peers and compare it with leverage to get the cushion. Account for float, takeover premium, dividends and buybacks, control premium and M&A multiples. Use longer-run multiple history. Monitor peer price and volume.

**Findings.**

- Two firms with the same 2.4x leverage can have very different protection. The cable company has the larger cushion because cable trades at higher multiples than chemicals.
- The equity market discounts leverage. In the example the least-levered company has the highest valuation.
- Stock prices miss control premiums and buyer synergies. One determined buyer can reset an industry's valuation.
- Unusual price or volume moves in an issuer's stock or its peers, without news, can signal stories or large position changes.
- Databases often miscount shares and skip EBITDA adjustments. So their multiples need checking.

**Use for bond signals.** Equity-implied cushion (TEV/EBITDA divided by leverage), issuer and peer stock returns, and abnormal equity volume are early-warning features for high-yield bonds.

### Chapter 21. Value, Relative Value, and Comparable Analysis

**Question.** How do you compare bonds and loans to decide which offers better value?

**Method.** Relative value sheet per security: spread-to-worst, bond-level debt/EBITDA, total debt/EBITDA, FCF/debt, equity-implied cushion and spread per turn of leverage $= STW/(\text{bond-level Debt}/\text{EBITDA})$. Add issue size, rating, next call, price, YTW, duration, loans and CDS levels. Benchmark against high-yield and loan indices, CDS indices, investment-grade comparables or equities. Operational comparison (growth, margins, industry KPIs). Tables of event probabilities over a 6 to 18 month horizon. Covenant headroom now and one year out.

**Findings.**

- Spread per turn of leverage is a useful start but not the answer. DataFone notes look cheapest on it, but the issuer has 7x all-in leverage and FCF/debt of only 0.1%.
- Compare securities, not just companies. Bond-level leverage measures where the tranche sits, while total leverage shows the burden the whole firm must service.
- Within one issuer, senior notes offering less spread per turn than the subordinated notes is an inversion. It points to special covenants or call features, or to rich pricing.
- Event probabilities should be limited to about six to 18 months because almost anything can happen over a longer horizon.

**Use for bond signals.** Spread per turn using bond-level leverage, filtered by total leverage, FCF/debt and equity cushion, is a direct bond-level relative-value signal. Intra-issuer spread-per-turn inversions are a pair-trade screen.

### Chapter 22. New Issuance

**Question.** What should an analyst examine in a new bond or loan offering?

**Method.** Read the use of proceeds, risk factors, structure and covenants, and affiliate transactions. Compare the purchase price with deal and public multiples, using target proxies that show competing bids. Review pro forma adjustments to EBITDA and decide which add-backs to accept.

**Findings.**

- In strong markets new issues may price at or slightly inside peers. In soft markets they carry a new-issue discount relative to existing bonds.
- Target proxy statements often show the other bids, which helps judge whether the price paid was reasonable.
- Offering documents can turn negative reported EBITDA into positive adjusted EBITDA. Add-backs for sponsor fees, full planned cost savings and restructuring cash costs deserve doubt.

**Use for bond signals.** The new-issue concession should depend on market tone. The gap between reported and adjusted EBITDA at issuance is a candidate aggressiveness or quality feature.

Concepts: [[concepts/new-issue-concession|New-Issue Concession]] · [[concepts/lbo-risk|LBO Risk]]

### Chapter 23. Distressed Credits, Bankruptcy, and Distressed Exchanges

**Question.** How do you analyze liquidity, claims, valuation and recoveries for a distressed issuer?

**Method.** Liquidity runway to actual payment dates, with alternatives: asset sales, securitization, revolver draws, new investors. Claims equal principal (accreted value for discount and OID bonds) plus pre-petition accrued interest. Post-petition interest only for over-collateralized secured debt. Class voting needs two-thirds by amount and half by number of holders, so about one-third is a blocking stake. Subordination clauses move value only between the parties named. DIP loans and expenses take super-priority. Valuation uses comparable multiples, precedent deals and DCF, plus separable assets. Recovery waterfall and fulcrum security. Present value today $= R/(1+r)^T$ for expected recovery $R$, required return $r$ and case length $T$.

**Findings.**

- A bankruptcy claim on a discount bond equals its accreted value at filing, such as 83.3% of face. OID bonds are treated the same way, for example a bond issued at 97.
- Holding a third of an impaired class lets an investor block a plan.
- In the reorganization example debt of 9.0x EBITDA meets a court value of 5x, or $650 million, and exit leverage capped at 3x. The senior subordinated notes become the fulcrum security.
- An expected recovery of 50 after a two-year case is worth 34.7 at filing for an investor seeking a 20% IRR.
- A pre-packaged bankruptcy can bind holdouts and may take about 90 days.

**Use for bond signals.** Recovery estimates by seniority and implied enterprise value (sector multiple times EBITDA) give a value anchor for distressed bonds. Bond price divided by discounted recovery is a candidate cheapness signal. Accreted-value claims matter when building recovery-adjusted returns for discount bonds.

### Chapter 24. Preparing a Credit Snapshot

**Question.** What should an analyst look at first when a credit must be assessed quickly?

**Method.** Ordered checklist: (1) what the company does, (2) debt capitalization table with cash, undrawn availability (subject to covenants and borrowing bases) and maturities, (3) funds from operations versus capex over the last 12 months, (4) LTM adjusted EBITDA, debt/EBITDA and (EBITDA - capex)/interest, (5) market EV/EBITDA and recent equity moves, then structure and covenants, detailed liquidity if cash generation falls short, and operating trends.

**Findings.**

- The purpose (a price drop or an announced deal) should set the order of work.
- Operating cash flow versus capex gives a faster read on cash generation than building EBITDA.
- Snapshots of several comparables build a sector baseline of ratios and trends.

**Use for bond signals.** The checklist defines a minimum feature set: leverage, coverage net of capex, liquidity and maturities, market EV/EBITDA and equity moves.

### Chapter 25. The Investment Decision Process

**Question.** How should analysis be turned into a buy, sell or hold decision, and what traps should be avoided?

**Method.** Process: big picture (macro, themes, supply and demand technicals), the company, credit fundamentals, event analysis with probabilities and time frames, security analysis (ranking, covenants, size, liquidity), relative value and return, then the decision (fit, risk and return, sizing, review triggers). Ratings grid: credit risk high, medium or low, plus overweight, evenweight, underweight or avoid.

**Findings.**

- Review triggers should include a meaningful move in the issuer's or peers' equity, perhaps 15% or more, and meaningful moves in the bonds.
- Yield cannot make up for a credit that cannot survive. A low yield can also lull investors into ignoring risk.
- Relying only on ratio and yield sorts is a trap. Such screens miss structural issues and event risk at the security level.
- The author warns against overweighting hard assets and against herd behavior.

**Use for bond signals.** A 15% equity move is a concrete re-review trigger usable as an event feature. The author's warning implies that ratio-based screens need structure and event filters on top.

### Chapter 26. Closing Comments

**Question.** What are the book's closing lessons for leveraged credit analysts?

**Method.** Summary of themes: liquidity and asset value as the base, plus business trends, event risk and structural issues, and a goal-directed decision.

**Findings.**

- Leveraged credit is less forgiving than other markets, so thorough work matters.
- Events can help or hurt debt holders. Cynicism should not blind the analyst to positive events.
- Analysts should not assume that one instrument's terms match the next one's.

**Use for bond signals.** The closing restates that bond-level structure and event features must sit next to liquidity and asset-value ratios in any signal set.
