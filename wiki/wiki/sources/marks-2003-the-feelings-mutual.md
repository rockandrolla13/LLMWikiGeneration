# The Feeling's Mutual

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** October 2, 2003 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2003-10-02-the-feelings-mutual.pdf)

## Summary

Prompted by the New York Attorney General's September 2003 settlement with hedge fund Canary Capital, Marks explains mutual fund timing and late trading, both ways to exploit stale prices in a once-a-day NAV. He judges the direct harm to fund investors small relative to assets, but argues the scandal points to deeper structural problems. Mutual fund companies are fiduciaries yet also for-profit marketing machines; independent directors almost never fire the adviser; funds are sold on hot recent performance; and expense ratios rose rather than fell as the industry grew. He concludes mutual funds are good overall but need to put shareholders' interests first.

## Key Ideas

1. Fund timing exploits stale prices in the once-daily NAV (e.g. a UK stock priced at the London close); late trading places orders after the 4:00 p.m. cutoff in breach of the forward pricing rule.
2. Both are 'knowledge-advantaged short-term trading' that transfers profit from long-term holders to the trader; the profit depends on very short holding periods.
3. The wrongdoing lay in fund companies granting exceptions to their own prospectus policies in exchange for capital commitments and fees.
4. Money managers are fiduciaries who should give 'the whole loaf' to the client, but fund firms are also for-profit asset gatherers.
5. Independent fund board oversight is 'largely a legal fiction': fund companies are essentially never fired as advisers.
6. Investors chase hot funds, so the average fund investor does worse than the average fund; bottom-quartile funds get merged away (survivorship).
7. In efficient markets expense minimisation is the surest edge, yet active fund expense ratios rose as assets grew, showing no economies of scale passed on.

## Market Context

Autumn 2003, after a run of corporate, auditor, investment bank and hedge fund scandals. On September 3, 2003 the NY Attorney General (Eliot Spitzer) announced the Canary Capital settlement, followed by disclosures at Alliance Capital and Millennium. The tech bubble had recently burst, burying investors in the funds featured on magazine covers.

## Notable Claims

- Canary Capital paid $40 million to settle the charges.
- Exploiting a new 1-cent disparity daily on a $10 NAV adds 25% a year.
- An academic study estimated $4 billion a year diverted, about $6 per $10,000 of fund assets.
- Top-25 equity fund expense ratios rose from .64% to 1.50% while assets grew 845 times (Bogle).
- One fund board chairman was paid $816,000 the prior year.

> They are not supposed to "split the loaf" between themselves and their clients.
