# The Seven Worst Words in the World

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** September 26, 2018 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/the-seven-worst-words-in-the-world.pdf)

## Summary

Released the week before 'Mastering the Market Cycle', this memo applies its theme ('history doesn't repeat itself, but it does rhyme') to the 2018 credit market. The seven worst words are 'too much money chasing too few deals'. Revisiting 'The Race to the Bottom' (February 2007), Marks frames markets as auctions in which the asset goes to whoever accepts the lowest return and the most risk. He explains that Oaktree turned defensive in 2005-06 because of imprudent deals, not a subprime forecast, and held a nearly $11 billion reserve fund. Ten years after Lehman, with ten years of low rates and QE, a decade-old recovery and bull market, and investors who had never seen a bear market, he finds the conditions for froth fulfilled. His case study is direct lending's shift from selective post-crisis lending to crowded, borrower-friendly terms. He supports the case with many data points on rising leverage, covenant-lite share, EBITDA add-backs, tight spreads and anonymised aggressive deals, and identifies corporate debt as the likely soft spot. He is not calling a bubble or crash. He recommends 'move forward, but with caution', favouring strategies that limit losses in declines.

## Key Ideas

1. Markets are auctions: when money is plentiful and eager, the winning bidder accepts the lowest expected return and the most risk.
2. Oaktree's 2005-06 defensiveness came from observing imprudent deals ('the less prudence with which others conduct their affairs...'), not from predicting subprime.
3. The 'ten-year' setting (low rates, QE, a long recovery and bull market, managers untested by a bear market) fulfils the book's conditions for a frothy market.
4. Direct lending illustrates 'what the wise man does in the beginning, the fool does in the end': early lenders were selective, later entrants compete by loosening terms.
5. Nothing is a good or bad idea per se; it depends on timing, price, terms and the skill of whoever does it.
6. There is no single gauge of prudence; 'taking the temperature of the market' means assembling anecdotal evidence about deals, valuations and attitudes.
7. Debt, not equities, is the likely ground zero next time; conditions are not as bad as 2007, and Marks makes no crash prediction.
8. Being too far ahead of one's time is indistinguishable from being wrong (19 months passed between 'The Race to the Bottom' and the real crisis); turn cautious when conditions are precarious, without timing a collapse.

## Market Context

Late September 2018, ten years after Lehman's bankruptcy, in the longest US bull market on record (3,453 days without a 20% drop as of August 22). Rates were low after a decade of QE, the federal government had passed a large pro-cyclical tax cut and spending increase, leveraged loans and private credit had boomed, and SoftBank's Vision Fund, Bitcoin's 2017 surge and FAANG valuations reflected risk tolerance.

## Notable Claims

- Oaktree's reserve distressed fund announced in early 2007 eventually reached nearly $11 billion.
- Covenant-lite loans were $375bn (75% of leveraged loan issuance) in 2017 versus $97bn (29%) in 2007.
- Total leveraged debt outstanding was $2.5 trillion, double 2007.
- BBB bonds were $1.4 trillion and about 47% of investment grade in the US and Europe.
- Argentina's 100-year bond priced at 90 (7.92%) fell to 75.

## Quotes

> Thus the idea for this memo came from the seven worst words in the investment world: “too much money chasing too few deals.”

> “I tell my students real estate has ten-year cycles, but luckily bankers have five-year memories.”

— Arthur Segel, quoted by Marks

> Thus the best we can do is turn cautious when the situation becomes precarious.
