# Doesn’t Make Sense

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** July 31, 2008 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2008-07-31-doesnt-make-sense.pdf)

## Summary

Written midway through the subprime crisis, this memo catalogues irrational features of business, markets and policy. Marks starts with Chuck Prince's 'still dancing' remark and argues that Citigroup could not have sat out the boom, because short-termism punishes anyone who is right too early. He attacks the credit rating agencies for issuer-paid, rating-shopping incentives and cites Moody's CPDO error and analyst-switching reports. He mocks the accounting rule that let Radian book a gain as its own credit worsened, defends oil 'speculators' against scapegoating, and compares Fed rescues to forest-fire suppression that builds up brush and creates moral hazard. He ends by warning against expecting a V-shaped recovery, laying out a chicken-and-egg loop among home prices, mortgages, write-offs and bank capital, and recommends investing gradually.

## Key Ideas

1. Being too far ahead of your time is indistinguishable from being wrong; a CEO who sat out the boom early would have been fired sooner.
2. Short-termism is the worst flaw in American business: companies and managers are rewarded for short-run results that can raise long-run risk.
3. Credit ratings are error-prone and slow; managing a bond portfolio by ratings would be unavailing or disastrous, while gaming against them can be profitable.
4. Issuer-paid ratings let issuers shop for the highest rating, and agencies do not attract the above-average judgment needed for useful ratings.
5. Mark-to-market rules can produce absurd results, such as a gain when a company's own credit worsens, and liquidation values can replace going-concern values.
6. Repeated Fed rescues created moral hazard, like forest-fire suppression that lets brush build up; good decisions need the fear of loss to remain.
7. Recovery may be saucer-shaped, not V-shaped, because home prices, mortgage availability, bank write-offs and bank capital each depend on the others.
8. With few beaten-down assets outside housing and financials, investing gradually keeps capital available for later bargains.

## Market Context

July 2008, about a year into the subprime and credit crisis and before the Lehman collapse. Banks such as Citigroup were taking subprime write-downs and raising equity at depressed prices, structured mortgage ratings had been cut en masse, oil prices had surged with politicians blaming speculators, and housing and financial institutions were in a downward spiral. Optimists such as Deutsche Bank's Josef Ackermann saw the beginning of the end while Alan Blinder saw a worsening system.

## Notable Claims

- Prince's statement on continuing to take risk.
- Moody's misrated European CPDOs because of a programming error.
- Radian reported a profit thanks to a fall in the value of its own liabilities.
- Scale of the oil market relative to speculative buying.
- The 1970s bear market as a counterexample to V-shaped recoveries.

## Quotes

> Being too far ahead of your time is indistinguishable from being wrong.

> Good business decisions can be made only if the hope for gain is balanced by the fear of loss.

> Thus it isn’t impossible that spending will stay strong . . . just illogical.
