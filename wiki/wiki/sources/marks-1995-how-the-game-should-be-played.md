# How the Game Should Be Played

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** May 26, 1995 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/1995-05-26-how-the-game-should-be-played.pdf)

## Summary

Written shortly after Marks and colleagues left to found Oaktree, this short memo explains what running the firm 'our way' means. Marks contrasts the Babe Ruth 'credo of the home run', which he says much of the investment world follows, with golfer Tom Kite, who earned more prize money than any golfer before him by consistently finishing in the money without having won a major. Oaktree's game plan is the Kite approach: accept that the big-picture future cannot be known, work in inefficient markets where specialization and hard work add value, and give the highest priority to avoiding losses rather than to bold bets.

## Key Ideas

1. Oaktree rejects the 'swing for the fences' approach and will not treat bottom-quartile years as an acceptable cost of chasing top-quartile ones.
2. Consistency matters more than headline wins: a high long-term batting average beats occasional home runs.
3. Few managers can actually forecast markets, yet many act as if they can and never publish a scorecard of their forecasts.
4. Because the macro future is unknowable, Oaktree chooses inefficient markets where specialization, skill and hard work can earn above-average results.
5. Acknowledging one's limitations leads to putting avoidance of losses first.
6. The danger lies with people who do not know but are confident they know (Tversky quotation).

## Market Context

Mid-1995, just after the founding of Oaktree. The memo explains the new firm's philosophy to clients; it does not discuss specific market conditions.

## Notable Claims

- Tom Kite had won about $7.2 million in prize money over 20 seasons, more than any golfer, before winning a major.
- Marks wrote a 1990 memo rebutting a manager who accepted bottom-5% years as the price of top-5% years.
- Oaktree's stated three-part response: accept not knowing the future, work in inefficient markets, prioritise avoiding losses.

## Quotes

> What we think matters isn't whether you hit a home run or win the Masters on any given day, but rather what your longterm batting average is.

> It's frightening to think that you might not know something, but more frightening to think that, by and large, the world is run by people who have faith that they know exactly what's going on.

> Thus our "game plan" is directed at avoiding strikeouts and building a high batting average over time, not at hitting a home run each trip to the plate.
