# Hey, Steward!!

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** March 16, 2004 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2004-03-16-hey-steward!!.pdf)

## Summary

A follow-up to his October memo 'The Feeling's Mutual', this piece uses the 2003-04 mutual fund scandals to argue that money managers have drifted from stewardship to salesmanship, borrowing Jack Bogle's phrase. Marks catalogs asset-gathering practices that put fund sponsors ahead of investors: revenue sharing with brokers, directing client-paid commissions to brokers for 'preferred list' placement, and sales incentives such as paid trips. He argues that legal disclosure buried in prospectuses is not effective disclosure. He criticizes fund boards that benchmark fees to the industry median rather than to institutional rates, and he uses Morningstar data to show 12b-1 distribution fees do not lower expense ratios and persist even at closed funds. He extends the critique to corporate boards and related-party deals after Enron-era scandals. He closes with Oaktree's founding test: if all practices became known, no one should have grounds to complain.

## Key Ideas

1. The fund industry's focus shifted from stewardship to salesmanship as gathering assets under management became the goal.
2. Brokers are fiduciaries, not supermarkets, so payments for 'shelf space' (revenue sharing, directed brokerage, sales trips) are conflicts that need direct, plain-language disclosure.
3. Using client-owned commissions to reward fund sales means sponsors pay for recommendations with clients' money.
4. Regulators should require effective disclosure in everyday English, made directly by the fiduciary, not boilerplate in prospectuses.
5. Fund directors who cap fees at the industry median ignore whether fees are fair relative to institutional rates; an aggregate of small investors should pay institutional fees.
6. 12b-1 distribution fees were meant to lower costs through growth, but Morningstar data show they do not reduce expense ratios and persist even at closed funds.
7. Corporate scandals and related-party transactions reflect the same failure: stewards serving themselves and directors not policing management.
8. Oaktree's 'page one' test: assume every practice will become public and ensure no one would have grounds for complaint.

## Market Context

Early 2004, after the 2003 mutual fund late-trading and market-timing scandals and SEC scrutiny of revenue sharing, and in the wake of the 2001-02 corporate scandals at Enron, WorldCom, Adelphia, HealthSouth and Tyco.

## Notable Claims

- Edward Jones concentrated sales in revenue-sharing fund families.
- SEC found most broker-dealers took cash from fund companies.
- High yield mutual fund fees exceed the institutional norm.
- Most funds charge 12b-1 fees.
- Closed funds still collect 12b-1 fees.

## Quotes

> Unfortunately, “legal disclosure” too often seems to be an oxymoron

> Why shouldn’t the aggregation of 1,000 little guys, each with $50,000, pay the same fee as an institution investing $50 million?

> It is our fundamental operating principle that if all of our practices were to become known, there must be no one with grounds for complaint.
