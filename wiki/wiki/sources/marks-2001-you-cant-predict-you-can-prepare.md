# You Can't Predict. You Can Prepare.

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** November 20, 2001 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2001-11-20-you-cant-predict-you-can-prepare.pdf)

## Summary

Marks argues that investors cannot forecast the timing or size of cyclical moves, but they can know where they stand in a cycle and prepare. He sets out general rules: cycles are inevitable, financial memory is short, trends create the causes of their own reversal, and people describe up and down moves in lopsided language. He then walks through five cycles. These are the economic cycle, the credit cycle, the corporate life cycle, business fads, and the market cycle driven by investor psychology. He gives most weight to the credit cycle, where small economic changes cause large swings in credit availability and where loose lending feeds bubbles. He closes with the tech bubble as a case where many cycles were ignored at once, and he notes that early caution, including his own, can look the same as being wrong.

## Key Ideas

1. Knowing where you are in a cycle differs from predicting the timing, extent and shape of the next move, and only the first is achievable.
2. Consensus forecasts mostly extrapolate current conditions, so they miss the big surprises where the profit potential lies.
3. In the credit cycle, prosperity breeds looser lending, which breeds losses, which shuts lending and ends prosperity; contrarian lenders at the trough can demand high returns with high creditworthiness.
4. Marks worries less about the economic future than about the supply and demand for capital; investing in an uncrowded arena is an advantage.
5. The corporate life cycle makes ultra-high growth unsustainable, so expect growth rates to regress toward the mean.
6. Business practices such as diversification versus specialization swing like a pendulum because no position is permanently right.
7. Bull markets pass through three stages, ending when everyone concludes everything will get better forever; market moves come mainly from changes in psychology, not fundamentals.
8. Being too far ahead of your time is indistinguishable from being wrong, which makes staying power essential.

## Market Context

November 2001, after the tech bubble burst and the September 11 attacks. The economy was in recession, credit was tightening, and books such as "Dow 36,000" were being called silly. Oaktree was positioning its investments on the assumption that the difficult part of the credit cycle lay ahead.

## Notable Claims

- Few large companies sustained 15% EPS growth over two-decade periods.
- 1999 investors paid p/e ratios of 50-plus on 20%-plus growth and lost 90%-plus.
- ITT made 52 acquisitions in one year during the conglomerate era.
- Loose credit fed a series of booms and collapses from 1989 to 2001.
- Marks expects cash to become valuable as the credit cycle turns.

## Quotes

> Thus I like to say success carries within itself the seeds of failure, and failure the seeds of success.

> In the financial world, if you offer cheap money, they will borrow, buy and build – often without discipline, and with very negative consequences.

> We may never know where we're going, or when the tide will turn, but we had better have a good idea where we are.
