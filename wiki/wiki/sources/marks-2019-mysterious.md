# Mysterious

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** October 17, 2019 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/mysterious.pdf)

## Summary

Asked by colleague Ian Schapiro to write about negative interest rates, Marks's answer is that nobody really understands them, and that is the point. He recounts his own experiences: being charged to hold cash in a Spanish bank in 2014, and watching his laddered Treasury portfolio roll from about 6% to five-eighths of a percent in late 2008. He explains why anyone would buy a negative-yield bond (flight to safety, expected further rate falls, deflation, currency bets) and catalogues candidate causes: central bank intent, QE, excess savings over investment demand, demographics, low inflation, and regulation requiring sovereign holdings. Negative rates invert many normal processes. They reverse compounding, push investors into risk under TINA, undermine insurers' float, bank margins, pension discounting and pricing models, and may even be contractionary. He guesses, with low confidence ('6-to-5'), that the US will not go negative this cycle. He recommends moving out the risk curve cautiously, toward durable, dependable cash flows bought at sensible prices.

## Key Ideas

1. Knowing what negative rates are doesn't mean knowing why they exist, how long they'll last, what ends them or what they do; like inflation, they are 'mysterious'.
2. Interest rates reflect the time value of money plus expected inflation or deflation (plus a credit premium); deflation expectations could theoretically justify negative yields.
3. Many candidate causes compete (central bank policy, QE, excess savings, demographics, low inflation, regulation), and no one should feel the causes are fully understood.
4. Negative rates turn usual processes upside down: compounding works in reverse, float loses value, early payment becomes preferable, and pension and present-value maths are distorted.
5. Under TINA, negative safe yields discourage risk aversion and push investors into risky assets at low prospective returns.
6. Negative rates may be contractionary, because they signal pessimism and cause households to save more (eurozone savings near 13%).
7. Forecasting something without precedent is impossible; Marks would give only about 6-to-5 odds that the US stays positive.
8. The response: move out the risk curve with caution, favouring durable, dependable cash flows while making sure the price allows a good return.

## Market Context

October 2019. Large shares of European and Japanese government debt traded at negative yields, the Fed had made two recent rate cuts (fed funds below 2%), US manufacturing was slowing, and the US-China trade war was on-again/off-again.

## Notable Claims

- Negative-yield bonds were roughly two-thirds of European bonds and 25-30% of global investment grade debt.
- Over $17 trillion of debt traded at negative nominal yields, $35.7 trillion in real terms (Bloomberg).
- A 2067 Italian bond yielding 2.877% was almost six times oversubscribed.
- The ECB took its credit facility rate to -0.10% in 2014.
- Eurozone household savings ratio at a five-year high near 13% (FT).

## Quotes

> “I can’t. I don’t know anything about them.” And then I realized that’s the point. No one does.

> Whatever we knew in the past about how things worked, I think we know less when rates are negative.

> In my view, durability and dependability are highly desirable (rather than hail-Mary attempts at a moonshot). They are the Oaktree way.
