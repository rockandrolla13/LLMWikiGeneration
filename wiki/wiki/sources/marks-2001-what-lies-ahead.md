# What Lies Ahead?

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** October 4, 2001 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2001-10-04-what-lies-ahead.pdf)

## Summary

Written about three weeks after the September 11 attacks, this memo asks whether investors should assume a return to normal. Marks argues that all investing depends on extrapolating the past, and that this usually works, but that the range of possible futures is now wider than usual. He identifies confidence as the core driver of the economic cycle: confident units borrow and buy, fearful ones hunker down and build cash. Monetary and fiscal stimulus can help at the margin but cannot force spending, and he cites Japan's decade of near-zero rates as a warning about 'pushing on a string'. He worries that the stock market's quick rebound shows complacency. Oaktree will not bet on a single bleak scenario, but it will keep investing in inefficient markets with a very large margin for error and a fixed defensive style.

## Key Ideas

1. Investing consists of divining future profits and values and turning them into prices to pay today, so uncertainty is both the challenge and the source of an edge.
2. Most forecasts extrapolate the past; the valuable ones predict radical change, but such forecasts are rare and rarely right, and forecasters overstate their credibility.
3. After 9/11 the range of possible futures is wider than normal, so investors must allow for greater-than-normal uncertainty and low-probability, high-impact events.
4. Confidence drives the economic cycle: it governs whether households and businesses spend and borrow or defer purchases and hoard cash.
5. Government liquidity, fiscal deficits and rate cuts work at the margin but can amount to 'pushing on a string' when spenders have hunkered down, as Japan shows.
6. A quick market rebound after a shock can signal complacency; be more afraid when others are less afraid.
7. Reject 'single scenario investing': build portfolios that succeed across many scenarios, with a substantial margin for error.
8. Investors rarely switch styles successfully; sticking to one disciplined, defensive approach is better than chasing every turn ('wait at the bus stop').

## Market Context

October 2001, weeks after the September 11 terrorist attacks. Markets closed for four days and the Dow fell 7% on reopening, then recovered most of the loss. The US economy was already in a slowdown, the Fed was cutting rates, the administration announced emergency spending that would consume the projected surplus, and capacity utilization was low.

## Notable Claims

- Dow fell 685 points (7%) on reopening.
- Dow had recovered to within 6% of the pre-attack close.
- Dow up 10% from the September 24 low.
- Emergency fiscal programs announced after the attacks.
- Prompt monetary action had averted crises before.

## Quotes

> Sometimes I think in the economy, confidence is all there is.

> Some of the greatest dilemmas in investing surround highly unlikely events with highly negative implications

> It's my conclusion that if you wait at a bus stop long enough, you're sure to catch your bus, while if you keep wandering all over the bus route, you may miss them all.
