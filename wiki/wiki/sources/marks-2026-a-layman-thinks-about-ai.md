# A Layman Thinks About AI

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** November 11, 2026 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/a-layman-thinks-about-ai.pdf)

## Summary

Marks, disclaiming technical expertise, organises his thinking about investing in artificial intelligence around eleven questions: how AI works, what it will be able to do, who will profit, the competitive landscape, company profitability, data centers, the economy's reliance on one sector, credit market implications, predictability, whether AI is a bubble, and how to invest. He stresses that an AI thesis must be right about many variables at once (technology, compute demand, competition, beneficiaries, financing, timing), so the joint probability of being right is low. He flags commoditisation risk for models, fragile moats, chip depreciation, data-center bottlenecks, heavy AI concentration in GDP growth, S&P 500 earnings and the IG bond index, circular vendor financing, and off-balance-sheet SPVs. Using Claude's own predictability scores (AI overall about 3 of 10; frontier labs 2; regulated utilities serving AI load 6), he concludes that answering whether AI capex will earn adequate returns is largely a matter of faith, and recommends calibrated humility, sizing, price discipline and choosing which uncertainties one is equipped to bear.

## Key Ideas

1. Investing in AI requires being right about many independent variables, so the combined probability of any thesis is low (illustrated by 0.6 to the fourth power = 13% for four top-down calls).
2. Transformative technologies do not necessarily reward investors; if models commoditise, benefits may flow to users rather than vendors.
3. Leadership can shift fast: OpenAI and Anthropic were unknown a few years ago, and dot-com-era leaders such as Yahoo and Netscape fell away.
4. Proposed test: would you bet that an AI company's EPS in five years lands within +/-50% of your estimate?
5. Data centers are not a 'layup': the shell is a small share of cost, GPUs carry obsolescence risk, and power, sites, fiber, financing and lease out-clauses all matter.
6. AI is an undiversified 'fault line' running through the economy, equity indices and credit markets.
7. AI's rate-insensitive borrowing may crowd out other borrowers and push up government rates; hyperscaler ratings may erode as leverage rises; circular transactions and SPVs need scrutiny.
8. Bubble test in three parts: is the technology overhyped, is spending excessive, are investors paying too much? Answering is largely a matter of faith.

## Market Context

November 2026: massive AI capex by hyperscalers (Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet, Meta, Oracle), Magnificent 7 earnings outgrowing the rest of the S&P 500 for 14 straight quarters through June 30, heavy AI debt issuance, OpenAI and Anthropic having filed for IPOs, recent moratoriums on data center construction, and a souring mood driven by inflation, federal deficits, rising interest rates, an intractable war in Iran, cheap Chinese and open-source models, and falling prices per token.

## Notable Claims

- AI-related debt issuance reached nearly $600 billion globally in 2026 (Goldman Sachs), about $400 billion of it in US IG.
- AI-related bonds are about 12% of the Bloomberg USD IG Corporate index and about 6% of US high yield.
- 42 AI-related companies generated about two-thirds of S&P 500 price gains since January 2024 (J.P. Morgan).
- Alphabet and Amazon booked $151 billion of equity-investment gains in Q2, mostly on Anthropic and SpaceX (FactSet).
- AI is likely to need about $1 trillion of new capital per year.
