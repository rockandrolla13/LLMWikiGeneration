# I Beg to Differ

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** July 26, 2022 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/i-beg-to-differ.pdf)

## Summary

Marks pulls together his recurring theme that superior results require being different. He recalls the Nifty Fifty collapse of 1973-74 and his own profitable move into unloved convertibles and high yield bonds, restates the Dare to Be Great conventional/unconventional matrix, and explains why active bets are zero-sum relative to the average: any bet that can beat the average can also lag it. He revisits second-level thinking, contrarianism done at a second level (not knee-jerk), and the decision to risk being wrong, using David Swensen's Yale endowment as a case of 'uncomfortably idiosyncratic' success. He closes with a June 2022 conference anecdote: since short-term views on inflation, rates and recession are unknowable and hard to act on, investors can diverge profitably by ignoring the short term and focusing on long-term capital deployment.

## Key Ideas

1. Assets on the 'pedestal of popularity' (the Nifty Fifty) can collapse; Marks' early success came from investing in what nobody knew about, cared about or wanted.
2. Collectively, investors earn the average; active bets are zero-sum (negative after costs), so every bet that can produce above-average returns can also produce below-average ones.
3. Second-level thinking: to outperform, your thinking must be different and better, weighing outcomes, probabilities, consensus and what is priced in.
4. Readily available quantitative information about the present cannot be the source of superior performance (Andrew Marks); edge comes from judgment about meaning, qualitative factors and the future.
5. Contrarianism must also be second-level: understand what the herd is doing, why, and what is wrong with it, rather than simply doing the opposite.
6. 'Can't lose' strategies (T-bills, index funds) are also 'can't win'; each investor must choose consciously between pursuing superiority and accepting average.
7. Swensen's Yale Model shows that early, large, skilful unconventional moves can outperform, while late imitators did not match the results.
8. Short-term macro concerns are unknowable and their pricing is unknowable; a coming recession is a reason to reduce risk only if it is not reflected in prices. Focus on long-term capital deployment.

## Market Context

July 2022. Markets were falling, and investor questions centred on inflation, how far the Fed would raise rates, and whether a soft landing or recession would follow. The S&P 500 dropped from about 3,900 on May 19 to about 3,750 on June 21.

## Notable Claims

- The 1973-74 recession took the S&P 500 down 47%; Nifty Fifty P/Es fell from 60-90 to single digits.
- Marks moved to the bond department in 1978 to start convertible and high yield funds.
- Since 1920 there have been 17 recessions, yet the S&P 500 returned about 10½% a year.
- S&P 500 fell almost 4% between Oaktree's LA and London conferences in 2022.
- Swensen ran Yale's endowment from 1985 until his death in 2021.
