# Thinking About Macro

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** July 29, 2021 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/oaktreecaplibraries/default-document-library/thinking-about-macro.pdf)

## Summary

Marks opens with Buffett's test that useful information must be important and knowable. He argues that the macro future is important but not knowable, and that most forecasts are either unhelpful consensus or rarely-right outliers. Because markets since 2000, and especially since 2008, have focused on macro, he devotes the memo to the central 2021 question: whether post-Covid inflation will prove permanent or transitory. He lays out the stimulus-driven case for inflation (CPI 4.2-5.4% in April-June 2021, shortages, roughly $2 trillion added to consumer balance sheets) and the case for transitory (restart effects, expectation-driven price swings like lumber's 540% rise and 60% fall, fading stimulus, technology and globalization), and he concludes it is impossible to know. He shows that the Fed admits uncertainty, that stocks, bonds and gold gave contradictory signals, and that Wall Street's median S&P forecast missed by an average 12.9 points from 2000-2020 and never called a down year. He worries that persistent Fed accommodation creates a 'Fed put', moral hazard, wealth disparity and a non-free market in money. Asset prices look fair relative to record-low rates. Oaktree holds a normal offense/defense balance and suggests only marginal inflation hedges, not major reallocation.

## Key Ideas

1. Buffett's test: information is desirable only if important and knowable; the macro future is important but not knowable.
2. Macro forecasters rarely publish track records ('an economist is a portfolio manager who never marks to market').
3. Inflation is 'mysterious': central banks couldn't reach 2% for years despite stimulus, and the Phillips curve failed in the 2010s.
4. The 2021 permanent-vs-transitory inflation debate has intelligent arguments on both sides and no knowable answer.
5. Markets are good observers but poor predictors: in June 2021 stocks fell on inflation fears while bond yields and gold also fell.
6. Wall Street's median annual S&P 500 forecast never predicted a decline from 2000-2020 and missed by an average 12.9 percentage points.
7. Prolonged Fed accommodation creates a 'Fed put', moral hazard, more leverage, wealth disparity and fewer tools for the next slowdown; Marks wants a free market in money with naturally occurring rates.
8. Asset prices are fair relative to record-low rates (S&P earnings yield 4-5% vs ~1.25% 10-year), so tilt at the margin toward inflation hedges rather than inverting allocations.

## Market Context

July 2021, during the post-Covid reopening. The Fed held rates at 0-0.25% and bought $120 billion of bonds a month, Q1 2021 real GDP grew 6.4% annualized, CPI was rising fast, the 10-year Treasury yielded roughly 1.25%, and the S&P 500 traded in the low 20s P/E after a 68% rise from the March 2020 low.

## Notable Claims

- CPI year-over-year readings in spring 2021.
- Lumber price swing.
- Median Wall Street forecast never called a down year since 2000 (quoting NYT/Bespoke data).
- Average forecast miss of 12.9 points.
- Gold fell 14% from its August 2020 high.

## Quotes

> It may be hard to admit – to yourself or to others – that you don’t know what the macro future holds, but in areas entailing great uncertainty, agnosticism is probably wiser than self-delusion.

> “It’s one thing to have an opinion, but something very different to assume it’s right and bet heavily on it.”

— an Oaktree saying, quoted by Marks

> I consider it reasonable for investors to give a nod to the possibility of higher inflation, but not to significantly invert asset allocations in response to macro expectations that may or may not prove accurate.
