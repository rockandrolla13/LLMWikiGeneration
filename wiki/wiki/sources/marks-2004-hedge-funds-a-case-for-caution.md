# Hedge Funds: A Case for Caution

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** October 6, 2004 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2004-10-06-hedge-funds-a-case-for-caution.pdf)

## Summary

Marks argues that hedge funds have become the latest would-be 'silver bullet', following common stocks in the 1990s and venture capital in 1999-2000, and that their popularity will likely end in disappointment rather than a bust. He starts from the principle that no asset class has a birthright to a return and that the balance of capital to ideas decides future results. Scalability is the key issue: a few hedge funds once fed on the crumbs left by big institutions, but thousands of funds with about a trillion dollars cannot all thrive on the same inefficiencies. Leverage and freedom from constraints are two-edged swords; only genuine skill adds asymmetrically. Using the CSFB/Tremont index he questions whether 2000-04 returns justified the inflows, lists drawbacks (data biases, liquidity mismatch, crowding, fee incentives), and guesses 5-6% net returns for the average fund ahead.

## Key Ideas

1. There is no 'good' asset class; results depend on price paid and on the supply of ideas relative to the money chasing them.
2. Scalability ('can a good little idea become a good big idea?') is the central hedge fund question: big fish and little fish analogy.
3. Leverage is not a source of alpha, only a way to magnify exposure to alpha or its absence.
4. Pay above-average fees only for asymmetric value added; only genuine skill provides it, not leverage or unconstrained mandates.
5. Hedge fund returns since 2000 looked correlated with markets and low in absolute terms, undermining the 'absolute return' premise.
6. Performance data suffers from post-selection and survivorship bias, and illiquid holdings understate beta and overstate Sharpe ratios.
7. A fund can offer more liquidity than its portfolio, creating run risk from 'hot money'.
8. Management fees on very large funds and high-water marks can misalign manager incentives.

## Market Context

Autumn 2004: after the 2000-02 bear market, money market yields were about 1%, Treasurys 3-5% and high-grade corporates 4-6%, rates were expected to rise, and institutions were pouring capital into non-mainstream assets including hedge funds (Bank of New York forecast another $250 billion of U.S. institutional inflows by 2008).

## Notable Claims

- About 7,000 hedge funds exist, up from 1,640 a decade earlier.
- Hedge fund capital is $850bn-$1tn, up about tenfold in ten years.
- The 2000-02 swoon took the S&P 500 down 49% and the NASDAQ down 78%.
- CSFB/Tremont index returned 4.9%, 4.4% and 3.0% while the S&P fell 9%, 12% and 22% in 2000-02.
- Marks guesses 5-6% net returns for the average hedge fund ahead.

## Quotes

> hedge funds are a compensation system often mistaken for an industry.

> Leverage is not a source of alpha; it's a way of increasing your exposure to a given amount of alpha . . . or lack of alpha

> Why should we believe the last 20,000 managers to join the sector are as smart as the first 1,000?
