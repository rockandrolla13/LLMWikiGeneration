# What Can We Do For You?

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** January 10, 2012 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2012-01-10-what-can-we-do-for-you.pdf)

## Summary

Marks sets out what an investment manager can and cannot do. Managers cannot see the macro future, so they should diversify more, time markets less and use less leverage. They can still add value by highlighting fruitful asset classes, building a knowledge advantage about companies and securities, buying bargains, limiting risk, controlling ego and emotion, acting as contrarians and behaving counter-cyclically. He reconciles Oaktree's founding tenets against macro-forecasting and market timing with its tactical adjustments: Oaktree reacts to observable market conditions such as spreads, valuations, deal quality and investor ebullience, not to predictions ('we may never know where we're going, but we'd better know where we are'). He proposes three questions for asset allocators: whether to expect prosperity, whether to fear losing money or missing opportunity more, and which attributes the moment calls for, 'money and nerve' or risk control and selectivity. For early 2012 he sees macro risks, including the European crisis, balanced against cheap US stocks (P/E about 12) and high yield spreads near 700 bp, and he recommends balancing offense and defense.

## Key Ideas

1. Not knowing the future is less dangerous than thinking you know it; acknowledging ignorance implies more diversification, less market timing and less leverage.
2. Managers can add value through a knowledge advantage, bargain hunting, risk control, emotional discipline, contrarianism and counter-cyclical behavior, aiming at risk-adjusted rather than maximum return.
3. Oaktree's tactical shifts respond to currently observable conditions (spreads, valuations, ease of doing dumb deals, investor ebullience), not forecasts: 'we'd better know where we are'.
4. Having an opinion about the future is fine, but positions should never require that opinion to be right to succeed.
5. Oaktree's tenets: bottom-up research instead of macro-forecasting, and staying fully invested when bargains exist rather than raising cash to time markets.
6. Three allocator questions: prosperity or not, fear of loss vs fear of missing opportunity (answered mainly by cheapness), and whether conditions reward 'money and nerve' or caution and selectivity.
7. Investment gains are above average when expectations underestimate the eventual reality; 1999's ardor set up a lost decade, while 2012's low valuations and investor indifference may set up upside surprises.

## Market Context

January 2012, during the European sovereign debt crisis, with talk of a US double-dip recession, US fiscal austerity concerns, Treasury yields around 2%, high yield spreads near 700 bp and US stocks at about 12 times earnings after a flat twelve years.

## Notable Claims

- US equity P/E history.
- Normal high yield spread range vs current spread.
- Buying high yield above 550 bp spread historically outperforms.
- Stocks rose 20% a year on average in the 1990s.
- S&P 500 flat for twelve years while earnings grew.

## Quotes

> In any endeavor involving uncertainty, not knowing what lies ahead isn’t nearly as bad as thinking you know if you don’t.

> “we may never know where we’re going, but we’d better know where we are.”

> It’s one thing to have an opinion; it’s something very different to assume it’s right and act on that assumption.
