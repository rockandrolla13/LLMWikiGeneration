# "Risk in Today's Markets" Revisited

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** April 11, 1994 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/1994-04-11-risk-in-todays-markets-revisted.pdf)

## Summary

A follow-up written seven weeks after Marks's February 1994 memo 'Risk in Today's Markets', which had warned that strong 1993 returns and low safe yields had pushed investors far out the risk curve. After the Fed's small February 4 rate rise, bonds sold off sharply and the damage spread to equities, foreign bonds and commodities. Marks uses the episode to draw lessons: price paid matters as much as fundamentals, investor psychology had swung to a euphoric extreme, interest rates link all asset prices, and globalised, leveraged hedge funds tied markets together. The blow-ups at Soros's Quantum Fund, Steinhardt and Askin's Granite Funds illustrate that labels like 'hedge' or 'market neutral' mean little, that leverage on volatile positions is dangerous, that forced selling degrades portfolio quality, and that incentive fees create asymmetric payoffs for managers. He closes by contrasting this with his own approach: unleveraged, low-risk exploitation of inefficient markets that puts avoiding losses ahead of chasing profits.

## Key Ideas

1. Investment success depends at least as much on the price paid for an asset as on its fundamentals; good fundamentals can be overvalued.
2. When market participants behave as though nothing can go wrong, psychology has reached an extreme of the pendulum and should be acted against.
3. Interest rates affect the discounted value of every asset, and relative-value pricing ('investing is the discipline of relative selection') transmits a bond selloff to stocks and other assets.
4. Globalised investors and a small group of large leveraged macro hedge funds made asset prices more interconnected than expected, undermining diversification.
5. Labels such as 'hedge fund' or 'market neutral' guarantee nothing; investors must know what their manager actually does and how much risk is taken.
6. One-year incentive fees give managers a heads-I-win-tails-you-lose payoff, making net outcomes worse than symmetric for clients.
7. Volatility plus leverage is dangerous: margin calls force sales, and in illiquid markets managers sell their best, most liquid holdings first, degrading portfolio quality and removing hedges.
8. Marks's stated approach is the unleveraged, low-risk exploitation of inefficient markets, putting loss avoidance ahead of profit seeking.

## Market Context

Early 1994 bond market rout. The Federal Reserve raised short rates on February 4, 1994 after strong 1993 returns in risky strategies. Long Treasury yields jumped, and the selloff spread to equities, foreign bonds and commodities. Other worries named in the memo: tensions with Korea, Japan and China, a strong yen, Whitewater, and Mexico's stock-market correction after the assassination of the leading presidential candidate. Large macro hedge funds (Soros, Steinhardt) and Askin's mortgage-backed Granite Funds suffered large losses.

## Notable Claims

- The 30-year Treasury yield rose from 6.21% to 7.40% between January 28 and April 4, 1994, with price down 14%.
- Steinhardt lost $1 billion of $5 billion; Askin's Granite Funds investors may have lost all $600 million.
- With a 20% incentive fee taken on a 50% gain, a subsequent 33% decline leaves the client 7% under water.
- A leveraged long-Treasury position with 6% down is wiped out by a 1% rise in yield.
- The correction began with the Fed's February 4 rate rise.
