# Uncertainty

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** May 11, 2020 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/uncertainty.pdf)

## Summary

Written during the COVID-19 lockdown, this is a philosophical memo devoted entirely to our inability to know the future. Marks argues that economics has tendencies, not laws, and that four simultaneous unprecedented shocks (pandemic, contraction, oil collapse, policy response) leave no relevant history to extrapolate from. Being an above-average forecaster is hard for the same reason markets are hard to beat: consensus forecasts are already in prices, and the profitable forecasts of radical change are rarely right. He then turns to bias, especially confirmation bias, and to the value of doubt: 'investing scared' leads to due diligence, conservative assumptions and a margin of safety. He illustrates with Bruce Karsh deploying about $450 million a week after Lehman despite doubts. He closes on intellectual and epistemic humility: investors need confidence, but it should be proportional to the evidence, and they should judge how likely each of their forecasts is to be right.

## Key Ideas

1. Economics has only tendencies, not 'if a, then b' rules, so the macro future cannot be known with precision.
2. With no historical precedent for several interacting shocks, extrapolation and past-average responses fail.
3. Out-forecasting others is as hard as beating an efficient market: consensus forecasts are priced in, and forecasts of radical deviation are rarely right.
4. What we do know about the macro future is usually what everyone knows, so it does not produce above-average returns.
5. Confirmation bias shapes how investors form views; an objective process and an open mind are the ideal.
6. 'Investing scared' sharpens focus, demands a margin of safety and limits losses, which explains survival in high yield and distressed debt.
7. Confidence is essential for contrarian positions, but it must be reason-based and proportional to evidence, not hubris.
8. Forecasters should state both a view and the likelihood that the view is correct, and size bets accordingly.

## Market Context

May 2020, nearly two months into US COVID-19 lockdowns. The pandemic, a sharp economic contraction, an oil price collapse and an enormous Fed and Treasury response were happening at once, with heated debate over V, U, W or L-shaped recoveries and reopening.
