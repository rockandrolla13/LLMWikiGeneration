# Go Figure!

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** November 14, 2016 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/go-figure.pdf)

## Summary

Written the week after the November 2016 US presidential election, this memo uses Trump's surprise victory to show that forecasters cannot predict events and that no one can predict how markets will react to them. Polls and models had given Clinton 71-90% odds, and markets had treated a Trump win as negative, yet the Dow rose every day of election week to a record high. Marks offers the after-the-fact logic of the rally: a pro-business agenda of tax cuts, deregulation and infrastructure spending. He lists the risks: Fed independence, trade wars and international relations. He notes the sharp rise in bond yields and quotes Henry Kaufman on the end of the bond bull market and Stanley Druckenmiller's new optimism. He explains the polling miss through enthusiasm and turnout in Pennsylvania, Wisconsin and Michigan, and calls USC's 'right for the wrong reason' popular-vote poll a Talebian question. He also covers Senate constraints on Trump and the Electoral College's winner-take-all distortion. His conclusion: the range of outcomes under Trump is too wide to predict.

## Key Ideas

1. No one really knows what events will happen, and no one knows how the market will react to them; this reinforces Marks's view that investment decisions should not rest on macro forecasts.
2. Markets are often irrational in the short run because people drive them; the Dow rose both when Clinton was expected to win and after she lost.
3. Post-hoc logic for the rally: an expected pro-business agenda (tax reform, deregulation, $1 trillion infrastructure, repatriation holiday) implying fiscal stimulus, faster growth, higher inflation and more debt.
4. Bond yields rose sharply after the election; Henry Kaufman called it the end of the three-decade bond bull market; equity and bond investors view inflation differently, but the two cannot diverge forever.
5. Being right for the wrong reason (USC poll predicted a Trump popular-vote win, which did not happen) is common in investing and does not validate the forecaster.
6. Probabilistic forecasts that gave Trump a non-zero chance were not necessarily wrong, but few people accepted that the unlikely could happen.
7. Winner-take-all systems (Electoral College, early GOP primaries) convert vote shares into outcomes non-proportionally; Trump turned 47.3% of the popular vote into 57.9% of electoral votes.
8. The range of possible actions and outcomes under Trump was too broad to predict; that is the only certainty.

## Market Context

Mid-November 2016, immediately after Donald Trump's election. The US stock market had its best week since 2014, with the Dow at an all-time high, while interest rates rose quickly and bondholders took losses. Brexit had also surprised forecasters earlier in 2016. Investors were weighing expected fiscal stimulus, deregulation and tax cuts against risks to Fed independence and trade.

## Notable Claims

- FiveThirtyEight gave Clinton a 71% probability; most others gave 80-90%.
- The Dow rose almost 5% in election week to a new all-time high.
- A shortfall of 113,000 votes across PA, WI and MI decided the election.
- Trump's 47.3% of the popular vote translated into 57.9% of electoral votes.
- Trump got 5.1 electoral votes per million popular votes vs Clinton's 3.8.

## Quotes

> First, no one really knows what events are going to transpire.

> And second, no one knows what the market’s reaction to those events will be.

> These observations reinforce my belief that it’s a mistake to base investment decisions on macro forecasts.
