# Everyone Knows

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** April 26, 2007 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2007-04-26-everyone-knows.pdf)

## Summary

Marks argues that what the broad consensus finds obvious is almost always wrong in investing, because popular opinion bids prices up and removes the profit potential. He contrasts his early experience with the Nifty Fifty growth stocks at First National City Bank, which collapsed from p/e ratios of 80-90 to 8-9, with Michael Milken's 1978 case for B-rated bonds, which inspired Oaktree's motto about avoiding losers. He states that risk is highest where it is least perceived, that the herd is wrong about risk as often as about return, and that asset classes do not have fixed expected returns independent of the entry price, using the 1999 consensus of 11% equity returns as an example. He closes by warning about residential real estate, private equity and other 'silver bullets', while noting that being too far ahead of your time is indistinguishable from being wrong.

## Key Ideas

1. The consensus view is almost always wrong because coalescing opinion eliminates the profit potential of an investment.
2. There is no such thing as a good idea, only a good idea at a price; a too-high entry price can ruin any investment.
3. Superior returns come from seeing a quality others miss and having it turn out true; this requires being unconventional and often lonely.
4. Risk resides most where it is least perceived: feared assets get cheap and safe, adored assets get expensive and risky.
5. Quality does not determine risk; price does. The Nifty Fifty (high quality, high price) lost heavily while B-rated bonds outperformed.
6. Asset classes do not have a dependable return regardless of entry point; the 1999 S&P 500 at 29 times earnings returned 0.9% a year over seven years.
7. To be superior you must be more right than the consensus, and being too early looks the same as being wrong.

## Market Context

Written April 26, 2007, after the condo boom had deflated and before the wider 2007-08 credit crisis. Private equity, hedge funds and art were being touted as 'silver bullets'. The consensus equity return expectation had moved from about 11% in 1999 to about 7%.

## Notable Claims

- Nifty Fifty p/e ratios fell from 80-90 to 8-9.
- High yield new issue universe grew from $2 billion to $200 billion 1978-1990.
- S&P 500 returned 0.9% annualised in the seven years to March 31, 2007.
- Hickman's 1958 study found lower-rated bonds out-returned higher-rated ones 1900-1949.
- Valuation-adjusted S&P returns from p/e of 17.2 ranged 10.4%-11.1%.

## Quotes

> There’s no such thing as a good idea. Only a good idea at a price.

> The truth is, the herd is wrong about risk at least as often as it is about return.

> Being too far ahead of your time is indistinguishable from being wrong.
