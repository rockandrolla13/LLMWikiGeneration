# Tell Me I’m Wrong

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** January 22, 2010 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2010-01-22-tell-me-im-wrong.pdf)

## Summary

Self-described as a worrier, Marks lists the negatives he sees for the US economy after the 2009 rally, which he says has outpaced weak fundamentals. Profit gains came from cost cutting rather than revenue growth, and the recovery depends on government stimulus, likened to a tow truck pulling a stalled car. Near-zero rates helped banks and pushed investors into riskier assets, but they pose a dilemma: global considerations call for higher rates while domestic weakness needs low ones. He doubts consumers will return to debt-fueled spending, expects commercial real estate loans to go unpaid at maturity and small banks to fail, and flags state budget deficits, dependence on China, slow job creation, inflation and dollar risk, and rising populism. He expects an anemic, uneven recovery rather than a V. Because the distribution of outcomes has a fat left tail, he argues assets should be priced with a substantial margin for error, and that caution and discipline have replaced guts as the key to profit.

## Key Ideas

1. The 2009 rally outran fundamentals: 'green shoots' were mostly slower declines, and profits rose through cost cutting rather than top-line growth.
2. Judge stimulus against the counterfactual of no stimulus; it likely prevented a worse outcome, but the economy may only sputter once the 'tow truck' lets go.
3. Near-zero rates stimulate spending, subsidize banks and push savers into riskier assets, which helps explain why the riskiest assets rallied most.
4. Low US rates conflict with the need to keep foreign lenders, especially China, funding US deficits and supporting the dollar.
5. Consumer-credit-driven spending, commercial real estate valuations and state and municipal finances all face lasting headwinds after the boom.
6. Inflation hedges only matter if sized meaningfully (30-40% of a portfolio, not 5-15%); long-term bonds are most exposed to inflation and rising rates.
7. The future is a distribution, not a single scenario; with a large left tail, investors should demand margin for error, ample risk premiums and below-norm valuations.
8. The 1992-2007 period was unusually good and should not be taken as the 'normal' to return to.

## Market Context

January 2010, after the strong 2009 rally that recovered much of the 2008 decline. The Fed held short rates near zero, the government supported housing and autos (mortgage guarantees, first-time buyer credits, cash for clunkers), unemployment was high with 7.2 million jobs lost since December 2007, and states such as California and New York ran large deficits.

## Notable Claims

- Government backing of most new mortgages.
- Commercial real estate cap rates fell to 4% or less pre-crisis.
- Savings rate rebounded to about 5%.
- Return to 5% unemployment could take until late 2017 (quoting a Rutgers estimate).
- Inflation hedging needs a large allocation.

## Quotes

> Global considerations call for higher rates, but fighting domestic economic weakness relies on low rates.

> The question isn’t whether there’ll be a recovery, but what type.

> With most assets valued about fairly today, caution, discernment and discipline – not much needed in 2009 – have replaced guts as the essential elements in profitable investing.
