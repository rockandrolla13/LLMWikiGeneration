# What Really Matters?

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** November 22, 2022 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/what-really-matters.pdf)

## Summary

A year-end synthesis that first lists what does not matter for investors, then what does. Short-term macro events do not matter because few can predict them and prices react to events relative to unknown expectations. The trading mentality does not matter: Marks uses the 'trading sardines' joke to argue that stocks should be owned as business interests, not traded. Short-term performance is a weak signal because outcomes do not reveal decision quality. Volatility is not risk; the Sharpe ratio is an imperfect proxy, low-volatility goals cost return (as with hedge funds after 2000), and long-lived investors can tolerate fluctuations. He questions whether private funds' small reported 2022 losses reflect reality, citing an FT article on 'volatility laundering'. Hyperactivity hurts returns. What matters is long-run performance of holdings, built by studying companies, buying at attractive prices, holding, and riding compounding. He closes on asymmetry: alpha shows up as gaining more in good markets than one loses in bad ones, illustrated with manager tables A to G.

## Key Ideas

1. Prices move on events relative to expectations, and nobody knows what macro expectations are already priced in.
2. Treat stocks as ownership interests, not 'trading sardines'; DALBAR data show short-holding investors lag the index.
3. Outcome alone cannot judge decision quality; one quarter's return is like judging a hitter on one at-bat.
4. Volatility is not risk; risk is the probability of a bad outcome, and reducing volatility for its own sake usually lowers returns.
5. Private fund marks in 2022 may reflect 'volatility laundering' that both GPs and LPs want, though public prices may overreact.
6. Trade less: you make money on what you hold, not on what you buy and sell ('Don't just do something; sit there').
7. Equity investors' main event is participating in secular growth and compounding; active tilts are 'embroidery around the edges'.
8. Asymmetry, capturing more upside than downside relative to the market, is the observable sign of alpha and the only justification for active fees.

## Market Context

November 2022. The first nine months of 2022 were among the worst on record for both stocks and bonds, inflation was high and the Fed was raising rates, and investors were preoccupied with inflation, rate hikes and recession odds. Private funds were reporting only small losses despite public market declines.

## Notable Claims

- DALBAR found investors lagged the S&P 500 by three points a year in 1992-2012 with six-month average holding periods.
- The S&P 500 returned 10.5% a year since 1926, turning $1 into over $13,000.
- Private equity reported Q1 2022 gains of 1.6% while global equities fell 22% (FT quote).
- Over about 18 years the average hedge fund delivered low volatility with modest single-digit returns.
- The Fed held the fed funds rate at zero for seven years after the GFC.

## Quotes

> But volatility is not risk.

> Think of buying and selling as an expense item, not a profit center.

> Asymmetry can only be demonstrated by the relatively few people with superior skill and insight.
