# We're Not In 1999 Anymore, Toto

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** December 31, 2000 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2000-12-31-were-not-in-1999-anymore.pdf)

## Summary

A year-end post-mortem on the tech-media-telecom (TMT) bubble that Marks had flagged a year earlier in 'bubble.com'. He catalogues how every 1999 extreme reversed in 2000: dot-com business models, IPO first-day pops, venture capital returns, star analysts' price targets and the NASDAQ itself. He traces the boom to a self-reinforcing 1990s macro backdrop and dates the turn in investor psychology to the 1998 Russian default and LTCM collapse, treating the TMT run as a late-cycle exception. The memo closes with a set of enduring lessons -- beware generalisations, worry about time, distrust 'this time is different', respect cycles, never forget valuation, read investor psychology -- and Oaktree's mindset of contrarianism, skepticism, humility and defensive investing.

## Key Ideas

1. The 2000 TMT crash reversed every 1999 excess; the IPO mania was the single biggest contributor to the bubble.
2. Venture capital distributions valued at market price can overstate realised returns if investors cannot sell at that price.
3. Analysts' price targets were circular, set on what investors might pay rather than on earnings and multiples.
4. Risk tolerance turned to risk aversion in Q3 1998 (Russia, LTCM); TMT kept rising as the classic last popular sector of a bull market.
5. Psychology can change much faster than fundamentals; a little fundamental deterioration plus pessimism can wreck prices.
6. Markets over-generalise at extremes, which lets distressed and principal investors find value when the baby is thrown out with the bathwater.
7. Long-run truths like 'stocks always outperform' need time, and time (or capital) can run out.
8. Oaktree mindset: contrarianism, skepticism, humility about forecasting, defensive investing; avoid the losers and the winners take care of themselves.

## Market Context

End of 2000, after the tech-media-telecom bubble burst: NASDAQ down 39% for the year, dot-com failures, IPO and venture capital markets shut, retail and auto sales down, layoffs and profit warnings, rising defaults and bankruptcies, tight credit, energy price spikes, Middle East tensions and the contested US presidential election. NASDAQ still traded at about 90 times earnings.

## Notable Claims

- NASDAQ rose 86% in 1999 and fell 39% in 2000.
- The 14 'bubble.com' stocks were down 82% from year-end 1999 and 87% from their 2000 highs.
- 2000 IPOs averaged a 55% first-day gain but two-thirds traded below issue price.
- Distressed bonds yielding over 20% grew $6bn/yr before mid-1998 and $38bn/yr after, while defaults grew $18bn/yr.
- Analysts' six revisited price targets predicted +64% on average; the stocks fell 88%.

## Quotes

> if we avoid the losers, the winners will take care of themselves.

> Trees don't grow to the sky. Few things go to zero.
