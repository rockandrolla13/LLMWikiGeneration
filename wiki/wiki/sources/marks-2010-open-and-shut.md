# Open and Shut

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** December 1, 2010 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2010-12-01-open-and-shut.pdf)

## Summary

Built mostly from passages of earlier memos, this memo argues that the credit cycle is the most volatile and influential of the cycles: economies fluctuate a little, profits more, but the credit window swings from wide open to slammed shut. Marks lays out the self-reinforcing sequence in which prosperity brings expanded lending, then unwise lending, losses, a lending freeze and the end of prosperity. He contrasts the traits of tight and generous capital markets and applies them to 2010, when government stimulus, near-zero rates and QE pushed investors toward riskier assets and revived pre-crisis deals such as CCC, covenant-lite and PIK bonds, dividend recaps and 100-year bonds. Bargains like those of late 2008 are gone, so the needed qualities are now risk control, selectivity, discipline and patience.

## Key Ideas

1. Investor psychology swings like a pendulum and spends more time at the extremes than at the midpoint.
2. The credit cycle is the most volatile cycle; it opens wide and slams shut, magnifying modest changes in GDP and profits into market booms and busts.
3. Prosperity brings expanded lending, then unwise lending, large losses, a lending stop and the end of prosperity, and the cycle repeats.
4. Generous capital markets mean high prices, skimpy risk premiums and declining issue quality; credit crunches produce bargains.
5. Near-zero rates and QE drive investors out the risk curve: buyers who refused high yield at 12% or 20% accept 7% because Treasurys yield 2%.
6. Pre-crisis behaviour had returned by 2010: CCC and covenant-lite issuance, PIK bonds, dividend recaps, 100-year bonds and drive-by deals.
7. In a market without bargains, capital and nerve are not enough; risk control, selectivity, discernment, discipline and patience are needed.

## Market Context

December 2010, after the 2009 recovery and around the start of QE2. Credit markets were again wide open, Treasury yields were very low, and assets such as Treasurys, gold, commodities, non-dollar currencies and emerging-market real estate and stocks had appreciated. Marks notes high-yield spreads were above average and equity p/e ratios below the historic average.

## Notable Claims

- The credit cycle is the most volatile cycle.
- Self-reinforcing credit cycle sequence.
- Low Treasury yields push buyers into high yield at 7%.
- TIPS issued at a real yield of minus 0.55%.
- Cheap central bank funding is equivalent to a subsidy to banks.

## Quotes

> Look around the next time there’s a crisis; you’ll probably find a lender.

> the credit window opens wide and then slams shut

> Instead of capital and nerve, then, the indispensable elements are now risk control, selectivity, discernment, discipline and patience.
