# Hemlines

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** September 10, 2010 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2010-09-10-hemlines.pdf)

## Summary

Marks compares investment fashions to hemlines: they can only go up and down, so they do. He traces a recurring cycle of discovery, popularity, mania and crash, summed up by the adage 'What the wise man does in the beginning, the fool does in the end.' He then tells the postwar history of stocks (from a speculative asset to the nifty-fifty, the 'Death of Equities' in 1979, the 1980s-90s cult of equities and the post-2000 lost decade) and of bonds as its mirror image, with bond allocations at all-time lows just when they were needed in 2008. In 2010 investors were pouring money into bonds and fleeing stocks. Marks argues the right question is always 'at what price?', that a 2.5% ten-year Treasury has more room to go wrong than right, and that high-quality large-cap stocks and high yield bonds looked more attractive than Treasurys and high grade bonds.

## Key Ideas

1. Investment styles swing like a pendulum between fixed limits because people take trends to extremes and neither extreme is a permanent solution.
2. Fads follow a cycle: discovery of undervaluation, rising popularity, mania in which price becomes immaterial, then disillusionment and crash.
3. Above-average past returns usually borrowed from the future and imply below-average returns ahead.
4. The right question in 1999 was how stocks perform when bought at a P/E of 33, not how they perform on average.
5. There is no such thing as a good or bad idea in investing; anything can be good at one price and bad at another.
6. Investors extrapolate trends that should regress to the mean, and turn conservative only after a crisis.
7. Relative attractiveness depends on price relative to merits, not on an asset's label or capital-structure position.
8. In 2010 Treasurys and high grade bonds were priced for everything to go right, while stocks and high yield bonds offered better odds across scenarios.

## Market Context

September 2010, after the 2007-09 crisis. Stocks had recovered only about half their losses and stood at 1998 levels. Investors were pulling money from equity funds and putting record sums into bond funds. The ten-year Treasury yielded about 2.5%, there were fears of a double dip and deflation, and corporations held more than $1.8 trillion in cash.

## Notable Claims

- S&P 500 averaged 15.4% a year from 1979 through 1990.
- S&P P/E rose from 7 in 1980 to above 33 in 1999.
- Stocks fell 58% from the 2007 peak to the 2009 trough.
- Record bond fund inflows (Bloomberg, citing ICI).
- S&P 500 P/E of 14.4 vs a post-1954 average of 16.5.

## Quotes

> Thus the best way to view investment trends may be through an analogy to hemlines: all they can do is go up and down, and so they do.

> “What the wise man does in the beginning, the fool does in the end.”

> Turning conservative after a crisis smacks of closing the barn door after the horse has left, but it’s a regular feature of investor psychology.
