# The Role of Confidence

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** August 5, 2013 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2013-08-05-the-role-of-confidence.pdf)

## Summary

Marks defines confidence as a mix of belief, optimism and certainty and argues it works like the wealth effect: confident people spend and invest, which strengthens the economy and reinforces confidence, a self-fulfilling loop that stops when confidence dies. He contrasts 2007, when people felt certain they understood the economy, with 2013, marked by a long list of uncertainties (halting US recovery, QE and a $3 trillion Fed balance sheet, eurozone austerity, China, Japan under Abe). High confidence feels good but breeds leverage and overpricing, so the less confident 2013 environment is a sounder base -- except that Fed low-rate policy coerced unconfident investors into risk, which showed in the May-June 2013 taper-tantrum selloff. He covers the confidence pendulum, all-good versus all-bad thinking, the three stages of a bull market, and 'sure things' such as portfolio insurance, portable alpha, risk parity and the Greenspan/Bernanke put. He concludes that markets sit in a middle ground where prudence, not cleverness, is called for, and quotes Seth Klarman on long-run fiscal risk.

## Key Ideas

1. Confidence = belief + optimism + certainty; like the wealth effect, it is circular and self-fulfilling for both the economy and asset prices.
2. High confidence in 2007 produced overspending, overleverage and asset prices out of proportion to value; low confidence in 2013 is less pleasant but more prudent.
3. Central bank low-rate policy forced unconfident investors into risk, leaving holdings fragile, as the May-June 2013 swoon on taper talk showed.
4. One of the key swings of the pendulum is between too much confidence and too little; confidence peaks after prices have already soared.
5. All-good or all-bad thinking eliminates balanced analysis; the air goes out of the balloon much faster than it goes in.
6. Three stages of a bull market: a few believe, most see improvement, everyone is sure it will last forever; cheapness is the raw material and too much confidence makes tops.
7. No mechanical 'sure thing' (portfolio insurance, portable alpha, risk parity, the Fed put) works in all conditions.
8. When there is nothing clever to do, the mistake lies in trying to be clever.

## Market Context

August 2013: a slow US recovery five years after the crisis, tax increases and sequestration, Fed QE with a balance sheet above $3 trillion, Bernanke's May 2013 tapering signal and the resulting June stock and bond selloff (the 'taper tantrum', a term not used in the memo), eurozone austerity and a coming UK EU referendum, China slowdown worries, Abenomics in Japan, and US fiscal and entitlement concerns.

## Notable Claims

- The Fed's balance sheet had swelled to over $3 trillion.
- The Dow fell 22.6% on Black Monday 1987 and portfolio insurance sell orders were not executed.
- Confidence went from 'It's All Good' (July 16, 2007) to 'Now It's All Bad?' (September 10) in eight weeks.
- Risk parity failed to prevent losses in 2013 when Bernanke signalled tapering.
- Most assets were neither dangerously elevated (except perhaps long Treasuries and high grades) nor compellingly cheap.

## Quotes
