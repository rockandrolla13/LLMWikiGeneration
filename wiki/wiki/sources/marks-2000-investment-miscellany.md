# Investment Miscellany

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** November 16, 2000 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2000-11-16-investment-miscellany.pdf)

## Summary

A collection of short essays written as the dot-com bubble deflated. Marks describes how the bubble that crested in March 2000 'disrespected' money and traditional values: risk was treated as a friend and steady returns such as 11% on high yield bonds were ignored. He critiques a consultant's asset allocation model that projected 14% equity returns and defined risk so that more equities meant less risk, arguing that single-digit earnings growth and no further p/e expansion imply single-digit equity returns. The centrepiece summarises Richard Bookstaber's framework of market crises as liquidity events: liquidity demanders value time over price, liquidity suppliers vanish in a crisis, prices become 'countereconomic', and diversification fails because assets held by the same traders become correlated. Marks ties this to Oaktree's distressed debt approach of buying from forced sellers. He closes by rebutting James Glassman's claim that faster information makes markets safer: efficiency is not accuracy, and information is not knowledge.

## Key Ideas

1. The 1999-2000 tech bubble inverted traditional values: risk paid off best and caution, fixed income and value investing were ignored.
2. Defining risk as the chance of missing an 8% target made a model show more equities as lower risk; Marks rejects that framing in favour of portfolio volatility.
3. Long-run equity return averages (10% for 1929-92, 13% for 1940-99) were flattered by rising p/e ratios and should not be extrapolated from high starting multiples.
4. Bookstaber's framework: markets exist to meet liquidity demand; in crises falling prices trigger more selling and drive buyers away.
5. Diversification fails in a crisis because what matters is who holds the assets, not their economic link; LTCM and emerging markets in 1998 dragged high yield down.
6. Oaktree's distressed approach seeks forced sellers and acts as a liquidity supplier rather than an eager buyer.
7. A buying panic, as in 1999, is as much a liquidity-driven crisis as a selling panic like 1987.
8. Fast information is not the same as knowledge; market efficiency does not mean the consensus is accurate.

## Market Context

November 2000, after the tech/IPO bubble crested in March 2000. Dot-com firms such as Priceline spin-off WebHouse Club were shutting down, high yield bond prices were sagging on outflows into equity funds, and 1999's best portfolios were doing worst in 2000. The memo also looks back at the 1987 crash and the September 1998 LTCM and emerging markets crisis.

## Notable Claims

- The consultant model projected 14% equity returns with risk defined as missing an 8% five-year average.
- Equities returned 28% in 1995-99.
- Historic average returns depend on the chosen period.
- A false press release moved Emulex from $103 to $45 in twenty minutes on August 25, 2000.
- Flows into high yield will resume when 11% is seen as a good return.

## Quotes

> Efficiency and accuracy are two very different things.

> Information isn't knowledge.

> We call them forced sellers, and they provide our best bargains.
