# Inspiration from the World of Sports

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** October 22, 2015 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2015-10-22-inspiration-from-the-world-of-sports.pdf)

## Summary

Prompted by Yogi Berra's death, Marks draws investing lessons from sports. Berra's consistency and few strikeouts illustrate the value of minimising error, and his sayings show that markets are made of people, so psychology matters as much as analysis. Daily fantasy football shows that the goal is to find players (assets) whose price understates their merit, not the best players. New York Post football forecasters clustered around 50% accuracy, did worse on their 'best bets', and would have lost money after the bookie's vig, mirroring active investors after fees. Pete Carroll's intercepted Super Bowl pass shows that a decision cannot be judged by its outcome alone, and Novak Djokovic's comments show that superior results need self-confidence, but only when warranted.

## Key Ideas

1. Consistency and minimisation of error (Yogi Berra's few strikeouts) are dependable routes to superior long-term results, and Oaktree prioritises them.
2. Markets are made of people, so beating them requires understanding human behaviour as well as financial analysis.
3. Bargains that everyone believes in cannot still be bargains; near-unanimous enthusiasm drives bubbles.
4. The goal is not the best assets or the cheapest, but assets whose price understates their potential, as in fantasy football drafting.
5. Forecasters' accuracy near 50% with symmetric dispersion suggests little skill; costs (the vig, fees) turn a coin-flip edge into a loss.
6. Judge decisions by process, a priori probability, alternatives and alternative histories, not by a single outcome.
7. Great investments start in discomfort and require warranted self-confidence; over-confidence is more dangerous than too little.

## Market Context

Written October 2015, shortly after Yogi Berra's death in late September. Marks mentions social media companies as the 2015 object of 'everyone knows' enthusiasm and the growth of daily fantasy sports, which Nevada had just banned. The memo is mainly philosophical rather than a market call.

## Notable Claims

- New York Post forecasters were right about half the time.
- Forecasters did worse on their best bets.
- Following the Post picks would have lost $789 despite >50% accuracy.
- Over 100 passes from the one-yard line that season, none intercepted.
- Reggie Jackson's strikeout-to-home-run ratio was four times Berra's.
