# Safety First . . . But Where?

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** April 10, 2001 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2001-04-10-safety-first-but-where.pdf)

## Summary

Written after the 2000 tech-bubble peak, this memo asks where investors should look for good risk-adjusted returns once sheer aggressiveness stops paying. Marks reviews the 1990s favourites one by one: 'stocks for the long run', active management, S&P 500 index funds, stocks of great companies, high-grade bonds, media pundits and faith in Greenspan's Fed. He finds each was oversold or misunderstood, usually because price was ignored and because markets compete away excess returns. His prescription is to accept change and work with cycles, to seek genuine alpha (skill that others lack) in inefficient markets, and to pursue returns not dependent on market advances, such as high yield bonds, convertibles, distressed debt and absolute-return strategies. He predicts hedge funds will become the next fad and lists the caveats: modest realistic returns, imperfect 'absolute' returns, asset growth destroying records, weaker managers getting funded, and rising fees eating alpha.

## Key Ideas

1. Stock returns cannot outrun corporate profit growth for long; periods of p/e expansion are followed by poorer periods, so return expectations must be reasonable.
2. Volatility becomes permanent loss when an investor is forced to sell at the bottom; time frame determines what fluctuations can be survived.
3. Index funds stopped being unbiased in 1999 when the S&P 500 added hot tech names, creating a self-reinforcing momentum loop that reversed in 2000.
4. Buying great companies regardless of price is among the riskiest approaches; Nifty-Fifty and 2000-2001 tech leaders show that a great company is not a great investment.
5. Alpha is skill, it is differential advantage over others, and it is personal; it can only pay off consistently in markets that are not efficient.
6. Every popular strategy eventually draws enough money to guarantee its failure, because markets act to eliminate excess returns.
7. With single-digit equity returns expected, investors should seek non-market-based returns: coupon-bearing high yield and convertibles, distressed debt, and absolute-return strategies.
8. Hedge funds will become the next fad; asset inflows, second-string managers and fee increases will dilute alpha and produce many disappointments.

## Market Context

April 2001, after the 1999/early-2000 tech-stock peak and the 2000 downturn. Index funds underperformed active managers in 2000, high-grade bonds had a strong 2000 (Lehman Government/Credit Index up 11.9%), the Fed under Greenspan had begun cutting rates in January 2001, and hedge funds and absolute-return strategies were attracting attention after doing well amid the chaos of 2000.

## Notable Claims

- Tech stocks were about 40% of the S&P 500 by end-1999.
- Microsoft, Intel and Cisco were down 53%, 68% and 83% from their highs.
- Estimated annual returns from 1973 highs for Avon, Kodak and Polaroid were .4%, (.4%) and (10.4%).
- S&P 500 averaged 17.6% per year from 1978 through 1999.
- An unprecedented 3% of investment-grade corporate bonds fell 30% or more in 2000.

## Quotes

> Pursuit of return must be balanced against aversion to risk.

> we'd much rather buy a so-so asset cheap than a great asset dear.

> Just as nature abhors a vacuum, markets act to eliminate an excessive return.
