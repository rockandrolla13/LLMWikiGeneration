# The Outlook for Equities

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** March 13, 2013 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2013-03-13-the-outlook-for-equities.pdf)

## Summary

Triggered by a Pensions & Investments line that 'the long-term equity risk premium is typically between 4.5% and 5%', Marks attacks investment generalisations and the use of the present tense for the equity risk premium. He distinguishes four meanings: historic excess returns, past demanded premium, currently demanded premium, and the future excess return, and argues only the first can be measured while only the fourth matters. Unlike a bond's credit spread, a stock's prospective return cannot be computed without large guesses about the far future. Strong past returns borrow from future returns, as the 1990s borrowed from the 2000s. He compares the S&P 500 earnings yield to the T-bill rate: about 325 bp in the post-war norm, 112 bp in 2000, and 525 bp in 2013, which looks very cheap but depends on artificially low rates. He lists pros and cons, then argues investor attitudes remain tepid and places equities in the first half of stage two of a bull market, expecting a pleasant surprise relative to a 6% consensus.

## Key Ideas

1. The equity risk premium has four different meanings; only the historic one is measurable and only the future one matters.
2. A bond's promised return is a simple calculation; a stock's requires guesses about earnings growth to infinity or a terminal p/e.
3. Above-trend returns pull future appreciation into the present, so strong recent performance lowers expected future returns.
4. In the 1990s equities returned about 20% a year, and investors raised their return expectations just before the 2000 peak.
5. Earnings yield versus the risk-free rate (525 bp, 6.25x in 2013) makes stocks look cheap, but this depends on low interest rates.
6. Cons include Shiller CAPE pointing to full valuation, record profit margins, and slower expected earnings growth.
7. Three stages of a bull market (and of a bear market) frame where sentiment stands; Marks puts 2013 in the first half of stage two.
8. A rally can be powered by a switch from fear of losing money to fear of missing opportunity when allocations are low.

## Market Context

March 2013. Interest rates were near zero due to central-bank stimulus, the S&P 500 had risen about 16% in 2012 and 10% so far in 2013, and pundits were discussing a 'great rotation' from bonds into equities. Pension funds were reluctant to add stocks. Equities were emerging from a 'lost decade' after 2000.

## Notable Claims

- At the 2000 high the equity yield differential was only about 112 bp.
- In 2013 the earnings-yield differential over T-bills was 525 bp.
- Equity returns averaged 20% a year in the 1990s.
- Stocks beat bonds by an unusually wide margin in the six months to January 31, 2013.
- The S&P 500 returned about 13% in the year after 'Déjà Vu All Over Again'.

## Quotes

> A move upward can be powered by a switch from the fear of losing money to the fear of missing opportunity.

> Extrapolation or analysis? They’re two very different things.

> When attitudes are moderate and allocations are low, it doesn’t take much.
