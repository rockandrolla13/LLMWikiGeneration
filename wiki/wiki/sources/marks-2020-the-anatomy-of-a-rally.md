# The Anatomy of a Rally

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** June 18, 2020 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/the-anatomy-of-a-rally.pdf)

## Summary

Marks dissects the record-fast stock market recovery after the Covid-19 crash of February-March 2020. He catalogues the reasons for the rally: faith in the Fed and Treasury, a flattening infection curve, vaccine optimism, 'looking across the valley' to 2022 earnings, and above all ultra-low rates that lower demanded returns across the yield curve and capital market line and spill over from Fed-bought assets to assets the Fed does not buy. He adds behavioural fuel: buy-the-dips, a perceived 'Powell put', FOMO replacing fear of loss, and speculative retail trading such as in bankrupt Hertz. He lists the negatives investors overlooked and maps the episode onto the three stages of a bull market, concluding that the rally rests on optimism and liquidity and that, at current prices, the odds are not in investors' favour.

## Key Ideas

1. Market timeline: S&P 500 peak of 3,386 on Feb 19, a 34% fall in five weeks to 2,237 on March 23, then a nearly 45% rebound by June 8.
2. A low risk-free rate is the origin of the yield curve and capital market line, so cutting it lowers demanded returns on every asset, including ones the Fed does not buy.
3. Fed buying is non-discriminating on value and keeps zombie companies alive, which signals tolerance for prices above financial reality and for moral hazard.
4. FOMO replacing fear of losing money is the pivotal shift in market mood; passive index/ETF flows may perpetuate trends once set in motion.
5. Psychology exaggerates both positives and negatives, so extreme highs and lows are the rule in cycles; perception swings from 'flawless' to 'hopeless'.
6. The 2020 episode moved through the three stages of a bull market, skipping quickly from stage one to stage three.
7. 'Overpriced' is not the same as 'sure to decline soon'.
8. A checklist for judging a rally: are positives and negatives weighed dispassionately, are drivers fundamental or liquidity-driven, how do valuations compare to norms?

## Market Context

Mid-June 2020: after the fastest-ever bear market triggered by Covid-19, the Fed's March 23 expansion of support, near-zero rates guided to stay through 2021-2022, massive Treasury stimulus, record bond issuance, retail speculation in bankrupt stocks like Hertz, while GDP was expected to fall 20-30% in Q2 and unemployment to sit near 10%.

## Notable Claims

- S&P 500 fell 34% in five weeks to 2,237.
- A 17.6% three-day gain on March 24-26, the biggest in over 80 years.
- High yield bond yields tripled in four and a half weeks during the selloff.
- S&P 500 rose another 13% after Druckenmiller and Tepper called it overvalued.
- S&P 500 at 3,113, only 8% off its all-time high, when the memo was written.

## Quotes

> Thus FOMO – fear of missing out – seemed to take over from the prior fear of losing money, a transition that's always pivotal in determining the mood of the market.

> In this line of work, if you never feel humbled, it just means you haven't realistically appraised your performance.

> In other words, the fundamental outlook may be positive on balance, but with listed security prices where they are, the odds aren't in investors' favor.
