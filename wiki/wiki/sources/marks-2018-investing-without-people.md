# Investing Without People

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** June 18, 2018 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/investing-without-people.pdf)

## Summary

Marks examines three trends that reduce the role of people in markets: passive and index investing (including ETFs and smart-beta funds), quantitative investing (systematic factor investing and statistical arbitrage), and artificial intelligence and machine learning. He traces indexing from his Chicago professors through Malkiel and Bogle, and argues that passive investing free-rides on the price discovery of active investors, so its wisdom depends on some people still investing actively. He warns that passive flows can lift index and smart-beta constituents for non-fundamental reasons, that ETFs cannot be more liquid than their underlying assets, and that cap weighting overweights the most expensive stocks. He sees quant investing as rule-following that excludes emotion but is capacity-limited and subject to reflexivity, citing LTCM and Renaissance. He concludes that computers will beat most investors, but the best few with qualitative, long-term judgment should keep a role.

## Key Ideas

1. Index investing is a 'can't lose' and also a 'can't win' strategy: it cannot fall behind or beat the index.
2. Passive investors free-ride on active investors' pricing; if active investing shrinks enough, mispricings should return and active management could pay again.
3. Passive and smart-beta inflows give index constituents an artificial popularity that can lift prices, and that buying could reverse into forced selling in a downturn.
4. Cap-weighted indices put more money in the more expensive stocks, so weighting by fundamentals such as earnings may be better (Rob Arnott's argument).
5. ETFs are not more liquid than their underlying assets; investors who expect otherwise may be disappointed when the tide goes out.
6. Quant strategies (factor investing and stat arb) remove emotion and human error, but capacity is limited and, per Soros's reflexivity, formulas lose their edge as they are adopted.
7. Quant rules must be constantly renewed; most quants have operated only in a period of falling rates and low inflation and volatility.
8. Computers handle what can be counted; until they have creativity, taste and judgment, investors with qualitative, long-term insight keep a role.

## Market Context

Written in June 2018, roughly nine years into the post-2009 bull market, after a long period of falling rates and low inflation and volatility. Flows had been moving from active to passive funds since about 2012, ETFs had proliferated into narrow smart-beta themes, quant firms such as Renaissance and Two Sigma were prominent, and machine learning was attracting attention. ETFs had not yet been tested in a serious market break.

## Notable Claims

- Passive share of US stock fund assets in 2017 per the LA Times.
- Growth of the Vanguard 500 Index Fund.
- BTS fund held nearly all assets in two junk bond ETFs.
- Renaissance's own description of its edge.
- Passive investing grows because active results have been poor, not because passive returns are great.

> . . . not everything that can be counted counts, and not everything that counts can be counted.

— William Bruce Cameron, quoted by Marks
