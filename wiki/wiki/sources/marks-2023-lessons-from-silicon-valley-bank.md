# Lessons from Silicon Valley Bank

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** April 17, 2023 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/lessons-from-svb.pdf)

## Summary

Marks argues that SVB's March 2023 failure was largely a special case, not a harbinger of widespread bank failures, but that it may deepen lenders' existing wariness and tighten credit. SVB was unusual: concentrated in VC-backed startups in Northern California, deposits that tripled to $189 billion with little loan demand, half its assets in long-dated Treasurys and agency MBS bought at very low yields, and 94% uninsured, highly connected depositors who could withdraw online. What it shared with all banks is the toxic combination of an asset-liability mismatch, high leverage and reliance on trust, which makes banks 'highly levered fixed income investors'. He contrasts 2023 with the GFC, reviews the cyclical loosening of regulation (the 2018 threshold rise), defends the full deposit guarantee against moral-hazard objections, explains why Credit Suisse AT1 holders should not have been surprised to be wiped out, and flags commercial real estate loans as the bigger risk for banks.

## Key Ideas

1. SVB failed because of sector and regional concentration, a flood of deposits with little loan demand, long-dated bond purchases at tiny yields, and uninsured, interconnected depositors.
2. Banks combine an asset-liability mismatch with high leverage and depend on trust. No bank can have enough liquidity if enough depositors ask for their money at once.
3. Surviving on average is useless: 'Never forget the six-foot-tall person who drowned crossing the stream that was five feet deep on average.'
4. Buying long bonds in 2020-21, when the 30-year Treasury yielded between 0.99% and 2.45%, was mindless yield-chasing: 'profound risk and miniscule potential for return'.
5. Digital herding (social media, online withdrawals) accelerates bank runs: over a third of SVB's deposits left in one day, versus days of queues at Northern Rock in 2007.
6. 2023 is not 2008: nothing is analogous to subprime mortgages, SVB was not systemically central, and post-GFC rules left major banks well capitalised.
7. Regulation is cyclical: crises bring rules, success brings pushback, and the 2018 rise of the strict-standards threshold from $50 billion to $250 billion helped SVB grow.
8. AT1s were legally written down to zero. If people can take value from you legally, you shouldn't be surprised if they do (as with event risk in high yield). CRE loans are the larger looming bank risk.

## Market Context

April 2023, a month after the failures of Silicon Valley Bank and Signature Bank, the rescue of First Republic, and UBS's forced takeover of Credit Suisse, in which FINMA wrote $17 billion of AT1s to zero. The Fed had raised rates sharply through 2022 to fight inflation and responded to SVB by guaranteeing all deposits and adding liquidity. Office and other commercial real estate faced higher rates, refinancing needs and remote-work pressure.

## Notable Claims

- SVB deposits tripled from $62 billion to $189 billion in two years.
- 94% of SVB deposits were above the $250,000 FDIC limit.
- SVB's bond holdings fell $21 billion in market value as the Fed raised rates.
- The 30-year Treasury yielded between 0.99% and 2.45% when SVB bought its bonds.
- Banks hold about $1.8 trillion of CRE mortgages, roughly equal to the average bank's capital.

## Quotes

> There’s no source of meltdown – in any sector – as potentially toxic as the combination of high leverage and an asset/liability mismatch.

> The bottom line is that banks are, essentially, highly levered fixed income investors.

> It seems to me that if people can take value from you legally, and especially if doing so isn’t unambiguously immoral, you shouldn’t be surprised if they do.
