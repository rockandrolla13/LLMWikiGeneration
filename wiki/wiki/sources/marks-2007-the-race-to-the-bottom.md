# The Race to the Bottom

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** February 14, 2007 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2007-02-14-the-race-to-the-bottom.pdf)

## Summary

Marks treats money as a commodity: since one lender's capital is the same as another's, a provider who wants more market share must make its money cheaper. Capital can be cheapened by accepting lower returns, but also by accepting less safety, and in auctions the 'winner' is often whoever accepts the least protection. He uses UK lenders raising mortgage multiples to five times salary, the progressive loosening of US mortgage terms, dividend recapitalisations, SPAC terms, triple-A rated CPDOs levered 15-to-1, skipped due diligence and faster private equity deployment as examples. He spends a section on the erosion of high yield covenants, arguing that 'bad investors drive out good' and that covenant quality is a barometer of the market. He concludes that a global liquidity glut has produced a race to the bottom in prudence, and that the bigger the boom, the bigger the bust.

## Key Ideas

1. Money is a commodity, so capital providers compete on price, and on how much safety they will give up.
2. In capital markets, as in auctions, the deal goes to whoever accepts the least, which can make the 'winner' the loser.
3. Lending standards loosen in steps (teaser rates, higher loan-to-value, 100% financing, no documentation) as cycles reach extremes.
4. Originate-and-sell lending creates moral hazard because originators are insulated from later losses.
5. Covenant strength is a barometer of the credit market climate; undisciplined capital erodes it (Gresham's Law applied to investors).
6. Loose capital markets forestall defaults (lax covenants, toggle bonds), which raises later distress.
7. Everything else equal, the bigger the boom, the greater the bust; cycles are close to inevitable even if timing is not.
8. Refusing to follow carefree markets means lagging and looking old-fashioned for a while, which is a small price for preserving capital.

## Market Context

Early 2007, near the peak of the pre-crisis credit boom. A global liquidity glut, low interest rates, record LBO financing, dividend recaps, PIK-toggle bonds and weakening covenants were common. US subprime defaults had spiked the year before, while UK lenders were raising salary multiples.

## Notable Claims

- Abbey raised standard mortgage lending to five times salary.
- CPDOs lever 15-to-1 to deliver LIBOR+200.
- About 70% of syndicated loan buyers never visit data rooms.
- Private equity deployed capital roughly nine times faster in 2005 than 2002.
- Bigger booms produce bigger busts.

## Quotes

> Buying shows who was the highest bidder, not necessarily the smartest bidder.

> In other words, if you did a little of something and it didn’t work, try to recoup your losses by doing a lot.

> And usually, the air goes out of the balloon a lot faster than it goes in.
