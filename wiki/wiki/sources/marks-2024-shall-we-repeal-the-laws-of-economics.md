# Shall We Repeal the Laws of Economics?

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** September 19, 2024 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/shall-we-repeal-the-laws-of-economics.pdf)

## Summary

Written during the 2024 US presidential campaign, this memo argues that politicians' promises that ignore supply and demand eventually fail because the laws of economics apply to everyone. Building on his 2016 and 2019 memos on economic and political reality, Marks credits US prosperity to free markets and incentives, citing the North versus South Korea 'control group'. He examines Harris's proposed ban on grocery price gouging, arguing that 2021-22 food inflation came from pandemic demand, supply-chain disruption, stimulus and wage growth, and that raising prices in response to supply and demand is how markets work (Uber surge pricing, concert tickets, home sales, UAW wages). Price controls from WIN buttons to Venezuela failed because 'you can set prices for goods, but you can't make people produce them'. New York rent regulation has suppressed apartment supply. He also critiques down-payment subsidies, Trump's proposed 10% tariff and tax cuts, California's mandated minimum wages, the untouchable status of Social Security, and chronic US deficits. China's growth, he notes, rests heavily on its private sector. In politics there can be something for everyone, but in economics there are only tradeoffs.

## Key Ideas

1. Free markets direct capital, labor and effort to their most productive uses through incentives; they don't guarantee fair outcomes, but systems designed for fairness tend to fail economically.
2. 'Price gouging' rests on subjective words like unfair or excessive; price rises in response to supply and demand are normal market behavior, and collusion and price-fixing are already illegal.
3. Price controls produce shortages because governments can set prices but can't compel production; 'the best solution for high prices is high prices'.
4. New York rent regulation has kept rents too low to justify new building, with tiny supply growth and vacant stabilized units.
5. Demand subsidies such as down-payment grants tend to be capitalized into higher prices; tariffs act like consumer price increases and hamper globalization.
6. Chronic US deficits, the near-certain insolvency path of the Social Security trust fund, and interest exceeding the defense budget reflect ignoring economic constraints.
7. Interventions share common threads: commendable goals, uncontrolled second-order effects, zero-sum 'who gets what' choices, populist size thresholds, and officials exempting themselves.
8. China's growth reflects a pragmatic reliance on its private sector (60/70/80/90) rather than a refutation of free-market economics.

## Market Context

September 2024, in the US presidential election campaign between Donald Trump and Kamala Harris. Inflation had just fallen below 3% for the first time since March 2021 after the 2021-22 surge, grocery prices were up 26% since 2019, and annual interest on US national debt exceeded the defense budget.

## Quotes

> Here’s a shocker: you can set prices for goods, but you can’t make people produce them.

> Mandating lower prices is generally the least effective way to get them.

> In the world of politics, there can be limitless benefits and something for everyone. But in economics, there are only tradeoffs.
