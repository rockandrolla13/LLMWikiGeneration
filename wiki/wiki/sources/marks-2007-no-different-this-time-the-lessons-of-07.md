# No Different This Time – The Lessons of ‘07

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** December 17, 2007 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2007-12-17-no-different-this-time-the-lessons-of-07.pdf)

## Summary

Written five months after subprime defaults set off the 2007 credit crunch, this memo argues that nothing about the crisis was new. In a low-return world with a low, flat capital market line, investors insisted on high returns and reached for them through riskier assets, leverage and the 'silver bullets' of securitization and structure. Skepticism was absent: people believed disintermediation had reduced bank risk, relied on AA ratings for CDO tranches that yielded more than AA corporates, and ignored the many layers of fees in the CDO chain. Cheap Fed credit fuelled leverage, and leverage fattens the tails, turning falling prices into a vicious circle of credit withdrawal and forced sales. Marks explains why risk is hard to manage: it exists only in the future, extreme events are underestimated, worst cases are not bad enough, losses come lumpily, and models fail when markets change. He closes with twelve lessons that reduce to one: watch the supply and demand for investable capital and the eagerness to spend it.

## Key Ideas

1. In a low-return world, insisting on high returns led investors to reach for return through riskier assets and leverage, the main cause of the 2007 crunch.
2. Upswings are marked by a dearth of skepticism; myths such as 'disintermediation reduced risk' and reliance on credit ratings were exposed.
3. Higher yield for the same rating (AA CDO tranches vs AA corporates) was a false free lunch; the CDO chain paid at least five parties in fees.
4. Leverage does not make investments better; it magnifies gains and losses, fattens the tails, and borrowing short to buy long assets produces meltdowns (Bear Stearns fund, LTCM, Granite).
5. Using leverage to turn an inadequate return into an adequate one means buying more of an asset that is under-returning and due for a price decline.
6. Models and VaR extrapolate the past and fail when relationships change; UBS breached its VaR on 16 days in one quarter.
7. Risk is hard to manage because it exists only in the future, 'worst case' is not bad enough, and losses are concentrated in time.
8. Twelve lessons reduce to one: be alert to the supply and demand for investable funds; too much capital chasing too few ideas leads to low returns, thin margins for error and losses.

## Market Context

December 2007, early in the global financial crisis. Subprime mortgage defaults and downgrades of related securities began around July 30, 2007, and by December a full credit crunch was under way. Bear Stearns's High-Grade Structured Credit Strategies Enhanced Leveraged Fund had collapsed, asset-backed commercial paper could not be rolled, CDO debt was downgraded sharply, and banks including UBS and Citibank reported large losses. Background: years of Fed rates as low as 1%, record-low risk premiums, and a boom in buyouts, hedge funds and structured credit.

## Notable Claims

- UBS's trading losses exceeded prior-day VaR on 16 days in Q3 2007, versus none since 1998.
- The largest buyout funds grew from $3-5 billion to $20 billion-plus in a year or two.
- Borrowing $400 at 5% to add to $100 in a 6% asset turns the 6% into a 10% return.
- At least five parties were paid each time a mortgage loan was placed, securitized and distributed.
- Anticipated $5 billion writedowns became $8 billion in a few weeks.

## Quotes

> One must never forget that leverage doesn’t make investments better; it just magnifies the gains and losses

> Widespread disregard for risk creates great risk.

> No one ever went bankrupt because of an excess of risk consciousness.
