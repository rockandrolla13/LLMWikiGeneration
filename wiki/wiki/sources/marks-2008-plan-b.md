# Plan B

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** September 24, 2008 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2008-09-24-plan-b.pdf)

## Summary

Written days after the $700 billion TARP proposal, Marks explains that credit and the whole economy run on belief, and that financial institutions are vulnerable to runs because of opacity, leverage, risk bearing and funding illiquid assets with short-term money. He identifies two root causes of the crisis: huge losses on overrated complex mortgage-backed securities held by overleveraged institutions, and a vast credit default swap market whose counterparty chains no one could trust. He supports the Treasury plan, trusts Bernanke and Paulson, discusses the pricing, ownership, compensation and political obstacles, and argues that a short-selling ban is acceptable short-term but not long-term. He concludes that stimulus is right now, regulators should not tighten too early, and the plan can break the cycle of negativity.

## Key Ideas

1. Credit comes from 'credere', to believe; the economy runs on trust, and loss of faith causes runs on financial institutions.
2. Financial institutions are fragile because of opacity, leverage, conscious risk bearing and short-term funding of long-term illiquid assets.
3. A simple example: $1 million equity, $29 million borrowed, 20% defaults at two-thirds recovery wipes out equity twice over.
4. Root cause one: overrated mortgage-backed securities created by risk tolerance, securitisation, belief that home prices could not fall and rating-agency failures.
5. Root cause two: credit default swaps (over $60 trillion) create a daisy chain of counterparty risk.
6. The TARP price question (market price or subsidy) must be made explicit; Marks proposes government sharing returns above 20% rather than ownership.
7. Short selling is legitimate long-term, abusive short selling should be stopped, and a temporary ban was acceptable to protect the system.
8. The Fed over-stimulated in 2003-07; now stimulus is right, and tightening too early would repeat a Depression-era mistake.

## Market Context

September 2008, at the peak of the global financial crisis: Bear Stearns, IndyMac, Lehman Brothers, AIG, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac had failed or been rescued, Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley became bank holding companies, money market funds were backstopped, and the TARP proposal was being debated six weeks before the US presidential election.

## Notable Claims

- TARP proposed up to $700 billion to buy toxic mortgage securities.
- Over $60 trillion of CDS outstanding.
- Leverage arithmetic wipes out equity twice.
- Short selling banned in about 800 financial stocks.
- Berkshire Hathaway invested $5 billion in Goldman Sachs.
