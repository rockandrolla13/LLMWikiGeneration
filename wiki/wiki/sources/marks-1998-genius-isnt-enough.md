# Genius Isn't Enough (and Other Lessons from Long-Term Capital Management)

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** October 9, 1998 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/1998-10-09-genius-isn-39-t-enough.pdf)

## Summary

Written days after the rescue of Long-Term Capital Management, this memo draws lessons from the fund's collapse rather than retelling it. LTCM ran convergence bond-arbitrage trades with about $4.6 billion of equity supporting roughly $150 billion of positions and $1.25 trillion of derivative notional; price moves between August and late September erased 90% of its equity. Marks's central lesson is that brilliance does not let anyone control the future and that awe of genius stops investors asking questions. He lists seven further lessons: volatility plus leverage equals dynamite, hedge funds offer no magic, too-good-to-be-true usually is, something always goes wrong, never confuse brains with a bull market, the credit cycle is a powerful force, and financial memory is short. He closes with Oaktree's axioms of humility and margin for error.

## Key Ideas

1. Probabilistic models give likelihoods, not certainties; historic relationships fail precisely when faith in them is greatest, so allow for the improbable disaster.
2. Volatility plus leverage equals dynamite: at 25x leverage a 4% price decline wipes out equity.
3. Leverage should only be used to exploit already-wide spreads, never to turn low spreads into wide returns; Oaktree declined to organise CBOs for this reason.
4. True arbitrage trades the same asset; trading different assets introduces basis risk, which is what killed LTCM's convergence trades.
5. Success comes from timing, aggressiveness and skill; well-timed aggressiveness in a bull market can masquerade as skill.
6. Credit-cycle swings are far more volatile than the economy and sit behind most booms and busts: 'Look around the next time there's a crisis; you'll probably find a lender.'
7. Fear and greed must be balanced; in the late 1990s too little fear let greed dominate.
8. Investors chase a 'silver bullet' (Nifty Fifty, portfolio insurance, market-neutral) and attach themselves to 'geniuses'.

## Market Context

Early October 1998: LTCM had just been restructured with additional investment from 14 financial institutions amid fears of a threat to the world financial system; emerging-market economies and currencies were sliding despite U.S. policy efforts (Rubin and Summers); non-Treasury bond prices had fallen while Treasurys rallied.

## Notable Claims

- LTCM's $4.6bn of equity supported ~$150bn of investments and $1.25tn derivative notional.
- 90% of LTCM's equity was erased between August 1 and late September 1998.
- At 25x leverage a 4% decline wipes out equity.
- Oaktree's 1990 distressed debt funds earned 50% gross returns largely because the credit window closed.
- Buffett joined Goldman Sachs and AIG in a $250m bid when LTCM's net worth was thought to be $600m.

## Quotes

> Brilliance, like pride, often goes before the fall

> leverage should never be used in an attempt to turn low spreads into wide ones, only to take advantage of already-wide spreads

> Look around the next time there's a crisis; you'll probably find a lender.
