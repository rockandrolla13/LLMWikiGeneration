# It's Greek to Me

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** July 19, 2010 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2010-07-19-its-greek-to-me.pdf)

## Summary

Written largely from London during the 2010 euro-zone sovereign debt crisis, the memo explains how Greece and the other 'PIIGS' borrowed at low rates by riding the E.U.'s and Germany's credit standing, ran deficits beyond the 3% cap, and built unaffordable welfare states with weak tax compliance. Marks argues that debt is the facilitator, not the cause; the root problems are low productivity, aging populations and generous benefits. Euro members cannot devalue, the traditional escape, so the remedies are bailouts (EUR 110 billion, then EUR 750 billion), ECB bond buying and austerity, which is anti-stimulative and politically fraught. He then turns the lens on the U.S. ('We have met the enemy and he is us'): structural federal deficits, unfunded state pensions and Medicaid, and the moral hazard of any state bailout. He closes that fear returning to markets makes them healthier, but pro-risk bets need the agility to exit before long-term realities bite.

## Key Ideas

1. E.U. membership let weaker nations borrow at German-like rates without shared fiscal discipline, like adding a profligate member to a joint credit card.
2. Debt is the facilitator, not the cause; structural issues are low productivity, demographics, generous benefits, early retirement and tax evasion.
3. Things that don't make sense can't go on forever, though they can go on longer than they should.
4. Devaluation is the easiest escape from excessive foreign debt, but it is unavailable inside the euro (the drachma fell ~85% versus the deutschemark from 1980 to 2000).
5. Austerity is anti-stimulative in weak economies; Fisher's debt-deflation insight says deleveraging is self-defeating when everyone does it at once.
6. The analysis of sovereign debt is largely political, not economic.
7. The U.S. differs from Greece in degree, not kind: structural deficits, unfunded state pensions, and moral hazard in any state bailout.
8. Markets are safer when fear balances greed; the swing from glass-half-full to half-empty creates better buying conditions.

## Market Context

Mid-2010: the Greek debt crisis broke in April, contagion fears spread to Portugal, Italy, Ireland and Spain (and Hungary, Estonia), the E.U. assembled a EUR 110 billion Greek package and then a EUR 750 billion facility on May 10, the ECB began buying government bonds, the Gulf oil spill and double-dip fears hit markets from late April, and U.S. states faced roughly $200 billion of budget shortfalls as federal stimulus wound down.

## Notable Claims

- Satellite photos found 16,974 Athens pools versus 324 declared.
- The drachma depreciated ~85% against the deutschemark from 1980 to 2000.
- Greece, Spain and Portugal owe EUR 2 trillion to foreigners.
- Total U.S. debt including private sector was 358% of GDP in late 2008.
- Pew: states had set aside $1 trillion less than needed for future pensions and medical benefits.

## Quotes

> But in a global economy, competitive forces make it hard for people – or countries – to live better than their output justifies.

> The analysis of sovereign debt is in large part political, not economic

> Markets are safer when fear balances greed, and when worry about losing money balances worry about missing opportunity.
