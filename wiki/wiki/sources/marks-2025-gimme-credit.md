# Gimme Credit

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** March 6, 2025 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/gimme-credit.pdf)

## Summary

Responding to client questions about spreads and private credit, Marks argues that credit remains attractive even though high yield spreads (about 290bp) are among the narrowest on record. He explains the yield spread as a risk premium and 'fear gauge' that reflects what investors expect about defaults, not what defaults will be. The real test is whether spreads cover expected credit losses: over Oaktree's 1986-2024 record, high yield defaults averaged 3.5% with about two-thirds lost, roughly 230bp a year, and high yield beat 10-year Treasuries by 269bp a year. He adds caveats that make today's spreads more adequate (median default rate lower than mean, stronger policy backstops, an upgraded rating mix, active management), shows that buying at the June 2007 all-time tight still beat Treasuries over 10-15 years thanks to 'the power of the coupon', and stresses the contractual nature of bond returns. On private credit he weighs higher levered yields against illiquidity, no marking to market and higher fees, warns that the tide has never gone out on it, but sees no systemic risk. He concludes credit offers a better deal than the richly valued S&P 500.

## Key Ideas

1. 'Can we talk about private credit?' should be 'can we talk about credit?' -- public credit should not be skipped.
2. A yield spread is a risk premium, like an insurance premium; it reflects investors' expected defaults, not actual defaults, so it is a fear gauge.
3. Adequacy test: does the spread exceed expected credit loss (default rate x loss given default)? Historically ~3.5% x ~2/3 = ~230bp vs a ~290bp spread today.
4. Historical 'normal' spreads (350-550bp, revised to 400-600bp) proved more than adequate, so a below-average spread need not mean high yield is unattractive.
5. The HY universe's quality has risen (BB share up from 32.7% in 1999 to 52.6% in 2024), and policy backstops may have made the macro environment safer.
6. Spread widening is temporary, like stock volatility; if the bond pays, the holder earns the yield signed up for ('the power of the coupon'); interim price drops can even raise reinvestment returns.
7. Private credit: higher, levered yields versus illiquidity, mark-to-model, higher fees and untested manager discipline; the tide has never gone out on it, but it is not systemic.
8. With S&P 500 P/Es implying roughly -2% to 2% ten-year returns, contractual credit yields are the better deal.

## Market Context

March 2025: after the 2022 rate hikes lifted HY yields to about 9½%, strong 2023-24 returns (10.8% annualised) compressed the average HY yield to just above 7% and spreads to about 290bp; 100bp of Fed rate cuts had occurred; heavy inflows into private credit; S&P 500 at elevated P/Es with the 10-year Treasury yield above the S&P earnings yield (WSJ, January 27).

## Notable Claims

- From 1986 to 2024 high yield returned 7.83% a year vs 5.14% for 10-year Treasuries (Barclays data).
- Average HY default rate 3.5% and about two-thirds loss implies ~230bp annual credit loss.
- Today's HY spread is about 290bp, among the narrowest since 1977-78.
- Buying at the June 2007 all-time-tight spread of 241bp returned 7.35% a year over 10 years vs 4.15% for Treasuries.
- The ICE BofA US High Yield index returned 8.2% in 2024 and 13.5% in 2023.

## Quotes

> When people ask me, “can we talk about private credit?” my answer is always the same: “can we talk about credit?”

> you can’t eat spread, or spend spread, or pay pension benefits with spread.

> Thus, surprisingly, interim price declines can raise the overall return earned from holding a bond to maturity.
