# The Value of Predictions II (or "Give That Man a Cigar")

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** July 22, 1996 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/1996-07-22-the-value-of-predictions-ii-or-give-that-man-a-cigar.pdf)

## Summary

Marks follows up his 1993 memo on forecasting by checking a handful of semi-annual Wall Street Journal economist surveys he kept in his desk drawer. He compares consensus forecasts of the 90-day bill rate, the 30-year bond rate and the yen/dollar rate with outcomes. The consensus missed levels by about 15% on average and missed every major change, such as the 1994 and 1996 rate rises and the 1995 rate decline. The forecasts tracked the levels prevailing when they were made, so they were mostly extrapolations. Survey winners who nailed one period were usually far off in the next, which suggests luck from holding outlier views rather than repeatable skill. He concludes that a forecast adds value only if it is both non-consensus and right, which is rare and hard to act on, so Oaktree focuses on knowable micro factors in inefficient niches.

## Key Ideas

1. Consensus macro forecasts in the WSJ surveys missed actual levels by about 15% on average and missed long rates six months out by at least 96 bp.
2. What matters for profit is predicting the likelihood and direction of change, and the consensus failed to foresee every major move in the sample.
3. Consensus forecasts were near-extrapolations of current levels: they sat within about 5% of the prevailing level, and the prevailing level was almost as good a predictor (16% error vs 15%).
4. Individual survey winners were not consistently accurate; persistent outliers (always lowest, always highest) eventually look prescient by chance.
5. Analogy to efficient-market theory: beating the market once is common, doing it consistently on a risk-adjusted basis is the hard claim.
6. To earn above-average returns a forecast must be both different from consensus and accurate, and non-consensus views are hard to believe in, act on and hold through early losses.
7. Oaktree's response is to 'know the knowable': specialized research in inefficient niches where micro factors dominate and bargains can be found.

## Market Context

Written mid-1996, reviewing WSJ surveys from December 1993 to December 1995. The period covers the 1994 interest rate rise, the 1995 rate decline, renewed rate increases in 1996 and large swings in the dollar/yen exchange rate.

## Notable Claims

- Consensus forecasts were off by about 15% on average.
- Consensus missed the 30-year rate by at least 96 bp six months out.
- Forecasts sat close to the then-current level.
- Naive current-level forecast nearly matched the consensus.
- Consensus missed every major move in the sample.

## Quotes

> Like many forecasters, these economists were driving with their eyes firmly fixed on the rearview mirror.

> It doesn't do any good to possess a survey of 64 forecasts that includes a few which are accurate; you have to know which ones they are.

> At Oaktree, we don't spend our time attempting to guess at the future direction of economies, rates and markets, things about which no one seems to know more than anyone else.
