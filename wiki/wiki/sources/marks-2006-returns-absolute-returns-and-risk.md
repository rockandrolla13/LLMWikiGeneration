# Returns, Absolute Returns and Risk

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** June 13, 2006 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2006-06-13-returns-absolute-returns-and-risk.pdf)

## Summary

Marks argues that investment labels mean little: no asset class or technique has a natural rate of return, and terms like 'hedge fund', 'absolute return', 'market neutral' and 'portable alpha' do not guarantee what they promise. Prompted by Waring and Siegel's 'The Myth of the Absolute-Return Investor', he accepts that absolute return is a relative term but finds it useful if it means alpha dominating beta, steadier returns and low (not zero) market correlation. Credit Suisse/Tremont data show hedge fund returns vary with markets and carry embedded beta. He sets out Oaktree's goal of matching the market in good times and outperforming strongly in bad times, stresses that adding value by reducing risk at a given return is as good as raising return, and warns that portable alpha's outperformance may often come from hidden leverage rather than true alpha.

## Key Ideas

1. Labels mislead: nifty-fifty, portfolio insurance, 'market neutral' Granite Fund and dot-com 'change the world' all disappointed; what matters is what you buy, the price and the risk.
2. No asset class has an embedded return; even a 20-year bond's return depends largely on reinvestment rates, and expected equity returns shift with opinion.
3. Absolute return is relative but useful: alpha visibly larger than beta, steadier returns than the market, and low correlation with markets.
4. Hedge fund indices rarely land in a steady 8-12% band, and aggregate hedge fund beta was estimated at 0.37, so returns are not insensitive to markets.
5. Investor wants swing with recent experience (participation after good times, protection after bad), which is why contrarian investing works.
6. 'In good times, it's good enough to be average'; outperformance matters most in falling markets.
7. Risk is not volatility and is invisible in good years; inefficient markets let a manager add value by earning the benchmark return at lower risk.
8. Portable alpha often delivers returns via leverage from unadjusted embedded beta, so it can lose more in down markets; the label is no substitute for skilled execution.

## Market Context

Mid-2006, during strong enthusiasm for hedge funds, absolute-return strategies and portable alpha. Expected equity returns had been marked down after the 2000-02 bear market, and the average equity long/short hedge fund was estimated to have lost 5% in the prior month (May 2006).

## Notable Claims

- Nifty-fifty stocks lost up to 90% in 1973-74.
- In 2005 best and worst hedge fund categories returned 17.4% and -2.6%.
- Expected S&P 500 returns cited by institutions fell from 9-11% in 1999 to 5-7% by 2006.
- Market neutral funds returned 14.3% on average in 1995-2000 and 7.3% in 2001-05.
- Aggregate hedge fund index beta was 0.37 over 1994-2000 (Clermont).

## Quotes

> In good times, it’s good enough to be average.

> Any time Wall Street packages existing elements to produce a surefire solution, my first thought is “alchemy!”

> Without great execution, “portable alpha” is just one more seductive label.
