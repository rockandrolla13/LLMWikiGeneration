# Taking the Temperature

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** July 10, 2023 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/taking-the-temperature.pdf)

## Summary

Marks reviews the five market calls he made in 50 years and uses them to explain how to 'take the temperature of the market'. The calls are bubble.com in January 2000, the defensive turn from late 2004 to mid-2007, aggressive buying after Lehman in late 2008, the Déjà Vu All Over Again optimism of March 2012, and buying in the Covid panic of March 2020. Each worked because markets were at extremes, and each followed from the excesses of the prior episode, which shows causality in cycles. He then lists the components of temperature-taking: pattern recognition, cycles as excesses and corrections, watching for 'things can only get better' or 'only get worse', contrarianism only at extremes, resisting one's own emotion and spotting illogical propositions. He closes by reconciling the calls with Oaktree's tenets of not basing decisions on macro forecasts and not market timing, and calls selling at the bottom the cardinal sin.

## Key Ideas

1. Market calls work only at extremes; Marks made five in 50 years, and calls in the middle ground have poor odds.
2. Cycles are a series of events each of which causes the next, best understood as excesses and corrections.
3. Taking the temperature relies on reading investor psychology, not on macro data or financial statement analysis.
4. Contrarianism pays at extremes but is different from always diverging from the consensus; you must know what the herd does, why, what is wrong with it, and what to do instead.
5. In late 2008 a binary 'will the world end?' framework justified investing $6 billion in one quarter, since inaction only pays if the world ends.
6. Skepticism is not pessimism: it calls for optimism when pessimism is excessive, as in late 2008 and after the 'Death of Equities' article.
7. Oaktree uses neutral macro assumptions with a margin of safety and never bets on a better-than-normal macro outcome.
8. Not market timing means not selling attractive holdings to raise cash and not waiting for things to get cheaper; selling at the bottom is the cardinal sin.

## Market Context

Written in July 2023, after the 2022 rate-hiking cycle that Marks had described in Sea Change as a shift away from the 2009-21 low-rate environment, and following his September 2022 memo The Illusion of Knowledge. The memo is retrospective rather than a current market call; it was prompted by an FT interview for which he selected five past memos.
