# So Much That's False and Nutty

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** July 8, 2009 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2009-07-08-so-much-thats-false-nutty.pdf)

## Summary

Taking its title from Buffett's 2009 Berkshire meeting remark, the memo reviews forty years of investment innovation (growth stocks, MPT, options, LBOs, high yield, securitization, portfolio insurance, LTCM, hedge funds, CDOs, derivatives, VaR) and identifies the practices that went off the rails before the crisis. Marks targets the popularisation of investing, risk tolerance that erased risk premiums, the embrace of illiquidity, comfort with quantitative complexity, misplaced trust in credit ratings, 'leverage inside' products, indiscriminate incentive fees, the decline of fiduciary duty, and an unbalanced choice between the risk of losing money and the risk of missing opportunity. He proposes 'good-enough returns' as an alternative to maximization. He closes with a cautious outlook: markets were up 30% or more from their lows, prices were 'closer to fair', fundamentals remained weak, and he expected better buying opportunities ahead.

## Key Ideas

1. Investors can't make much without taking risk, but taking risk is not sure to make money; when people stop fearing risk, risk compensation disappears.
2. Illiquidity is just another risk; the illiquidity premium exists only if investors prefer liquidity, and pre-crisis endowments crowded it out.
3. Quantitative complexity is sold in bull markets and tested in bear markets; 'not everything that can be counted counts'.
4. Misplaced trust in triple-A ratings was the one indispensable ingredient of the crisis; investors must never abdicate risk assessment.
5. Leverage was increasingly bought 'inside' products (PE funds, over-commitment, derivatives, portable alpha), so investors held more assets than their capital.
6. Incentive fees should go only to managers who add more than the fee without extra risk; '2-and-20' turned into a 'heads we win, tails you lose' norm, and the responsibility for overpaying lies with the payer.
7. Investors face two risks, losing money and missing opportunity, and they swing between extremes rather than balancing them.
8. 'Good-enough returns': decline extra risk once the expected return is satisfactory.

## Market Context

July 2009, roughly two years into the crisis. Debt markets had bottomed in December 2008 and stocks in March 2009, both up 30% or more since. Capital markets had reopened ('green shoots'), but unemployment was rising, real estate was weak, and large federal deficits and state fiscal crises were unresolved.

## Notable Claims

- A credit hedge fund at 2-and-20 needs 16.375% gross to match a high yield manager's 11.5% net, 36% more return.
- Mid-2008, the average $1bn-plus endowment had illiquid investments and commitments equal to half its net worth, some close to 90%.
- More than 90% of CDO debt built from risky mortgages was rated triple-A.
- Portable alpha gave investors exposure to $190 of assets per $100 of capital.
- Markets had recovered 30% or more from their bottoms.

## Quotes

> There are few things as risky as the widespread belief that there’s no risk

> Illiquidity is nothing but another source of risk, and it should be treated no differently:

> I’ve labeled this concept “good-enough returns.”
