# Something of Value

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** January 11, 2021 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/something-of-value.pdf)

## Summary

Prompted by months of living with his son Andrew, a growth and technology investor, during the Covid-19 pandemic, Marks rethinks the value-versus-growth split. Growth had beaten value for 13 years. He defines value investing as buying at a discount to intrinsic value estimated by discounted cash flow, and he argues that low valuation metrics are not the same as being underpriced. He traces how Graham's 'cigar butt' style worked when information was scarce and competition was thin. Today's efficient, data-rich markets have largely removed easy quantitative bargains, so an edge must come from judgment about qualitative factors and the future. Technology businesses can scale fast with high margins and strong moats, while legacy firms are more exposed to disruption, so neither style label fits. On winners, Andrew persuades him that selling great compounders early is a mistake, and Marks admits his mean-reversion bias and his unexamined skepticism about cryptocurrencies. He concludes that value is wherever you find it and should be one tool among many.

## Key Ideas

1. Value investing means buying below intrinsic value; low p/e or price-to-book is not the same as underpriced and can lead to value traps.
2. Value and growth should never have been treated as mutually exclusive; Buffett himself said there is no such thing as value and growth investing.
3. Graham's cigar-butt bargains existed because information was scarce and competition thin; in today's efficient markets, readily computable cheapness should not be profitable outside panics.
4. The edge now lies in superior judgments about qualitative, non-computable factors and how the future unfolds.
5. Digital businesses can scale quickly at low marginal cost with strong moats, so a few winners can justify very high valuations, but bubbles treat every company in a field as a winner.
6. Mean-reversion thinking misleads for 'fade-defying' businesses; the first rule of compounding is not to interrupt it unnecessarily, so selling winners just because they rose is costly.
7. Skepticism protects value investors but can become knee-jerk dismissal; it must be paired with curiosity, as Marks admits about crypto.
8. Growth stocks are more sensitive to interest rates because their value sits in distant cash flows.

## Market Context

January 2021, during the Covid-19 pandemic. Growth and tech stocks had led for 13 years and massively so in 2020. Monetary policy was very easy and rates were expected to stay low. A boom in IPOs and SPACs was under way, and tech leaders dominated the S&P 500 and ETF flows.

## Notable Claims

- Growth beat value for 13 years.
- Coke peaked at 46x earnings in 1972 and fell 65% (Marks's line in the dialogue).
- Coke still compounded 16% a year for 26 years from the 1972 high (Andrew's line).
- Software businesses traded at 30-40x sales.
- Internet users grew from 150 million globally in 1998 to more than that in Indonesia alone today.

## Quotes

> But “carrying low valuation parameters” is far from synonymous with “underpriced.”

> But fear of making a mistake is a terrible reason to sell something of value.

> I guess the answer is “value is where you find it.”
