# Are You An Investor or a Speculator?

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** September 3, 1997 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/are-you-an-investor-or-a-speculator.pdf)

## Summary

Marks argues that what decides an investment's success is the relationship between price and value, not the quality of the asset. He uses Keynes's quip (that a speculator takes risks he knows about and an investor takes risks he doesn't) to argue that knowingly buying unpopular, risky assets at compensating prices is more prudent than buying popular 'riskless' ones. His case is a distressed troubled-company commercial paper that a money market fund had bought at par as 'safe' with a 6% yield, and that Oaktree later bought at 77 cents for a hoped-for 20%+ return. Turning to 1997, he sees investors buying with euphoria rather than skepticism, worried most about not taking enough risk, and accepting 'new era' rationalisations while stock prices outran profit growth. He does not call a top, but says the market is fair to overvalued, not undervalued. He recommends protective strategies and closes with Julian Robertson's metaphor of fair-weather ships.

## Key Ideas

1. No asset is so bad it can't be attractive at some price, and none so good it can't be overpriced; price relative to value decides the outcome.
2. The pendulum swings between euphoria and depression; the easiest high risk-adjusted returns come from buying from depressed sellers and selling to euphoric buyers (Nifty-Fifty 1970 versus distressed debt 1990).
3. By Keynes's definition, 'speculating' on known, well-compensated risks is more prudent than 'investing' in popular assets thought riskless.
4. Low inflation and stable rates justify a certain level of stock prices, not perpetually rising ones; stocks cannot outrun profit growth forever.
5. Investors in 1997 feared missing out more than losing money: 'the risk of not taking enough risk'.
6. 'We're not expecting any surprises' is an oxymoron, since surprises move markets precisely because they are unexpected.
7. When others act with less prudence, we should act with more: Oaktree emphasised defensive convertibles, creditworthy cash-pay high yield and out-of-mainstream distressed assets.

## Market Context

September 1997, late in the 1990s bull market. The Dow had reached about 8,200 in July with record valuation indicators, 'new era' and globalisation narratives were common, IPOs and high yield issuance (including PIK preferreds and catastrophe-linked bonds) were strong, and big stocks had beaten small stocks by record margins. The Dow fell 7% in August.

## Notable Claims

- Oaktree's distressed fund bought a troubled company's commercial paper at 77 cents on the dollar.
- The issuer's stock fell 86% on the day Oaktree began buying.
- Distressed debt funds created in 1990 returned about 50% per annum.
- Nifty-Fifty stocks later fell 70-90%.
- In 1973-74 stocks fell an average of 2% a month for 24 months.

## Quotes

> We think no asset is so bad that there's not a price at which it's attractive for purchase, and no asset is so good that it can't be overpriced.

> Bought when "riskless," this paper proved to be a disaster; purchased off the trash heap, we found it very attractive.

> "We're not expecting any surprises," people say, and that has become our new favorite oxymoron.
