# The Tide Goes Out

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** March 18, 2008 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2008-03-18-the-tide-goes-out.pdf)

## Summary

Written in the week Bear Stearns collapsed, this memo uses Buffett's line about the tide going out to explain the 2007-08 credit crisis. Marks describes the 2002-07 boom as a leverage-driven 'virtuous circle' in the financial sector, and the current bust as the same process in reverse: falling prices, margin calls, withdrawals and fire sales. He argues mark-to-market accounting acted as an accelerant in both directions and that an asset has many possible prices, not one. He catalogs failures of things that 'should' have worked, such as independent mortgage defaults, auction rate notes, absolute return funds and CDS. He restates two principles: leverage magnifies outcomes without adding value and adds risk of ruin, and risk comes from what you buy plus how you finance it. He argues that widely trusted innovations, like portfolio insurance in 1987, help cause the crises that undo them. He judges this crisis broader and more systemic than past sector busts and places the market in the second of three stages of a bear market.

## Key Ideas

1. The 2002-07 boom was distinguished not by the real economy but by a leverage-driven inflation of the financial sector, a virtuous circle that reversed into a vicious one.
2. Mark-to-market accounting accelerated the cycle: it let balance sheets expand in the up leg and forced shrinkage, capital shortfalls and forced selling in the down leg.
3. An asset doesn't have 'a price'; in illiquid, panicked markets reported prices may reflect supply and demand rather than fundamentals.
4. Relying on what 'should' happen is dangerous: improbable things happen and portfolios must survive the low points (the six-foot man drowning in a stream five feet deep on average).
5. Leverage magnifies outcomes but doesn't add value, and it adds an asymmetric risk of ruin through margin calls.
6. Risk comes from what you buy and how you finance it; safe assets bought with enough leverage (Carlyle Capital Corp. at 31x) are not safe.
7. Widespread trust in an innovation creates the risk that undoes it: portfolio insurance helped cause Black Monday, and naked swimming can cause the tide to go out.
8. Three stages of a bull market and its converse for bear markets; major bottoms come when everyone forgets that the tide also comes in.

## Market Context

March 2008, after the credit crunch that began in summer 2007 and in the days of Bear Stearns' collapse. Subprime mortgages had ignited the bust, auction rate markets had failed, banks were raising capital from sovereign wealth funds, and the Fed had announced swapping Treasuries for illiquid AAA mortgage debt.

## Notable Claims

- Carlyle Capital Corp. was levered 31 times on agency AAA paper.
- CDS notional far exceeded the underlying debt.
- Commercial real estate bought at negative leverage at the 2007 top.
- Peloton's rise and fall.
- Buyout fund commitments soared through the boom: $52bn (2002-03), $200bn (2004-05), $557bn (2006-07).

## Quotes

> Investment safety doesn’t come from doing safe things, but from doing things safely.

> In short, an asset doesn’t have “a price.”

> Major bottoms occur when everyone forgets that the tide also comes in. Those are the times we live for.
