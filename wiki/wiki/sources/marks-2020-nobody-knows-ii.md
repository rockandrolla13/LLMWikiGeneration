# Nobody Knows II

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** March 3, 2020 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/nobody-knows-ii.pdf)

## Summary

Written over the weekend after the seven-day correction of February 20-28, 2020 as COVID-19 spread, and reusing the title of his memo two days after Lehman's 2008 bankruptcy. Marks says anything he could say about the virus would be a guess, distinguishes facts, inferences and guesses, and lists the open questions on spread, severity, countermeasures, economic impact and market reaction. He notes the supply-chain and travel effects and the spread of forecasts, argues that what matters is whether price declines are proportional to worsening fundamentals, points out illogical market moves (gold falling, 10-year Treasury at 1.1%), and warns that the Fed has limited ammunition with rates at about 150 bps. His advice: ask whether assets are cheap relative to value, not where the market goes next, and invest part of the cash you want deployed by the bottom.

## Key Ideas

1. Experts merely have a better guess (Annie Duke); separate facts, informed inferences and guesses, and know which you are dealing with.
2. The degree of uncertainty about the virus means no reliable statement about its market implications is possible.
3. Unforeseeable catalysts can appear from nowhere and hit hardest when markets are 'priced for perfection'.
4. Price declines come from bad news plus a decline in psychology; if psychology falls too far, declines can be excessive relative to the news.
5. Some market moves looked illogical: Amazon and Alphabet fell with the market, gold fell, and investors locked in 1.1% ten-year Treasury yields versus a ~6% equity earnings yield.
6. With the fed funds rate near 150 bps, the Fed lacks room for a normal ~500 bp easing cycle; stimulus used in prosperous times leaves less ammunition for crises.
7. Ask not whether stocks will fall further (unanswerable) but whether price is below value; it is 'a time' to buy, not 'the time'.
8. Deploy part of the capital you would want invested by the bottom, keeping cash and nerve to buy more if prices fall.

## Market Context

Early March 2020: COVID-19 was spreading beyond China, the WHO had raised its global risk assessment to very high, and the S&P 500 had fallen 12.8% from its February 19 high in seven trading days, with the VIX surging and the 10-year Treasury yield at a record low near 1.1%. Fed Chair Powell had signalled support and futures priced a 50 bp cut.

## Notable Claims

- The S&P 500 fell 432 points (12.8%) in seven trading days, February 20-28, 2020.
- Lipsitch's 40-70% infection estimate times 2% fatality implies about a million US deaths (presented as an example, not endorsed).
- Fed funds rate stood at about 150 bps versus a normal ~500 bp cutting program.
- S&P 500 P/E was about 19 before the rout, roughly 20% above the postwar average.
- The S&P 500 rallied 4.5% (135 points) on Monday March 2, 2020, its biggest point gain to that date.

## Quotes

— Annie Duke, from Thinking in Bets, quoted by Marks

> But no one can tell you this is _the_ time to buy. Nobody knows.
