# The Race Is On

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** November 26, 2013 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2013-11-26-the-race-is-on.pdf)

## Summary

Marks revisits his February 2007 memo 'The Race to the Bottom' and argues that a new race toward accommodative capital markets is under way. He describes the capital market cycle as among the most volatile: sometimes the credit window is open to anyone, and sometimes it slams shut. When lenders compete to put money to work, they accept higher valuation multiples, lower returns, weaker structures and more risk. Compared with 2006-07, financial innovation, derivatives and mega-buyouts are subdued, but cov-lite loans, PIK-toggles, CCC issuance, dividend recaps, CLOs and subprime auto ABS are back, and IPO and emerging-market fervour is rising. Unlike before the crisis, investors are not risk-oblivious; they feel forced out the risk curve by central-bank rate suppression in a low-return world. Marks says risk tolerance is rising and markets are the riskiest since the crisis, but valuations are not yet at bubble levels (S&P p/e around 16). He is not issuing a sell signal: his motto is 'move forward, but with caution', with caution to rise as others grow less prudent.

## Key Ideas

1. Most investment phenomena are cyclical; the cycles are not predictable in timing or extent but their patterns reliably recur ('history rhymes').
2. The capital market cycle swings between a credit window open to anyone and one that slams shut; this memo covers the swing toward accommodativeness.
3. Competing to deploy capital, lenders and investors 'pay the highest price' through higher multiples, lower yields, weaker deal structures and greater risk.
4. 2013 showed a return of cov-lite loans, PIK-toggle debt, CCC issuance, dividend recaps, CLOs and rising LBO leverage, though financial innovation and mega-buyouts were subdued.
5. Unlike 2006-07, investors were not complacent but felt forced out the risk curve by central bank policies that created a low-return world.
6. The riskiest thing in investing is the belief that there is no risk; risk comes mainly from prices too high relative to fundamentals.
7. One of the most important pendulums is the swing between risk aversion and risk tolerance; we cannot know where we are going, but we should know where we are (the market's temperature).
8. Rising risk tolerance warrants attention but is highly worrisome only with extended valuations; Marks's stance was 'move forward, but with caution'.

## Market Context

November 2013, several years into post-crisis recovery under central-bank rate suppression and quantitative easing, with 'taper' under discussion. Leveraged-loan and high yield markets were hot (cov-lite, PIK-toggle, CLO revival, CCC outperformance), Twitter's IPO had just priced at $26 and closed at $44.90, Snapchat rejected a $3 billion offer, and money was flowing back into emerging markets. Uncertainty remained high about US growth and deficits, Europe, Abenomics in Japan and China's transition.

## Notable Claims

- More than $200bn of cov-lite loans sold in 2013 versus $100bn in 2007; 56% of new leveraged loans (FT).
- Average LBO debt rose from 3.69x earnings in 2009 to 5.37x in 2013, versus a 6.05x boom peak (FT/S&P Capital IQ).
- CCC-or-lower bonds gained 11% in 2013 versus about 6% for all junk and a loss for investment grade (Bloomberg).
- S&P 500 p/e around 16, the postwar average, versus low 30s in 2000.
- At least $55.41bn of CLOs sold in 2013, the most since $88.94bn in 2007 (FT).

## Quotes

> I believe most strongly that the riskiest thing in the investment world is the belief that there’s no risk.

> Risk aversion is the essential element in sane markets.

> move forward, but with caution.
