# Implications of the Election

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** November 7, 2016 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/implications-of-the-election_11716.pdf)

## Summary

Written on the eve of the 2016 US presidential election, the memo sets aside who will win. It focuses on the forces behind Donald Trump's rise, which Marks expects to outlast the vote. He argues that many Trump supporters are older, white, non-college-educated men displaced by demographic and cultural change and by job losses from automation and globalization. Their anger at 'the system' is legitimate, and he links it to Brexit and to Germany's Wutbürger. He calls for bipartisan compromise to break decades of gridlock on entitlements, health care, trade, infrastructure and Supreme Court appointments. He also criticizes flaws in US democracy, including the Electoral College and the scale and concentration of private campaign money. He ends by saying the country needs two strong parties and by hoping for comity.

## Key Ideas

1. Voter anger driven by demographic dislocation, job loss and diminished prospects is a lasting political force whoever wins.
2. Technological change and globalization have outrun political systems' ability to build safety nets and retraining for those left behind.
3. Since the 1980s US politicians have grown more combative, and the 60-vote Senate threshold makes gridlock easy.
4. Gridlock is not safe: existing programs like Social Security must have their rules rewritten as demographics change.
5. Compromise means each side gets some of what it wants, which requires officials to vote for things they promised to fight.
6. The Electoral College and unlimited private campaign money weaken proportional representation and 'one man, one vote'.
7. The country needs two strong parties; free rein for one party is as undesirable as gridlock.

## Market Context

Early November 2016, the day before the US presidential election between Donald Trump and Hillary Clinton, when a Clinton win seemed likely. It followed Brexit in June 2016, rising populism in Europe, slow growth since the 2008 crisis and rising inequality. There is little direct market discussion.

## Notable Claims

- Only 21% of Americans were confident their children would live better, a record low (quoted from the Washington Post).
- About $6.6 billion was being raised and spent in the 2016 cycle (quoted from Business Insider).
- The top 100 families contributed 11.9% of total funds raised, up from 5.6% in 2012.
- The 26 least-populous states, with 17% of the population, could in theory choose the president.
- Trump attracted roughly 50 million voters.

## Quotes

> Regardless of the outcome of the election, they and their sentiments will remain a powerful force.

> No, compromise means, “We get some of what we want and you get some of what you want.”

> The complete opposite of gridlock – free rein – isn’t desirable either.
