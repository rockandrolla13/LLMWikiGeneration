# Notes from New York

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** September 16, 2001 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2001-09-16-notes-from-ny.pdf)

## Summary

A personal, non-investment memo written the weekend after the September 11 attacks, which Marks experienced as a bystander stranded in New York from Tuesday to Friday. He explicitly says it contains no investment recommendations or market predictions. He describes the subdued mood of the city, the value of mobile devices, the difficulty of retaliating against an amorphous enemy, the likely trade-off between civil liberties and domestic security, the spread of rumour in a crisis, the heroism of firefighters and passengers, and the concentrated pockets of loss. He closes that family, faith, principle, friends and the American spirit proved more important than money or technology.

## Key Ideas

1. The attacks breached Americans' sense of being insulated and invulnerable, pulling the US into a reality common elsewhere in the world.
2. Terrorists who care little for their own survival are not predictable in the usual self-interested way, which raises the danger they pose.
3. Responding effectively may force uncomfortable choices: using unsavoury intelligence sources and accepting limits on civil liberties.
4. Emotion plus fragmentary information breeds error: rumours grew from one detained man in a pilot's uniform to seven before being corrected.
5. 'Random violence' does not mean evenly spread: losses were heavily concentrated in particular firms, fire companies and towns.
6. If the attacks turn Americans against Americans and erode core values, the enemy will have won.

## Market Context

Written September 16, 2001, the weekend after the 9/11 attacks on the World Trade Center and the Pentagon. Marks notes people saying that letting stocks fall would mean the enemy won, but he deliberately gives no market view.

## Notable Claims

- Giuliani cut New York's murder rate by two-thirds over eight years.
- More than 300 New York firemen were missing and presumed dead.
- Losses were concentrated: Cantor Fitzgerald and Fred Alger lost large shares of staff.
- Oaktree had no losses among its people.

## Quotes

> Last week's events proved that money, position and technology are not the most powerful or important things in our lives.

> These events clearly prove that "random violence" does not mean "spread evenly."

> Hysteria is natural in crises, but hopefully it will subside – while hopefully vigilance will remain.
