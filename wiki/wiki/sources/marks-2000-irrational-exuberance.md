# Irrational Exuberance

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** May 1, 2000 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2000-05-01-irrational-exuberance.pdf)

## Summary

Written weeks after the Nasdaq peaked on March 10, 2000, this memo is a postmortem on the tech bubble's style of investing rather than a call on prices. Marks shows how the late 1990s turned reputations upside down: Greenspan, Julian Robertson, Soros and Druckenmiller, Gary Brinson, Buffett and Jeremy Siegel were dismissed, while the Dow 36,000 authors and a 27-year-old fund manager were lionised. He blames retail investors becoming the marginal price setter, amateurs ignorant of valuation, sell-side analysts serving investment banking, and simple rules like 'buy the dips'. He explains why Fed rate hikes failed to bite, argues that one-sided buying is really illiquidity, and defines market efficiency as 'speedy, not right'. Every traditional element of prudent investing underperformed in 1999.

## Key Ideas

1. The bubble rewarded optimism and love of a good story over reason, caution and skepticism, and inverted who was regarded as a genius.
2. Being on the wrong side of a trend for a long time is corrosive. Resisters often capitulate at exactly the wrong time (Robertson quit just before one of his best weeks).
3. Irrational markets punished both refusing to play (Robertson) and playing (Druckenmiller).
4. Retail investors and mutual funds became the marginal transactors, and sell-side analysts raised target prices instead of valuing companies.
5. Rate hikes only depress stocks when investors understand and anticipate their effects. In 1999 a 6½% bond yield could not compete with an expected 20% from stocks.
6. Explosive one-way price rises are also illiquidity, but only the downside version gets called illiquidity.
7. Markets are efficient in the sense of 'speedy', not 'right': prices may be wrong, but it is hard for one person to know when and in which direction.
8. Every element of a traditional prudent-investing checklist (value, diversification, bonds, cash, taking profits) underperformed in 1999.

## Market Context

Early weeks of the dot-com bust. The Nasdaq peaked on March 10, 2000 and fell sharply into mid-April (it traded at both 3,649 and 4,138 within seventy minutes on one day). The Fed had raised rates five times since June 30, 1999. Tiger Fund closed, Druckenmiller left Quantum, and Brinson resigned. Shiller's book 'Irrational Exuberance' had just appeared. The P.S. reports the 14 stocks named in 'bubble.com' fell an average 50% from year-end 1999 and 61% from their 2000 highs to April 14, 2000 (table figures).

## Notable Claims

- Robertson's Tiger Fund shrank from $22.8 billion to $5.2 billion in 18 months after an 18-year record of 31.7% a year.
- Nasdaq rose from 2,686 to 5,049 while the Fed raised rates five times.
- Berkshire Hathaway's stock lost 49% from its 1998 high to its 2000 low.
- Slattery's PBHG New Opportunities Fund rose 533% in 1999, then fell 57% between March 10 and April 14, 2000.
- A record 49% of Americans held stocks.

## Quotes

> I am not ringing the bell on stock prices, but hopefully on a style of investing without reason.

> The moral of this story is that irrational markets can kill you.

> In the choice of who should be canonized and who downgraded, the late 1990s were certainly a time when reason was turned upside down.
