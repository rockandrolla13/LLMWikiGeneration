# Hindsight First, Please (or, What Were They Thinking?)

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** October 17, 2005 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2005-10-17-hindsight-first-please-or-what-where-they-thinking.pdf)

## Summary

Marks argues that post-mortems of financial debacles always say 'it was too good to be true' and 'what were they thinking?', and that investors should ask those questions before the losses, using history and skepticism rather than recent experience. He works through a series of 2005 examples: the Bayou hedge fund fraud, where basic due diligence would have exposed red flags; the collapse of the once-unquestioned 9-11% equity return assumption; the flood of money into buyouts and other alternatives chasing an illiquidity premium that is not guaranteed; the market shrugging off a near-doubling of oil prices; Sarbanes-Oxley as a costly backward-looking overreaction; the fraying three-legged retirement stool; the leveraged housing boom built on interest-only and teaser-rate mortgages; and lenders competing by 'selling money' on looser terms. The unifying lesson is to assess what kind of times these are and act on critical, forward-looking analysis rather than the latest trend.

## Key Ideas

1. Investing requires a strong dose of disbelief; most debacles are recognised only in hindsight as 'too good to be true'.
2. Alpha is the ability to see in advance what others will see only afterwards in the rearview mirror.
3. Bayou investors bear responsibility: unknown auditors, an affiliated broker, unverified credentials and paid 'consultants' were all checkable red flags.
4. Return expectations follow prices: with stocks high people expected 9-11%, with stocks lower they expect 6-7%.
5. Illiquidity and risk premia should be demanded but cannot be counted on, especially over 5-10 year periods; strong past returns may mean less is left for the future.
6. Buyouts run in full cycles; late-cycle deals with high prices, high leverage and quick dividend recaps differ from early-cycle deals.
7. When capital suppliers compete on price ('selling money'), credit standards loosen and it is time to be a taker of capital, not a supplier.
8. Regulation tends to fight the last war and miss second-order consequences (Sarbanes-Oxley).

## Market Context

October 2005: aftermath of the Bayou hedge fund fraud, S&P 500 about 20% below its 2000 level, money pouring into buyouts and alternatives, low short-term rates fuelling lending competition and looser terms, a residential real estate boom financed by interest-only loans and ARMs, oil at about $62, personal savings rate turning negative, and post-Enron/WorldCom Sarbanes-Oxley compliance costs.

## Notable Claims

- Oil rose from $33 in January 2004 to $62, yet stocks were still up.
- Sarbanes-Oxley passed 423-3 in the House and 99-0 in the Senate.
- A small public company ($50-60m revenue) faces $1-1½m extra annual cost from being public.
- An estimated 42% of first-time buyers made no down payment in 2004 (quoted chain: Gloom Boom & Doom Report quoting Grant's quoting David Rosenberg).
- Dividend yields declined from 4-7% (1925-55) and 3-4% (1955-95) to 1-2% in the last ten years.

## Quotes

> The alpha that’s so much in demand today is really the ability to see ahead to things others will see only afterwards, in the rearview mirror.

> Investors should demand return premiums, but they shouldn’t count on them.

> We may never know where we’re going, but we’d better have a good idea where we are.
