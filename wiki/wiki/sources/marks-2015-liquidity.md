# Liquidity

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** March 25, 2015 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2015-03-25-liquidity.pdf)

## Summary

Marks defines liquidity as the ability to trade near the last price without moving it, not mere marketability, and argues that it is situational: it depends on direction, size and what everyone else wants to do. Liquidity is plentiful when you do not need it and vanishes when you do, which is why contrarians who sell into strength and buy from forced sellers earn a premium for supplying immediacy. He sets a rule that no vehicle should promise more liquidity than its underlying assets, using hedge fund gates, auction rate securities, mutual fund redemptions, high yield and loan ETFs, and liquid alternatives as cases. He also flags the Volcker Rule as removing bank buyers in crises. He recommends layering portfolio liquidity to fit real needs, notes that too much liquidity tempts investors to trade and lower their standards, and describes Oaktree's approach of buying assets that can be held to maturity.

## Key Ideas

1. The key liquidity question is not 'can you sell it?' but 'can you sell it at or near the last price?'
2. Liquidity is ephemeral and situational: an asset is liquid if you sell when others buy, and illiquid if you sell when everyone sells; size matters too.
3. Things tend to be liquid when you do not need liquidity and illiquid just when you need it most; in crises liquidity can go to zero.
4. No investment vehicle should promise more liquidity than its underlying assets afford; ETFs, mutual funds, auction rate securities and gated hedge funds show why.
5. Bearing illiquidity is neither winning nor losing per se; the liquidity premium is inadequate when investors are risk-tolerant, as with endowments that copied Yale to excess.
6. Too much liquidity can hurt by encouraging short-term trading and casual due diligence.
7. Portfolios should be built in layers from liquid to illiquid, sized to the investor's needs in bad times and to the market cycle.
8. The best defense is buying assets below intrinsic value that can be held to maturity, in structures that permit a long-term approach.

## Market Context

Written in March 2015, with central-bank interest rates near zero pushing retail investors from Treasurys and money funds into credit mutual funds and ETFs. Senior loan funds had flipped from 95 weeks of inflows to outflows in the second half of 2014. ETFs, liquid alternatives and the Dodd-Frank Volcker Rule ban on bank proprietary trading had not yet been tested in a serious downturn.

## Notable Claims

- Senior loan mutual funds had a long run of inflows before reversing.
- Size of the US ETF market in January 2014 per Wikipedia.
- Auction rate securities failed en masse in February 2008.
- AIG's 2008 liquidity crisis and bailout.
- Holding a large cash reserve for crashes would have paid off rarely.

## Quotes

> Thus the key criterion isn’t “can you sell it?” It’s “can you sell it at a price equal or close to the last price?”

> “No investment vehicle should promise greater liquidity than is afforded by its underlying assets.”

> When you can’t meet a margin call because you have insufficient liquidity, that’s profound.

— Jay Wintrob, Oaktree's CEO, given the last word by Marks
