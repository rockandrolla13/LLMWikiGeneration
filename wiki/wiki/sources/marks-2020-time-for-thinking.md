# Time for Thinking

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** August 5, 2020 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/timeforthinking.pdf)

## Summary

Written in August 2020 after a pause in his rapid pandemic memos, Marks frames the policy response as an induced coma: shut the economy, put it on life support with Fed and Treasury payments, treat the disease, then resuscitate. He argues the US skipped the cure by reopening before Covid-19 was controlled, so cases resurged. He insists the downturn is not a cycle but a meteor strike on a moderate expansion, and calls the likely recovery a checkmark rather than a V. Markets had nearly regained their February highs, which he credits to near-zero rates and Fed buying, and he asks with Randall Kroszner whether the Fed and Treasury can keep it up and what MMT implies. He lays out the bull case (low rates justify higher P/Es, tech leaders are exceptional), weighs it against the Nifty Fifty precedent, and finds markets opaque. A postscript shows the reported 32.9% Q2 GDP decline is an annualized figure that overstates the actual fall.

## Key Ideas

1. The policy response followed a medical-coma analogy, but the US reopened before the cure, so the lockdowns went partly to waste.
2. The pandemic downturn is non-cyclical: an exogenous meteor strike, so rate cuts and liquidity alone may not restart growth without virus control.
3. A V needs a fast and symmetric rebound; 'fast down and slow up' is a checkmark, not a V.
4. Rapid market recovery owed much to near-zero rates and Fed buying ('you can't fight the Fed'), raising the question of whether prices depend on that support continuing.
5. Kroszner: central banks can create reserves without limit if there is demand for safe liquid assets, but loss of government credibility could have titanic consequences.
6. The bull case: low rates justify higher P/Es (earnings yield = bond yield + equity premium - growth), and tech leaders have scale, low marginal cost and network effects.
7. Even the best companies' stocks can become overpriced, as the Nifty Fifty showed, so markets remain opaque.
8. Annualized quarter-over-quarter GDP changes such as the reported 32.9% Q2 drop are misleading in abnormal times; actual year-over-year real GDP fell 9.5%.

## Market Context

Early August 2020, five months into the US Covid-19 pandemic. Cases had surged again since late June after early reopening, Q2 GDP had been reported down an annualized 32.9%, the S&P 500 had risen 46% in four months to near its February 19 record of 3,386, the Fed had expanded its balance sheet by $3 trillion, the deficit was heading toward $4 trillion, and the dollar had fallen 9% since March.

## Notable Claims

- Scale of the US economic freeze.
- Combined Fed and Treasury liquidity injection.
- Concentration of 2020 equity gains in FAAMG.
- Dollar weakness after the rescue programs.
- Actual year-over-year real GDP decline.

## Quotes

> Fast down and slow up: to me, that’s no V.

> But in the current case, a moderate recovery – marked by reasonable growth, realistic expectations, an absence of corporate overexpansion and a lack of investor euphoria – was struck down by an unexpected meteor strike.

> Nothing went down by one-third, and nothing is likely to do so.
