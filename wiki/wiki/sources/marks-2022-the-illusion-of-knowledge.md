# The Illusion of Knowledge

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** September 8, 2022 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/illusion-of-knowledge.pdf)

## Summary

Prompted by a 2022 lunch where investors traded opinions on inflation, recession, Ukraine and Taiwan, Marks devotes a full memo to why helpful macro forecasts are so hard to make. He frames forecasting as a process turning inputs into output and argues that both fail. Economies are too complex to model, participants behave emotionally and inconsistently ('imagine how much harder physics would be if electrons had feelings'), relationships such as the Phillips curve are not stationary, and inputs are random, interdependent and often unknowable. Chained single-scenario forecasts compound error. As evidence he cites macro hedge funds underperforming the S&P 500, Paul Krugman's admission he was wrong about inflation, and the Fed's poor forward guidance record. He explains persistent forecasting through self-interest and cognitive dissonance. He concludes that extrapolation forecasts are usually right but unprofitable, while forecasts of deviation are potentially profitable but rarely right, so most forecasts don't add to returns. Investors should join the 'I don't know' school and focus on the knowable micro.

## Key Ideas

1. A useful forecast needs a reliable process converting inputs into output; for economies neither the model nor the inputs are dependable.
2. Economies involve hundreds of millions of participants whose psychology-driven behavior can't be reduced to stable mathematical relationships.
3. Economic relationships aren't stationary (the Phillips curve broke down after 2015), and Cromwell's rule warns against saying anything can't happen.
4. Chained single-scenario forecasts compound error: five predictions each 67% likely give only a 13% chance all are right.
5. One-factor views (e.g. 'the Fed will hike, so risk-off') ignore the other 999 variables and the separate step of predicting market reaction.
6. Evidence of forecasting's low value: macro hedge funds lagged the S&P 500 badly, Krugman misjudged inflation, and FOMC members misforecast their own rate path.
7. People keep forecasting because of self-interest and cognitive dissonance: selective memory, no track records and blaming exogenous shocks.
8. Extrapolation forecasts are usually right but already priced; deviation forecasts would pay but are rarely right, so most forecasts don't add to returns.

## Market Context

September 2022: high inflation after the pandemic stimulus, aggressive Fed rate hikes and an end to forward guidance, the war in Ukraine disrupting energy and food prices, Covid omicron sub-variants, and debate over whether a recession was coming ahead of the 2022 US midterm elections.

## Notable Claims

- Macro hedge funds underperformed the S&P 500 over 10 years (2.8% vs 13.8% annualized, through July 2022).
- Compounded probability of five forecasts.
- Unemployment fell well below 'full employment' without inflation.
- Fed's funds rate projections missed (quoting Gary Shilling).
- Active funds now hold less than half of US equity fund capital.

## Quotes

> The greatest enemy of knowledge is not ignorance, it is the illusion of knowledge.

— Daniel J. Boorstin, quoted by Marks

> Thus, we have (a) extrapolation forecasts, most of which are correct but unprofitable, and (b) potentially profitable forecasts of deviation, which are rarely correct and thus are generally unprofitable.

> Q.E.D.: Most forecasts don’t add to returns.
