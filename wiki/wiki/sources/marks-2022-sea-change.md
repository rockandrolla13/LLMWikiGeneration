# Sea Change

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** December 13, 2022 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/sea-change.pdf)

## Summary

Marks argues that markets may be in the third sea change of his 53-year career. The first was the shift to risk/return thinking that let fiduciaries buy non-investment grade bonds if compensated for risk; this enabled high yield bonds, leveraged buyouts, private equity, distressed debt and structured credit. The second was the four-decade decline in interest rates after Volcker broke inflation around 1980. Marks calls falling rates an airport 'moving walkway' that lifted asset values, cut borrowing costs and especially rewarded leveraged buyers. He describes 2009-2021 as an ultra-low-rate, QE-driven low-return world: an asset owner's and borrower's market dominated by FOMO, in which Oaktree spent years 'in the wilderness'. In 2021-22, inflation, rapid Fed hikes, the Ukraine war and recession fears flipped the environment. Credit windows constricted, hung bridge loans hit banks, and high yield bonds now yielded about 8%. He expects base rates to average 2-4% rather than 0-2%, though Oaktree will not bet on that. The conclusion is that we have moved from a low-return world to a full-return world where lenders and bargain hunters are favoured, and the strategies that won over the past 40 years may not win ahead.

## Key Ideas

1. First sea change (1970s): from avoiding non-investment grade risk to considering risk relative to return, enabling high yield bonds, LBOs, private equity, distressed debt and structured credit.
2. Second sea change: Volcker's defeat of inflation started a four-decade decline in interest rates, which Marks sees as the biggest single contributor to investor success.
3. Declining rates boost growth, subsidise borrowers, raise asset values via lower discount rates, create a wealth effect and are a bonanza for leveraged buyers (the 'moving walkway').
4. 2009-2021 was a low-return world with zero rates, QE and FOMO: an asset owner's and borrower's market that frustrated lenders and bargain hunters.
5. In 2021-22, inflation, rapid Fed hikes and recession fears flipped every condition in Marks's two-column table: credit window constricted, financing scarce, spreads normal, prospective returns ample.
6. Marks expects base rates to average 2-4% rather than 0-2% over the next several years, but stresses Oaktree holds opinions without acting as if they are right.
7. We never know where we're going, but we ought to know where we are; the shift to a 'full-return world' means credit can deliver equity-like returns from contractual cash flows.
8. Strategies that worked best during 40 years of falling rates may not outperform in the years ahead: the third sea change.

## Market Context

December 2022, after inflation reached a 40-year high, the Fed began tapering in November 2021 and started one of its quickest hiking cycles in March 2022. Stocks and bonds fell sharply in the first nine months of 2022, and Russia invaded Ukraine. Recession was widely expected, banks held hung LBO bridge loans, distress candidates grew from dozens to hundreds, and high yield yields had risen to about 8% from 4-5% a year earlier. The S&P 500 had rallied about 10% from its October low.

## Notable Claims

- The US high yield bond universe grew from about $2 billion to roughly $1.2 trillion.
- The S&P 500 compounded at 10.3% per year from 102 in August 1982 to 4,796 at the start of 2022.
- Marks's loan rate peaked at 22.25% in December 1980; four decades later he borrowed at 2.25% fixed for 10 years.
- High yield default rates averaged 3.6% in 1978-2009 but 2.1% in 2010-19.
- Consumer durables prices fell 40% over 1995-2020, which Marks estimates took 0.6% per year off inflation.

## Quotes

> Young people joining the industry today would likely be shocked to learn that, back then, investors didn’t think in risk/return terms. Now that’s all we do. Ergo, a sea change.

> Lastly, there is a forecast I’m confident of: Interest rates aren’t about to decline by another 2,000 basis points from here.

> As I’ve written many times about the economy and markets, we never know where we’re going, but we ought to know where we are.
