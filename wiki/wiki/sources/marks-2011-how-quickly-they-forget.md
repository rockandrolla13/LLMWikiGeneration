# How Quickly They Forget

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** May 25, 2011 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2011-05-25-how-quickly-they-forget.pdf)

## Summary

Marks warns that less than three years after the post-Lehman panic, investors are again forgetting the lessons of 2007-08. Starting from Buffett's line about junk bonds turning from weeds into flowers, he explains why investment memory fails (demographics, suppression of unpleasant memories, greed and self-deceit), recalls the pre-crisis 'silly season' signs, and documents a renewed drift toward risk in 2011: rising LBO leverage, covenant-lite loans, PIK toggles, dividend recaps and new CLOs. He judges prices to be between fair and full rather than bubbly, with investors acting as reluctant 'handcuff volunteers' forced out the risk curve by near-zero rates. He names cheapness as the single key to successful investing and argues that today calls for caution, conservatism and selectivity rather than the 'money and nerve' that paid off in late 2008.

## Key Ideas

1. Asset prices fluctuate far more than fundamentals because investors swing between greed and fear and forget past cycles.
2. Investment memory fails for three reasons: investor demographics, the mind's suppression of painful memories, and greed (a strong tendency to believe what would make one rich).
3. Watching other investors' attitudes toward risk is one of the most important tools; there is nothing riskier than a widespread belief that there is no risk.
4. Near-zero policy rates forced investors out of Treasuries into smaller risky markets; too much money chasing too few deals drives a race to the bottom.
5. The 2008 panic, and thus the best buying opportunity, lasted only about fifteen weeks after Lehman.
6. In 2011 prices sat between fair and full; investors were reluctant risk-takers rather than euphoric.
7. The single most important question about any investment is 'is it cheap?'
8. Responses to a low-return world (stay safe, reach for return, seek alpha managers) each carry their own risk; the right toolkit depends on conditions.

## Market Context

May 2011: markets had recovered sharply since early 2009 and many indices were near pre-crisis peaks; policy rates near zero; high yield spreads back to about 490bp; rising LBO activity and leverage, covenant-lite and PIK toggle deals, new CLOs; macro worries included a so-so US recovery, the US deficit and debt-ceiling impasse, eurozone periphery debt, possible inflation, currency uncertainty and Middle East instability affecting oil.

## Notable Claims

- Ten high yield bonds fell from a 45% average yield in 2002 to 6% in 2003, an 87% yield decline in thirteen months.
- High yield default rate fell from 10.8% in 2009 to 1.1% in 2010, the biggest one-year drop in history.
- Average LBO equity share fell to 30% in 2011 from 38% the year before (Bridgewater, quoted).
- Average high yield spread: 242bp at the June 2007 peak, 1,773bp at December 2008, 492bp at April 2011; 30-year norm 350-550bp.
- From 1975 through 1999 the S&P 500 had only three small annual declines.

## Quotes

> There’s nothing more risky than a widespread belief that there’s no risk

> If I had to identify a single key to consistently successful investing, I’d say it’s “cheapness.”

> when the market is defaulting on its job of being a disciplinarian, discernment becomes our individual responsibility.
