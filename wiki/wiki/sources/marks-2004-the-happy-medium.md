# The Happy Medium

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** July 20, 2004 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2004-07-21-the-happy-medium.pdf)

## Summary

Marks revisits the pendulum image from his April 1991 memo and argues that investor psychology spends far more time at the extremes than at the happy medium. He ranks the cycles that affect investors by amplitude (economic, business, profits, credit, market) and traces them to human frailty rather than institutions. He then walks through paired swings: greed versus fear, risk tolerance versus risk aversion, glass full versus empty, credence versus skepticism about the future, and value versus growth. He borrows Richard Bookstaber's liquidity framework to explain selling panics and adds the idea of buying panics such as 1999. He shows the credit cycle with distressed-debt tallies and S&P 500 return statistics, and closes by urging contrarian behavior backed by patient capital.

## Key Ideas

1. Market psychology swings like a pendulum and rarely rests at its midpoint; the move toward an extreme supplies the energy for the swing back.
2. Cycles grow in amplitude from the economy to business, profits, credit and finally the market, where psychology magnifies everything.
3. The greed/fear cycle reflects changing risk tolerance, which finance theory wrongly treats as constant; at times the risk premium becomes inadequate.
4. Investors' willingness to pay for future potential swings hugely, as shown by telecom assets valued richly in 1999 and at almost nothing in 2001.
5. Following Bookstaber, crises are liquidity-driven; a buying panic like 1999 is as much a crisis as a selling panic like 1987.
6. The credit cycle swings between wide open and slammed shut; rising issuance comes with falling quality, sowing future distressed opportunities.
7. The average is not the norm: S&P 500 annual returns rarely land near 10%, and extreme years cluster.
8. The right response is contrarian (buy when they hate them, sell when they love them), which requires stomach, experience and patient clients.

## Market Context

Written in July 2004, two years after the mid-2002 trough caused by the corporate scandals. Sentiment had recovered quickly, the Dow hovered around 10,000 amid worries about rising interest rates, and the high yield market had seen near-record issuance over 18 months, including many CCC-rated bonds, weak covenants and bonds-for-dividends deals.

## Notable Claims

- Distressed bond universe grew then shrank sharply between 1998 and 2004.
- Starting point of the distressed tally before LTCM.
- Distressed tally fell 85% by March 2004.
- S&P 500 annual returns were rarely near normal and often far from it.
- Range of S&P 500 annual returns over Marks's career to 2003.
