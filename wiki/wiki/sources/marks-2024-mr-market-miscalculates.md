# Mr. Market Miscalculates

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** August 22, 2024 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/mr-market-miscalculates.pdf)

## Summary

Prompted by the sharp market drop and rebound in early August 2024, Marks gathers his past thinking on investor irrationality around Ben Graham's Mr. Market. He recounts the setup: a 54% S&P 500 rally over 21 months, then on July 31 the Bank of Japan's rate hike disrupted the yen carry trade, followed by weak US manufacturing and jobs data, the Sahm Rule trigger and Berkshire's Apple sale. The S&P then fell 6.1% over three days. He explains that perception swings from 'flawless' to 'hopeless' while reality moves between 'pretty good' and 'not so hot'. Mood shifts bias attention and interpretation, bad news is ignored until a tipping point, cognitive dissonance rejects contrary data, correlations go to 1, and contagion spreads across time zones. Wishful thinking ('Goldilocks'), short financial memory and the absence of fixed rules add to the swings. Marks notes that Buffett denied ever making a profit-growth quote often attributed to him. The market is a voting machine in the short run and a barometer of sentiment, not a fundamental analyst. A few emotional traders set prices, and the investor's job is to exploit Mr. Market's overreactions through analysis, not emotion.

## Key Ideas

1. Mr. Market's mood-driven prices diverge from fair value, and his miscalculations create opportunities to sell to him when he is euphoric and buy from him when he is fearful.
2. In reality things fluctuate between 'pretty good' and 'not so hot', but investor perception swings from 'flawless' to 'hopeless'.
3. Mood shifts bias which information investors notice and how they interpret it; an optimistic market ignores bad news until a tipping point, then routs non-linearly, which contradicts rational expectations.
4. Cognitive dissonance leads investors to reject data at odds with prior beliefs; in crises all correlations go to 1 and contagion passes selling across time zones.
5. Wishful thinking (e.g. Goldilocks scenarios) and the extreme brevity of financial memory encourage aggressive behaviour that makes downswings inevitable.
6. Markets have no immutable rules; adages like 'buy on rumor, sell on news' or momentum vs reversal work only sometimes, so superior investing depends on analysis and insight, not formulas.
7. The day-to-day market is a barometer of sentiment (a voting machine), not a fundamental analyst; daily price changes mostly reflect changes in psychology and in who wants to own an asset.
8. Relatively few emotional marginal traders set prices for the whole company; the investor's job is to notice when prices stray from intrinsic value and act on analysis, not emotion.

## Market Context

August 2024. After 2022's bad year for stocks and bonds (Covid, inflation, rapid Fed hikes), a strong rally ran from late 2022 to July 2024 on hopes of Fed rate cuts. On July 31, Powell signalled a cut was closer, but the Bank of Japan raised rates, forcing unwinds of the yen carry trade. Weak US manufacturing and jobs data (Sahm Rule) and Berkshire Hathaway's Apple sale followed, causing a rapid three-day selloff and then a quick rebound.

## Notable Claims

- The S&P 500 rose 54% (ex-dividends) in the 21 months to July 31, 2024.
- The S&P 500 fell 6.1% over August 1, 2 and 5, 2024.
- US unemployment was 4.3% at end-July 2024, up from 3.4% in April 2023.
- The Bank of Japan's July 31 hike was its biggest in over 17 years, to 0.25%.
- Buffett told Marks he never said the oft-quoted line about corporate profits growing 7% on average.

## Quotes

> Its key observation was that in the real world, things fluctuate between ‘pretty good’ and ‘not so hot,’ but in investing, perception often swings from ‘flawless’ to ‘hopeless.’

> The human brain is wired to ignore or reject incoming data that is at odds with prior beliefs, and investors are particularly good at this.

> Emotion? No. Analysis? Yes.
