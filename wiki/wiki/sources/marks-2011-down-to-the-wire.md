# Down to the Wire

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** July 21, 2011 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2011-07-21-down-to-the-wire.pdf)

## Summary

Written two weeks before the August 2, 2011 US debt-ceiling deadline, especially for non-US clients puzzled by the impasse. Marks notes that the ceiling ($14.29 trillion) is a historical accident, but the underlying problem is real: decades of rising credit use by consumers, companies and governments, with almost no attention to repaying principal. Credit ratings describe the ability to refinance, not to repay, so lenders' security rests on the willingness of future lenders to roll the debt. Greece in 2010 showed what happens when that willingness stops. He lays out the US fiscal arithmetic, showing that revenues barely cover entitlements, defence and interest, so borrowing must continue in the short run. Partisanship and no-compromise pledges block a solution. He expects a short-term stopgap that kicks the can down the road. Real repair needs spending limited to revenue except in recessions, growth, and debt stable or falling relative to GDP.

## Key Ideas

1. The debt ceiling is an artificial, unindexed constraint, but the real problem is behaviour: persistent deficits and rising debt-to-GDP.
2. Over Marks's 42-year career, credit use has grown greatly across consumers (credit cards, low-amortisation mortgages, home equity loans), corporations (speculative-grade debt, commercial paper) and governments.
3. In modern credit markets no one repays principal; they roll debt over and add to it, so creditor safety depends on the continued willingness of other lenders to refinance.
4. Credit ratings measure ability to pay interest and refinance, not to repay principal, a deficiency exposed when lenders refused to roll in 2007-08 and Greece was cut off in 2010.
5. What matters most in the short run is not what is true but what is on people's minds; long-standing deficits became an issue only once attention shifted.
6. US revenues barely cover inescapable spending, so in the short run the ceiling must be raised; compromise is blocked by ideology and pledges.
7. Marks expects a stopgap that kicks the can down the road and doubts the dollar can remain the reserve currency without fiscal responsibility.
8. Sound policy limits spending to revenues except for counter-cyclical deficits that are later reversed, and keeps debt stable or falling relative to GDP.

## Market Context

July 2011 US debt-ceiling standoff, with an August 2 deadline after the ceiling was reached in June. A Republican House faced a Democratic Senate and White House. The European sovereign debt crisis was under way (Greece from April 2010, then other peripheral countries), Moody's had put the US on downgrade watch and S&P had reportedly warned of a downgrade if payments were missed. The US economy was described as anemic.

## Notable Claims

- The US debt ceiling limited net debt to $14.29 trillion.
- The US was borrowing about 36 cents of every dollar spent in 2011 (FactCheck).
- Social Security and Medicare were 33.5% of 2010 outlays and defence 20.1%; interest 5.7%.
- Revenues equal 64% of spending, just above the 59.3% that is inescapable.
- The ratio of workers to Social Security recipients fell from 17-to-1 in 1950 to 3-to-1 (quoting Cembalest).

## Quotes

> No one pays off their debt. They merely roll it over . . . and add to it.

> The problem isn’t the ceiling, it’s our behavior
