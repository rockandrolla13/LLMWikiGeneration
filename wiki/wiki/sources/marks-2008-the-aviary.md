# The Aviary

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** May 16, 2008 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2008-05-16-the-aviary.pdf)

## Summary

A multi-topic memo built around bird metaphors, written two months after the Bear Stearns rescue. "A Dead Duck" shows the credit cycle turning fast: Abbey was the first UK lender to lend five times salary in late 2006 and the last to drop 100% mortgages in April 2008. "The Canard of Free Market Infallibility" traces the long cycle of regulation and deregulation since 1929 and argues that free markets allocate well in the long run but must produce ruin as well as gains. "A New Canard Takes Flight" gives twelve reasons why heavier regulation will not prevent future crises. "The Black Swan" discusses Taleb's book and asks how much an investor should pay to guard against improbable disasters. "Birds on a Wire" traces the chain from subprime to credit crunch to bank troubles to the real economy. "Phoenix from the Ashes?" judges that March 17 was a bottom but perhaps not the bottom, and says the time to turn highly aggressive had not yet come.

## Key Ideas

1. The credit cycle swings from lending to anyone for any purpose at the top to shutting out even deserving borrowers at the bottom.
2. Faith in free-market efficiency became an irrational belief in a perpetually rising tide; markets allocate well long-run but must create losers and capital destruction too.
3. Regulation and deregulation move in decades-long cycles like hemlines, and whichever philosophy dominates will share the blame for crises.
4. Tighter regulation has limits: rules are easier to evade than to write, derivative risk is hard to quantify, banks were the most regulated yet lost the most, and good judgment cannot be mandated.
5. Black swans: improbable is not impossible, and models built on bell curves led people to treat a 1% chance of loss as riskless.
6. Survival has to be achieved in the short run, but protecting against the worst case costs return, so how much to spend on the improbable disaster is a trade-off each investor must choose.
7. Effects chain together (subprime, credit crunch, bank runs, economy), and a weak economy can feed back into further write-downs.
8. Only when markets hold a great deal of caution, ideally too much, is it time to turn highly aggressive.

## Market Context

May 2008, two months after JPMorgan Chase rescued Bear Stearns on March 17. Markets had rallied on bank CEOs' claims that the worst was over. A recession was widely expected, unemployment was rising, oil and food prices were up, bank write-downs continued, and calls for more regulation were growing. Stocks were only slightly below their 2007 highs.

## Notable Claims

- Abbey raised standard mortgage lending to five times salary in 2006.
- More than 20 UK lenders withdrew 100% mortgages within about a month.
- The IMF projected $1 trillion of mortgage-related losses.
- Hung bridge loans were selling at 90, often with seller financing.
- Democrats controlled the White House for 28 of 36 years from 1933 to 1969.

## Quotes

> At the highs of the credit cycle, anyone can get money for any purpose. At the lows, even deserving borrowers are shut out.

> Once in a while, however, people need a reminder that “unlikely” isn’t synonymous with “impossible.”

> Certainly that day was “a bottom,” but I’m not so sure it was “the bottom.”
