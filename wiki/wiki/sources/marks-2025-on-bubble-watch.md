# On Bubble Watch

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** January 2, 2025 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/on-bubble-watch.pdf)

## Summary

Written 25 years to the day after bubble.com, this memo asks whether the S&P 500 and its Magnificent Seven leaders are in a bubble. Marks defines a bubble as a state of mind rather than a calculation: irrational exuberance, adoration, FOMO and above all the belief that there is 'no price too high'. He revisits the three stages of a bull market, explains that newness removes the historical tether on valuations, and recounts his formative experience with the Nifty Fifty, which lost over 90% for buyers who held from 1969 for five years. He stresses that high valuations assume persistence, which is rare, showing how few of the 2000 top-twenty names remained in 2024. He lists cautionary signs (concentration, valuation, AI enthusiasm, passive buying, Bitcoin) and J.P. Morgan data linking a forward P/E of 22 to ten-year returns of plus or minus 2%. He balances these with counterarguments and declines to call it a bubble.

## Key Ideas

1. A bubble is more a state of mind than a quantitative calculation; 'no price too high' is the surest psychological sign.
2. The three stages of a bull market are about investor psychology: a few see improvement, most accept it, then everyone believes things can only get better forever.
3. Bubbles attach to new things, because with no history there is nothing to tether valuations; there is usually a grain of truth that gets taken too far.
4. It is not what you buy but what you pay; no asset is so good that it cannot become overpriced and dangerous.
5. High multiples assume persistence, which is rare: only six of the top twenty S&P 500 names of 2000 were still top twenty in 2024.
6. Bubbles can spread from a group of stocks to whole markets; the 1990s run led to a zero cumulative S&P 500 return from mid-2000 to December 2011.
7. Starting valuations strongly predict ten-year returns; at a forward P/E near 22, past ten-year returns ranged between plus and minus 2%.
8. Marks lists cautionary signs and counterarguments and declines to say authoritatively whether a bubble exists.

## Market Context

January 2025, after two consecutive years of 25%+ S&P 500 gains driven by the Magnificent Seven and enthusiasm about AI. The top seven stocks made up 32-33% of the index, US stocks were over 70% of MSCI World, the S&P forward P/E was about 22, leading banks were projecting low- to mid-single-digit ten-year returns, and Bitcoin had risen 465% in two years.

## Notable Claims

- Concentration of the top seven S&P 500 stocks.
- US weight in MSCI World.
- Valuation and ten-year return relationship per J.P. Morgan Asset Management.
- Back-to-back 20%+ S&P 500 years.
- Lost decade after the TMT bubble.

## Quotes

> But for me, a bubble or crash is more a state of mind than a quantitative calculation.

> There’s no asset so good that it can’t become overpriced and thus dangerous, and there are few assets so bad that they can’t get cheap enough to be a bargain.

> There’s usually a grain of truth that underlies every mania and bubble. It just gets taken too far.
