# Quo Vadis?

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** July 26, 2002 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2002-07-26-quo-vadis.pdf)

## Summary

Written 28 months into the post-2000 bear market, this memo asks where stocks go next and answers that no one knows, especially in the short run. Marks sets out caveats: the 1990s boom and bust is the most extreme of his career, markets are driven by emotional reactions beyond prediction, and historical patterns rest on few observations. He then weighs positives (the market's failure to follow its usual post-recession rally, a weaker dollar, earnings beating estimates, capitulation-style selling) against negatives, chiefly that a large fall from a very high starting P/E does not make stocks cheap. He also discusses lost wealth effects, pension and goodwill problems, post-Enron cynicism and the risk of over-regulation. He comes down on the cautious side and argues that alpha and risk control, not market beta, will drive results.

## Key Ideas

1. There is no conclusive sign of the market's next move, and short-term moves are even less predictable than long-term trends.
2. A big decline does not create bargains if it follows a huge advance: the 2000-2002 fall started and ended above 20 times earnings.
3. Psychological factors cut both ways: fear discourages buyers but can also produce the panic selling that forms a bottom.
4. Maximum panic usually coincides with minimum prices, so capitulation signs may hint at a bottom.
5. The case that low interest rates justify valuations amounts to accepting low stock returns because bond returns are low.
6. Regulators struggle to foresee second-order consequences, and rules set in market areas are hard to reverse.
7. Without a market tailwind, skill-based alpha and risk control matter more than risk bearing.

## Market Context

July 2002, 28 months after the March 2000 peak, during the tech-media-telecom bust. Corporate scandals (Enron, Arthur Andersen) had eroded trust, the economy was emerging from recession, the dollar was weakening, and new rules required executives to certify financial statements from mid-August.

## Notable Claims

- Market underperformed its typical post-recession move by 40 points (quoting NYT).
- 1973-74 vs 2000-02 decline compared by starting P/E.
- About $7 trillion of equity value erased since March 2000.
- On July 19, 2002 (Dow down 390), NYSE new lows outnumbered new highs about 14 to 1.
- The dollar fell 10%.

## Quotes

> There's no such thing as a conclusive sign, and there never will be.

> Maximum panic usually coincides with minimum prices.

> In short, the fact that stocks are down since the end of the recession, and down a great deal from their peak, doesn't mean they're cheap.
