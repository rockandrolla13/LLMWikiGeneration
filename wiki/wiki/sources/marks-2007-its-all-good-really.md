# It’s All Good . . . Really?

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** July 30, 2007 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2007-07-30-its-all-good-really.pdf)

## Summary

Written two weeks after 'It's All Good', as credit markets turned in July 2007, this memo traces how a small seed, bad subprime mortgage lending, spread across markets. Marks sorts contagion channels into fundamental (defaults, falling home prices, wealth effects), psychological (ebbing confidence and higher demanded risk premiums) and technical (deleveraging, margin calls and forced selling that drag unrelated assets down). He argues the real driver of the prior boom was not 'excess liquidity' but leverage, made limitless by derivatives, hedge funds and non-bank lenders, so the episode was a 'leverage bubble'. CDOs, which sold high-rated debt at low-grade yields thanks to over-generous ratings, were the bottomless buyer of RMBS and leveraged loans; their seizing-up left bridge loans hung and removed the 'LBO put'. He stresses that psychology can overwhelm the real economy and that no one knows how far the swing will go, but says the reversal is 'more like it' for Oaktree's bargain hunters.

## Key Ideas

1. Cyclical downturns often start from a small seed; here it was subprime loans made by originators paid for volume, not quality.
2. Attitudes to contagion are cyclical: optimists dismiss 'isolated incidents', pessimists generalise every negative into an epidemic.
3. Contagion runs through three channels: fundamental, psychological and technical, which feed back on each other.
4. Forced sellers sell what they can, so unrelated assets fall together and correlations go to one in a crisis.
5. The boom was a 'leverage bubble', not an 'asset bubble'; derivatives, hedge funds and non-bank lending removed old leverage limits.
6. CDOs offered high-rated debt at low-grade yields because rating agencies misjudged novel structures; their halt hit CMBS, leveraged loans and LBO financing.
7. Psychology can overwhelm the real economy because it all comes down to expectations; politicians have an incentive to downplay negatives.
8. When markets are priced for perfection and investors demand too little risk compensation, the pendulum eventually swings back.

## Market Context

Late July 2007, the start of the subprime crisis. Subprime delinquencies and foreclosures were rising, Bear Stearns told investors its funds were worthless, bridge loans for buyouts such as Chrysler and Boots were hung, the Dow had just had its worst week in years, and CDO formation was seizing up. The broad economy and high yield default rates were still strong.

## Notable Claims

- Leveraged loan volume in H1 2007 was $545bn, up 60% on H1 2006.
- High yield default rates were still at 25-year lows.
- It was a leverage bubble rather than an asset bubble.
- The Dow fell 312 points on July 26 and 580 points the previous week.
- Forced selling spreads losses to unrelated assets.

## Quotes

> Forced sellers sell what they can sell, not necessarily what they want to sell.

> Fundamentals influence psychology, which determines technicals, which feed back to further affect fundamentals.

> When markets are priced for perfection, they will disappoint.
