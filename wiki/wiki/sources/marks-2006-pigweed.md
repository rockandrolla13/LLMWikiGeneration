# Pigweed

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** December 7, 2006 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2006-12-07-pigweed.pdf)

## Summary

A post-mortem of Amaranth Advisors, the hedge fund that lost about 65% of its $9.5 billion capital on natural gas trading in September 2006 ('pigweed' is another name for the amaranth plant). Amaranth's calendar-spread trades were hedged arbitrage in form, but leverage, concentration and illiquidity turned low-risk positions into a firm-killing bet. Marks draws lessons: diversification financed with borrowed money can raise risk; commodities and currencies have no income stream to value, so superior returns there are hard to attribute to skill; very high short-term returns, like Brian Hunter's $1 billion in 2005, should prompt 'How'd you do that?' rather than awe. Using Taleb's alternative histories and a USC football example, he argues that outcomes do not prove decision quality. He also criticises over-reliance on quantitative risk models and lists classic mistakes made by Amaranth.

## Key Ideas

1. Arbitrage (offsetting positions in related assets) is low risk only until leverage is added; LTCM's 'scooping up nickels and dimes' was done in front of a steamroller.
2. Diversifying into uncorrelated assets with borrowed money can increase risk: multi-strategy plus leverage equals more ways to lose.
3. Assets without income streams (currencies, commodities, gold) lack an intrinsic value anchor, making consistent alpha there doubtful.
4. Short-term gains and losses are both 'impostors'; surprisingly good returns are often the flip side of surprisingly bad ones.
5. Alternative histories: you can't judge the correctness of a decision from the outcome.
6. Quantitative risk models should be 'on tap but not on top'; 9-sigma events occur far more often than normal distributions imply when derivatives meet leverage.
7. Classic mistakes: borrowing short to buy long and illiquid, confusing paper profits with real gains, misjudging liquidity, ignoring the impact of others, underestimating correlation.
8. Intelligent diversification means owning things that respond differently to the same environmental factors.

## Market Context

Late 2006: hedge funds were proliferating and using more leverage to reach return targets as inflows depressed prospective returns; Amaranth had collapsed in September on natural gas trades; oil had dropped from $77 in July to $55; there was intense competition for 'hot' traders. Marks notes he had spent six weeks in London.

## Notable Claims

- Amaranth announced a 40% loss of its $9.5bn capital, later revised to 65%.
- Brian Hunter was paid $75-100 million for 2005.
- Amaranth held 6,700 energy positions leveraged 4.5 to one.
- Hillary Till called Amaranth's loss a 9-standard-deviation event; LTCM's was about 8-sigma.
- Oil fell from $77 to $55 in four months while the bullish factors stayed true.

## Quotes

> But it's essential to remember that leverage is the ultimate two-edged sword: it doesn't alter the probability of being right or wrong; it just magnifies the consequences of both.

> Diversification is a good thing, but a lot depends on how you finance it.

> Experience is what you got when you didn't get what you wanted.
