# Assessing Performance Records – A Case Study

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** February 15, 2012 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2012-02-15-assessing-performance-records-a-case-study.pdf)

## Summary

Marks uses his decade as chairman of the University of Pennsylvania's Investment Board (June 2000 to June 2010) as a case study in judging track records. Penn's value-only endowment under John Neff lagged peers badly in fiscal 1995-2000. On taking over, Marks refused to chase tech and growth at peak prices and kept the portfolio defensive, diversifying slowly with a focus on risk control and keeping cash and Treasurys for operating needs. Over FY2001-10 Penn returned about 5½% against 6-7% for peers. In FY2009 it lost 15½% against 25-28% for many peers, and it kept spending and hiring while others cut back. Marks then asks two counterfactuals: what if his tenure had started two years earlier, and what if the crisis had not happened. He concludes that the start and end dates and chance events shape how a record looks, so a record must be examined for whether it was right, wrong, or right for the wrong reason.

## Key Ideas

1. Accurate assessment needs a long record covering good and bad years and a relevant benchmark or peer group.
2. How far a manager can ignore relative performance depends on the constituents; the manager's job is client satisfaction, so expectations matter.
3. The classic dilemma: a portfolio lacks an asset class that is right in principle but priced sky-high, so you must choose between tracking peers and refusing to overpay.
4. Risk is not just volatility; it is what happens to owners when losses arrive, made concrete in the cuts that peers had to make in FY2009.
5. Holding liquidity let Penn avoid forced selling and keep investing operationally during the crisis.
6. Start and end dates of a measurement window can flip a record from average to excellent, so consider alternative histories.
7. Excluding FY2009, Penn's cautious approach trailed peers by more, which raises the question of whether the caution was skill or luck.

## Market Context

Written in February 2012 looking back over FY2001-2010. That decade covered the post-1999 tech bust, the mid-2000s risk-taking boom (Marks grew more worried from late 2004 and made the portfolio more defensive in 2006-08) and the 2008-09 global financial crisis. Marks says the data is a little out of date but the lessons are not.

## Notable Claims

- Penn gained 16% a year in FY1995-99 while peers gained about 23%.
- Penn returned about 5½% for FY2001-10 versus 6-7% for peers.
- In FY2009 peers lost 25-28% while Penn lost 15½%.
- Excluding FY2009, Penn returned 8% versus peers' 10½-11½%.
- Penn ranked 70th nationally in endowment per student.

## Quotes

> But ultimately, the manager’s job isn’t to make money, it’s to deliver client satisfaction, so expectations have to matter.

> No, risk isn’t just volatility. It’s what happens to owners of capital when downward fluctuations occur and principal losses are experienced.

> No strategy works all the time, but defensiveness was right for Penn as things turned out. Lucky or good? It’s always hard to tell.
