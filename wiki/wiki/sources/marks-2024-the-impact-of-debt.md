# The Impact of Debt

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** May 8, 2024 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/the-impact-of-debt.pdf)

## Summary

A short memo prompted by Morgan Housel's essay 'How I Think About Debt', which notes that Japan's centuries-old 'shinise' businesses typically hold lots of cash and no debt. Marks agrees that debt is not bad in itself, but argues that the right amount depends on the size of the enterprise and the volatility of its earnings and assets. Drawing heavily on his December 2008 memo 'Volatility + Leverage = Dynamite', he explains that leverage magnifies gains and losses symmetrically but adds an asymmetric risk of ruin, especially when lenders pull credit, investors withdraw or covenants force selling. Tail events are underestimated after long calm periods, so leverage tends to rise just before risk appears. Attitudes to leverage are cyclical, and panics reveal capital already destroyed in good times. He concludes that leverage should be optimised, not maximised, with a margin of safety.

## Key Ideas

1. Debt is what creates the possibility of default, foreclosure and bankruptcy. Very long-lived firms ('shinise') tend to hold cash and no debt.
2. As debt increases, the range of outcomes you can endure narrows (Housel).
3. Prudent leverage is a function of the riskiness and volatility of the assets it buys: stable assets can carry more, risky assets less.
4. Leverage amplifies gains and losses symmetrically but adds a one-sided risk of ruin when lenders, investors or covenants force selling at low points.
5. Long stretches without extreme events make risk assumptions look too conservative, tempting investors to add leverage just before the tail event arrives.
6. Attitudes toward leverage are cyclical: in good times investors want more, lenders supply more and rules loosen, and the process reverses violently in bad times.
7. 'Panics do not destroy capital; they merely reveal the extent to which it has previously been destroyed' (John Mills, 1865, via Chancellor).
8. Optimise, don't maximise, leverage: when doing anything novel, risky or volatile, err on the side of caution and keep a margin of safety.

## Market Context

May 2024. The memo is largely timeless and gives little market context. It follows Marks's short memo 'The Indispensability of Risk' and his January 2024 memo 'Easy Money', and reflects on the GFC via the December 2008 memo it quotes.

## Notable Claims

- Japan has 140 businesses operating more than 500 years after founding (Housel).
- Leverage should be scaled to asset volatility.
- Financial institutions in the GFC underestimated mortgage risk and used too much leverage.
- Extreme volatility surfaces infrequently, which breeds complacency.
- Riskier assets warrant less leverage.

## Quotes

— Morgan Housel, quoted by Marks

> “as a rule, panics do not destroy capital; they merely reveal the extent to which it has previously been destroyed by [the taking on of excessive leverage in good times].”

— John Mills, a Manchester banker, in 1865, quoted by Marks

> “There are old investors, and there are bold investors, but there aren’t many old bold investors.”

— an old investing maxim, quoted by Marks
