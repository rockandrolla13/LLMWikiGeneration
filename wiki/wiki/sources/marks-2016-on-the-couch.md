# On the Couch

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** January 14, 2016 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/on-the-couch.pdf)

## Summary

Started on December 12, 2015, after a sharp one-day fall in stocks, credit and oil, this memo puts the markets 'on the psychiatrist's couch'. Marks recalls the worries of On Uncertain Ground and notes that despite them the S&P 500 returned 74% in 2012-14 and high yield spreads narrowed, so psychology and prices ran ahead of fundamentals. In 2015 negatives built up until an August tipping point, China's A-share collapse and renminbi devaluation, flipped investors from complacency to fear. He explains selective perception and skewed interpretation, the pendulum swinging from 'flawless' to 'hopeless', and the failure to see second-order effects. Three case studies show over-negative readings: falling oil, a long-expected Fed hike, and the Third Avenue Focused Credit Fund liquidation. Distressed debt began to gap down as investors judged bonds on recovery rather than yield. His prescription is understanding, emotional control, control over circumstances and contrarianism. Oaktree now moves 'forward with a little less caution', while he rejects a 2008-style rerun.

## Key Ideas

1. Investors ignore accumulating negatives for a while, then capitulate and overreact at a tipping point: first denial, then capitulation.
2. The pendulum of investor psychology rarely rests at the midpoint; perception swings from 'flawless' to 'hopeless' though reality moves between 'pretty good' and 'not so hot'.
3. Two failings: selective perception and skewed interpretation; a confluence of several negatives makes investors lose their cool.
4. The day-to-day market is a barometer of sentiment, not a fundamental analyst (Ben Graham).
5. A widely anticipated event, like the December 2015 Fed hike, should already be in prices; what matters is how far and how fast rates rise.
6. Illiquid assets plus potential capital flight is a sure recipe for disaster (Third Avenue Focused Credit Fund); no vehicle should promise more liquidity than its assets.
7. If you could know one thing about an investment, it might be how much optimism is embodied in the price (three stages of a bull market).
8. Markets linked by shared psychology (psychological contagion) decline in unison beyond what fundamentals justify.

## Market Context

Late 2015 to mid-January 2016: oil at a seven-year low (Brent $37.49), China's slowdown, A-share collapse and renminbi devaluation, the Fed's first rate increase in December 2015, the Third Avenue Focused Credit Fund liquidation, gapping-down distressed debt and tightening capital markets, plus terrorism in Paris and San Bernardino and a U.S. election campaign.

## Notable Claims

- The S&P 500 returned a cumulative 74% in 2012-14 despite many macro worries.
- High yield spreads narrowed from 706 bp to 522 bp between end-2011 and end-2014.
- The S&P 500 fell 11% between August 17 and 25, 2015.
- Oaktree's distressed funds shrank from $10.9bn (2007-08) to $5.5bn (2010) and $2.7bn (2011).
- China accounts for only about 1% of S&P 500 profits.

## Quotes

> But in the world of investing, perception often swings from "flawless" to "hopeless."

> Illiquid assets and the possibility of capital flight: there are few surer recipes for investment disaster.

> If I could know only one thing about an investment I'm contemplating, it might be how much optimism is embodied in the price.
