# Now It’s All Bad?

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** September 10, 2007 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2007-09-10-now-its-all-bad.pdf)

## Summary

Written seven weeks after 'It's All Good', this memo tracks the swing of market psychology from 'it's all good' to 'it's all bad' in summer 2007. Marks traces a virtuous circle from November 2002 -- low safe yields, a search for return, easy leverage, securitisation, CLOs and CDOs, buyout bridges -- that set up high leverage, untested vehicles and thin risk premiums. A bump in subprime mortgages, mass rating downgrades of mortgage securities and CDO debt, and the Bear Stearns hedge fund losses set off a vicious circle: frozen commercial paper, suspended redemptions, forced selling, hung buyout bridges and contagion into many unrelated markets. He argues leverage and liquidity were the main actors, that the 'risk reduction' story was a fairy tale because risk is only transferred, and lists lessons about market efficiency, quant models, diversification, manager conflicts and ratings. He ends with contrarian caution and a reference to Minsky's instability thesis.

## Key Ideas

1. The pendulum spends little time at its midpoint; psychology swung from 'priced for perfection' to wholesale pessimism in weeks.
2. The 2002-07 virtuous circle: low expected returns on safe assets led to an uninhibited search for return, cheap and abundant leverage, and untested structures (securitisation, tranching, CLOs, CDOs).
3. Contagion runs along hidden fault lines in portfolios that only show up in rare crises, making correlations hard to predict.
4. Slightly riskier positions look safe, but leveraged up enough they become extremely risky; this drives most fund collapses (LTCM, Amaranth, Bear Stearns funds, Sowood).
5. Liquidity assumptions were interrelated: one psychological shock can reverse financing, market depth and fund liquidity at once; 'the markets may hurt you, but your lenders will finish you off.'
6. Risk cannot be eliminated, only transferred; things that make the world look less risky tend to make it riskier.
7. Lessons not learned: over-trust in market efficiency, quant models, diversification into unfamiliar areas, misaligned manager incentives and issuer-paid ratings.
8. Protecting capital in bad times is incompatible with maximising returns in good times; investors must choose.

## Market Context

Summer 2007, the start of the global financial crisis: rising subprime delinquencies, rating agency downgrades of mortgage-backed securities and CDO debt, collapse of two Bear Stearns hedge funds, frozen commercial paper markets, money market funds suspending withdrawals, hung LBO bridge loans, quant fund losses in early August, unwinding of yen carry trades, and a symbolic Fed discount rate cut on August 17.

## Notable Claims

- Banks had committed $277 billion of buyout financing (excluding equity bridges and non-US deals).
- Oaktree's active distressed debt funds gained 20% in November 2002.
- Credit derivatives outstanding were estimated at seven times outstanding bonds (quoting Jeff Pantages).
- Lehman's Matthew Rothman said 1-in-10,000-year model events happened three days in a row.
- Real estate attracted capital even when cap rates fell below 5%.

## Quotes

> “The markets may hurt you, but your lenders will finish you off.”

> Risk cannot be eliminated; it just gets transferred and spread.

> When others are aggressive, we should be worried, but when others are worried, we can be confident.
