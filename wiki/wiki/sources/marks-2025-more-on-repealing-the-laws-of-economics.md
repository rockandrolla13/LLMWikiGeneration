# More on Repealing the Laws of Economics

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** June 18, 2025 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/more-on-repealing-the-laws-of-economics.pdf)

## Summary

A sequel to 'Shall We Repeal the Laws of Economics?' (September 2024), this memo argues that government attempts to override market laws such as supply and demand and incentives produce winners, losers and worse overall outcomes. Marks revisits rent control, then uses the 2025 Southern California wildfires to show how California's limits on fire insurance pricing (backward-looking 20-year loss averages, no reinsurance pass-through, slow approvals) drove insurers out and left fewer than a quarter of affected properties insured -- a background section he had the AI assistant Perplexity write. His main topic is the April 2025 'Liberation Day' tariffs: he treats a tariff as a tax, explains trade disequilibria with a two-country car example, traces the US shift from manufacturing to services with Niall Ferguson, and accepts targeted tariffs for national security, iconic industries or unfair practices while rejecting blanket tariffs. He closes on chronic US fiscal deficits and the 2035 Social Security trust fund exhaustion as failures to face economic reality.

## Key Ideas

1. Economies follow underlying laws (supply and demand, incentives); government overrides choose winners and losers and reduce overall welfare.
2. Price caps do not create supply: governments can limit rents or premiums but cannot make developers build or insurers write policies.
3. California's backward-looking insurance rate rules made fire policies uneconomic as wildfire risk rose, causing insurer exits and underinsurance before the 2025 fires.
4. A tariff is a tax ultimately borne largely by consumers unless exporters or importers absorb it to protect market share.
5. Tariffs act like a wall that blocks the trade forces that close wage and price disequilibria, protecting domestic producers at consumers' expense and possibly hurting exports.
6. The decline of US manufacturing employment reflects a normal development path toward services (Ferguson), not mainly unfair trade.
7. Targeted tariffs can be justified (national security, iconic industries, unfair trade); blanket tariffs on all goods from all countries have unavoidable costs.
8. Persistent deficits in prosperity and the unaddressed Social Security shortfall show politicians ignoring economic constraints; generational equity is at stake.

## Market Context

June 2025: two months after the April 2 'Liberation Day' tariff announcement, the aftermath of the January 2025 Pacific Palisades and Altadena wildfires, House passage of a bill extending the 2017 tax cuts, DOGE savings far below early claims, trillion-dollar-plus deficits for five straight years, and US interest costs exceeding defense spending.

## Notable Claims

- By the 2025 fires, fewer than a quarter of affected properties were insured against fire (Perplexity text quoted by Marks).
- The US ran a roughly $1.8 trillion deficit, 6.4% of GDP, in fiscal 2024.
- The US goods trade deficit was $1.2 trillion in 2024 while services showed a $290 billion surplus.
- Social Security trust funds are projected to be exhausted in 2035, after which receipts would cover about 79% of benefits.
- CBO estimated the House tax bill would add $2.4 trillion to deficits over 10 years.

## Quotes

> governments can limit the rents landlords can charge for their apartments, but they can’t make developers build new ones.

> Tariffs are like the wall posited above.

> free-market economies don’t produce perfect solutions, but efforts to significantly control them make things much worse.
