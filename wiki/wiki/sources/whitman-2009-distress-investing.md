# Distress Investing: Principles and Technique

**Authors:** [[entities/martin-j-whitman|Martin J. Whitman]], [[entities/fernando-diz|Fernando Diz]]

**Year:** 2009

**Publisher:** John Wiley & Sons

**ISBN:** 978-0-470-11767-5

**Converted text:** `markdown_output/whitman-2009-distress-investing.md`

## Summary

Whitman and Diz write a practitioner's guide to buy-and-hold investing in the debt of troubled US companies. The book grew out of a distress investing seminar the two taught at Syracuse University. It says little about trading. It focuses on legal rights, priority of claims and what each claim is likely to receive in a workout.

The central idea is that Chapter 11 is the start of the analysis, not the end. The authors assume a money default may happen and ask what each claim gets next. Some claims are reinstated, some take new securities in a reorganization, and some are wiped out. They split the field into four businesses: performing loans likely to stay performing, small reorganizations, large reorganizations, and capital infusions into troubled firms.

Return measures depend on which business applies. For a performing loan the measures are yield to maturity, yield to an event and current yield. For a claim that will take part in a reorganization the measure is the price paid against an estimated workout value over an estimated time. The authors state 2008 hurdles: at least a 25 percent yield to maturity or to an event for performing loans, and an IRR well above 30 percent for reorganization plays.

Later chapters cover Chapter 11 mechanics, valuation by adjusted EBITDA multiples and liquidation analysis, legal risks to priority, and the form of plan consideration. Two detailed cases follow: the small prepackaged reorganization of Home Products International and the large Kmart reorganization. The book closes with proposals for a restructuring system that puts debtor feasibility first.

## Key Contributions

- Frames distressed debt analysis around what happens after a money default, using Chapter 11 rules as the template for all workouts, in court or out.
- Separates performing-loan investing (judged by yield to maturity or to an event) from reorganization investing (judged by price against workout value over time).
- States explicit 2008 hurdle rates: a 25 percent yield to maturity or to an event for performing loans and an IRR well above 30 percent for reorganization participations.
- Explains claim classes, the two-thirds-in-amount and one-half-in-number vote, cramdown, absolute priority and the fulcrum security, with a worked secured-claim cramdown example.
- Distinguishes control investors, who seek the fulcrum security and a say in the plan, from outside passive minority investors (OPMIs), who need a cash bailout or market-out.
- Reports evidence from 48 large Chapter 11 cases that case duration tracks the number of law firms retained, not debtor size or capital-structure complexity.
- Gives a distress valuation recipe: adjusted EBITDA less maintenance capex, a conservative multiple below peers, resource-conversion values such as below-market leases and NOLs, and a Chapter 7 liquidation floor.
- Documents two full cases (Home Products International and Kmart) with plan classes, recoveries, blocking positions and investor returns.

## How the Book Is Organised

- **Part One: The General Landscape of Distress Investing** — chapters 1-6. Market history to 2008, the authors' theory of markets and risk, causes of distress, professional costs, tax and governance issues, and five basic truths about creditor rights.
- **Part Two: Restructuring Troubled Issuers** — chapters 7-9. Voluntary exchange offers and the holdout problem, a legal review of Chapter 11, and the workout process with each party's leverage.
- **Part Three: The Investment Process** — chapters 10-13. Valuation, due diligence documents, risks to recovery after a filing, and why the form of plan consideration matters.
- **Part Four: Cases and Implications for Public Policy** — chapters 14-17. Case studies of 2008-2009 performing loans, the Home Products International prepack, the Kmart reorganization, and proposed reforms.

## Chapter Guide

### Chapter 1. The Changed Environment

**Question.** How did financial innovation, new bankruptcy law and the 2007-2008 meltdown change the supply and structure of distressed credit?

**Data.** Federal Reserve flow-of-funds aggregates for US nonfinancial corporate debt (1946-2007), default data credited to Edward Altman, and National Shared Credit data on syndicated loans, as cited by the authors.

**Method.** Descriptive history of leverage, junk bonds, mezzanine debt and the syndicated loan market. Lists 13 provisions of the 2005 Bankruptcy Abuse Prevention and Consumer Protection Act (BAPCPA) that affect businesses. These include caps on exclusivity (18 months to file a plan, 20 months to solicit), a 210-day deadline to assume or reject nonresidential leases, KERP limits, longer vendor reclamation periods, a two-year fraudulent-transfer look-back and the new Chapter 15. Introduces the performing-loan return measures used throughout the book.

**Findings.**

- Real corporate debt grew faster than GDP over 60 years, and the credit cycle became more volatile than GDP after 1970.
- Credit market debt of nonfinancial firms rose from 17.8 percent of assets in 1979 to 26.2 percent by 1990. Secured loans of distressed firms that sold near 40 cents in the early 1980s later cost 85 to 90 cents.
- Banks moved out of the riskiest lending. Nonbank institutions held almost 70 percent of syndicated loan par and 56 percent of nonaccrual loans in 2006.
- The authors' 2008 performing-loan picks were MBIA 14 percent surplus notes near 49 (yield to call 39.9 percent) and Forest City notes near 53 (yield to maturity 28.0 percent). They expected realized returns of about 37-38 and 26-27 percent because coupons cannot be reinvested at the quoted yield.
- BAPCPA caps the debtor's exclusive right to file a plan at 18 months and to solicit acceptances at 20 months. The authors note that executives evaded the new KERP limits by relabelling payments as incentive plans.

**Use for bond signals.** Aggregate leverage and the nonbank share of leveraged-loan holdings are regime variables for distressed supply and funding fragility. The point that quoted yield overstates realized return on deep-discount bonds argues for a reinvestment-adjusted expected-return feature rather than raw YTM.

### Chapter 2. The Theoretical Underpinning

**Question.** Why do efficient-market and risk-reward views fail for distressed securities, and what working framework replaces them?

**Method.** Defines a market as any arena where participants agree on price and terms they think are the best reasonably achievable. Four characteristics decide whether a market tends to instantaneous efficiency: the participant, the complexity of the asset, the time horizon, and the strength of external disciplines. Risk is used only with an adjective, and the authors reject a risk-reward ratio when price is not in equilibrium. Sets OPMI rules (buy cheap, buy quality or well-covenanted debt, ignore market risk) and 2008 return hurdles. Covers capital structure, payment priority, classification of claims, Section 1129(b) fair-and-equitable treatment, and distress valuation as an LBO analysis run in reverse.

**Findings.**

- Only simple securities with a fixed end date tend to be priced with near-instantaneous efficiency: credits without credit risk, derivatives and risk arbitrage. Troubled debt that will enter a reorganization of unknowable timing is inherently inefficient by these standards.
- The authors' 2008 rules of thumb: a performing loan needs at least a 25 percent yield to maturity or to an event, with small loss if it reorganizes instead. A claim expected to default and reorganize needs an IRR well above 30 percent.
- Example prices in 2008: GMAC notes due in 15 months at 60 yielded 55 percent to maturity. MBIA surplus notes at 53 had a 27 percent current yield. The authors argue price moves do not matter to an unlevered holder if the loans keep performing.
- A claim's value in distress depends on its class and payment priority under a plan, not on the instrument's name. Subordination agreements survive in Chapter 11 and junior classes get nothing if a dissenting senior class is crammed down.
- Distress valuation follows LBO analysis: forecast operating income, EBIT, EBITDA and separable asset values, then apply a capitalization. The difference is that the analyst levers down rather than up. The authors list eight differences from LBO investing, including safe DIP financing and a lower chance that a creditor is wiped out.

**Use for bond signals.** A hurdle filter is directly usable: flag high-yield bonds whose yield exceeds 25 percent only when a separate model gives a low default probability. The efficiency argument implies mispricing should concentrate where the resolution date is uncertain. Recovery features should key on class and priority position, not on the instrument label.

### Chapter 3. The Causes of Financial Distress

**Question.** What puts companies into distress, and why does the cause matter for the workout value of each claim?

**Method.** Four case-based causes: lack of access to capital markets (Drexel Burnham Lambert, Bear Stearns), deterioration of operating performance (Home Products International), deterioration of GAAP performance from mark-to-market accounting (MBIA), and large off-balance-sheet contingent liabilities (USG asbestos). The authors prefer an ability-to-pay test over a balance-sheet insolvency test. They map the cause to the starting assumption of the analysis.

**Findings.**

- Loss of market access can sink a firm that is not insolvent. Drexel's parent filed because it could not refinance $30 million of commercial paper.
- Home Products International financed acquisitions almost entirely with debt. Margin erosion from resin costs, imports and dependence on three customers then led to a missed coupon on its 9 5/8 percent notes in November 2006.
- MBIA's 2007 GAAP losses came mostly from marks on insured credit default swaps held to maturity, not from expected claims. The common stock was the fulcrum security in its de facto reorganization, which raised $1.5 billion of equity and $1 billion of surplus notes.
- USG filed for Chapter 11 in 2001 while well capitalized, to contain asbestos claims rather than to fix operations or liquidity.
- Rule of thumb: if the business is sound but the capital structure is not, start from a feasible reorganization. If the business is unsound, start from a liquidating plan or Chapter 7. If a sudden tort or contract liability caused the distress, focus on claims likely to be reinstated or paid in full.

**Use for bond signals.** Cause of distress is a conditioning variable for recovery models: refinancing walls, margin erosion, accounting marks and contingent liabilities imply different workouts. Customer concentration and gross-margin trend are early-warning features. Oversecured claims are largely insensitive to the cause.

### Chapter 4. Deal Expenses and Who Bears Them

**Question.** How large are professional fees in Chapter 11, who pays them, and what drives them?

**Data.** Fee and expense data for 48 large Chapter 11 cases (30 conventional, 18 prepackaged or prenegotiated) from Lynn M. LoPucki's database. Case durations by filing type, 1980-2007. Retention and success-fee terms for financial advisers in 2003-2004 cases.

**Method.** Compares average fees by plan type and professional type. Tests which factors associate with case duration (debtor size, capital-structure complexity measured by number of plan classes, number of professional firms retained). Analyses attorney and financial-adviser fees separately because attorneys bill by time while advisers earn large time-independent success fees. Legal basis: Sections 503(b)(4) and 507 give professional fees administrative-expense super-priority, paid in cash as the case runs.

**Findings.**

- Professional fees are paid in cash ahead of unsecured claims. They come out of unsecured and not adequately protected creditors. In cases with reorganization value under about $300 million they can wipe out the estate.
- In the 48-case sample attorneys took on average 55 percent of professional costs and financial advisers 45 percent. The split did not depend on whether the case was conventional or preplanned.
- Average attorney fees and expenses in the 30 conventional cases were $9,881,535.
- The only factor statistically associated with case duration was the number of professional firms retained, driven by law firms. Size and complexity did not predict duration. Preplanned cases retained fewer firms regardless of size or complexity.
- Legal bills are predictable from duration, book assets and number of plan classes. Financial-adviser bills relate to none of these, only to the number of adviser firms. One incentive fee was tied to the rise in bond prices over a 9.83-cent baseline and capped at $7.5 million.

**Use for bond signals.** Expected professional-cost drag is a haircut on unsecured recovery. It should be larger for conventional filings, small estates and cases with many committees or retained firms. A prepackaged or prenegotiated filing flag should raise expected unsecured recovery and shorten expected time to resolution.

### Chapter 5. Other Important Issues

**Question.** How do management entrenchment, compensation and tax rules affect creditors of distressed companies?

**Method.** Describes management leverage from exclusivity under Section 1121 and KERP rules under Section 503(c)(1). Covers tax mechanics: cancellation of debt (COD) income under IRC Section 108 and the order of tax-attribute reduction, original issue discount (OID), repeal of the stock-for-debt exception in 1993, and Section 382 ownership-change limits with the 382(l)(5) bankruptcy exception. Gives a rule of thumb for when debt counts as a security for tax purposes.

**Findings.**

- Management usually stays in place in Chapter 11 and controls the plan during exclusivity. The authors say this yields high compensation, entrenchment and favourable terms for managers.
- Speed matters most for small debtors. With reorganization values under $300 million, a protracted case lets fees eat into feasibility and recoveries.
- Debt with an original maturity of ten years or more tends to be treated as a security for tax purposes. New-for-old debt exchanges of securities then qualify as tax-free recapitalizations.
- Section 382 limits NOL use after an ownership change of more than 50 percentage points within three years. The bankruptcy exception counts creditor stock only for debt held at least 18 months before filing ('old and cold').
- Courts increasingly grant NOL-preservation motions that restrict claims and equity trading. The authors call preserving NOLs this way a bad idea because it removes the noncontrol holder's market-out.

**Use for bond signals.** A flag for NOL-preservation trading restrictions should predict lower claim liquidity after filing. OID and market-discount tax treatment reduce after-tax returns on deep-discount bonds and matter for taxable investors' demand.

### Chapter 6. The Five Basic Truths of Distress Investing

**Question.** Which legal and economic facts should anchor every distressed-credit analysis?

**Method.** Five truths. (1) Outside a court proceeding no one can take away a creditor's right to a money payment without consent. (2) Chapter 11 rules shape every reorganization. (3) Securities have substantive characteristics, and value is the present worth of a future cash bailout. (4) Restructurings are costly for creditors. (5) Creditors have only contractual rights. Uses Trust Indenture Act Section 316 and typical indenture amendment clauses. Compares voluntary exchanges, conventional Chapter 11 and prepackaged plans. Scenario analysis of GMAC 7 3/4 percent notes.

**Findings.**

- GMAC 7 3/4 percent notes in October 2008 yielded 53.42 percent to maturity with a 12.50 percent current yield. The authors gave a 70-75 percent chance the notes stayed performing and a 25-30 percent chance of Chapter 11, with workout value of $60 to $90.
- Outside Chapter 11, a shorter maturity gives de facto seniority. Inside Chapter 11, seniority rests on covenants and intercreditor agreements, and maturity dates of unsecured debt no longer matter.
- Under the Trust Indenture Act, holders of at least 75 percent of principal may postpone interest for up to three years if the indenture allows. Every other change to money terms needs each holder's consent.
- Value requires a cash bailout from one of three sources: payments by the company, sale to a market, or control. Rule 1145 limits sales by holders of 5 percent or more of new stock to the greater of 15 percent of daily volume or 1 percent of the issue.
- The authors reject the academic estimate that direct Chapter 11 costs average 3.1 percent of debt plus equity value. They argue fees should be measured against the debtor's cash at reorganization because fees are paid in cash with super-priority.

**Use for bond signals.** A scenario-weighted value (probability of staying performing times the contractual path, plus probability of reorganization times workout value) can be compared with price to form a signal. Time to maturity is a de facto seniority feature for distressed issuers that are likely to avoid a filing.

### Chapter 7. Voluntary Exchanges

**Question.** When can an out-of-court exchange offer succeed, and how does it compare with Chapter 11 on tax?

**Method.** Analyses the holdout problem: a non-tendering holder is credit-enhanced if others exchange. Two forms of seniority are contractual priority and earlier maturity. Two coercive fixes: exit consents that subordinate holdouts (most indentures let 50 percent amend nonmoney terms) and new bonds that mature before the old ones. Covers tax: COD income for a solvent firm, the insolvency exclusion, OID for exchanging holders, and the Section 3(a)(9) registration exemption.

**Findings.**

- Exchange offers often fail when they seek 80 percent or more of an outstanding issue.
- In the authors' example, $100 million of 6 percent debentures trade at 60 and the offer needs 90 percent acceptance. Holdouts expect their bonds to trade near 80 after a successful exchange, a 9.09 percent yield, so the offer fails.
- An exchange works only if non-exchangers face a downside. Exit consents can subordinate them, because nonmoney indenture terms can usually be amended by 50 percent of the issue.
- Bonds likely to be paid in full with interest on interest after a reorganization act like quasi zero-coupon bonds. MBIA surplus notes in October 2008 cost $48.10 with a 29.1 percent current yield and 39.6 percent yield to call.
- OID taxes phantom income. A new bond first priced at 60 and paying 100 in 50 months accretes 80 cents of taxable income a month.

**Use for bond signals.** An exchange-offer outcome model can use the participation threshold, presence of exit consents, relative maturity of new versus old paper and the price discount. Holdout bonds can rally after a successful exchange, which suggests an event feature around announced distressed exchanges.

### Chapter 8. A Brief Review of Chapter 11

**Question.** Which legal mechanics of Chapter 11 decide which claims get what?

**Method.** Covers liquidation (Chapter 7) versus reorganization, involuntary petitions, venue and forum shopping, parties, the automatic stay, adequate protection, Section 363 asset sales with stalking-horse protections, executory contracts and lease-rejection caps, preferences and fraudulent conveyances, secured versus unsecured status under Section 506(a), setoff, contractual and equitable subordination, DIP financing under Section 364, exclusivity, statutory priorities, critical-vendor payments, disclosure statements, the five mandatory plan provisions, voting, and confirmation tests (best interests, cramdown, feasibility). Includes a worked cramdown of a secured claim and the Section 1111(b) election.

**Findings.**

- A claim is secured only up to collateral value. A $1 million loan backed by property worth $800,000 splits into an $800,000 secured claim and a $200,000 unsecured claim.
- A class accepts a plan if holders of at least two-thirds in amount and more than half in number of the allowed claims voting approve. Classes of interests need only the two-thirds in amount.
- Cramdown of a secured claim requires payments whose present value at the cramdown rate equals the collateral value. A $10 million claim on $7 million collateral at 8 percent over five years needs equal payments of $1,753,195.2, so $\text{Payment} = \frac{PV \cdot i}{1-(1+i)^{-n}}$ (our rendering of an OCR-damaged displayed formula).
- Absolute priority applies only when a dissenting impaired class is crammed down. A consensual plan can give value to a junior class before a senior class is paid in full. Some courts allow a 'new value' exception for old equity.
- Venue matters for trade claims. Kmart got approval to pay about $300 million to unnamed critical vendors, but the district court reversed the order and the Seventh Circuit affirmed.

**Use for bond signals.** Recovery estimates need the capital structure as ordered classes with collateral values, subordination agreements and the secured/unsecured split. Filing venue can proxy for how trade claims and critical-vendor motions will be treated.

### Chapter 9. The Workout Process

**Question.** How do each party's goals and leverage shape a reorganization, and how do conventional, preplanned and Section 363 cases differ?

**Data.** Lynn M. LoPucki's Bankruptcy Research Database: 368 large Chapter 11 dispositions, 2000-2007, split by degree of prefiling planning and by reorganization versus Section 363 sale.

**Method.** Profiles the judge, management, secured creditors, trade creditors, general unsecured creditors, noncontrol and control vulture investors, and professionals. Classifies cases as conventional, prenegotiated, prepackaged or Section 363 sales. Lists leverage factors: exclusivity for management, stay relief and adequate protection for secured creditors, collateral valuation (liquidation value if sold, replacement cost if used), equitable subordination, lien-perfection challenges, fraudulent conveyance, structural subordination and substantive consolidation.

**Findings.**

- Of 368 large-case dispositions in 2000-2007, 73 percent came from conventional filings and 27 percent from prenegotiated or prepackaged filings. About 12.5 percent ended as confirmed Section 363 asset sales.
- For firms under about $200 million in assets, a prepackaged or prenegotiated plan is the only realistic way to survive as a going concern. It also removes the holdout problem because the two-thirds and one-half votes bind the class.
- Public companies typically stay in Chapter 11 between 18 months and three years.
- Noncontrol vulture investors care about near-term prices and IRR on market prices. Control vulture investors hold controlling amounts of the fulcrum security and care about feasibility and long-term business value.
- Without subsidiary guarantees, trade claims at an operating subsidiary rank ahead of parent unsecured claims (structural subordination). Substantive consolidation can erase this ordering but courts use it reluctantly.

**Use for bond signals.** Filing type (prepackaged, prenegotiated, conventional, 363 sale) maps to expected duration and recovery and can be coded as an event feature. An issuer-entity flag (holding company versus operating subsidiary, with or without guarantees) captures structural subordination for recovery models.

### Chapter 10. How to Analyze: Valuation

**Question.** How should a distress investor value a troubled company to estimate the workout of each claim?

**Method.** Values firms as both going concerns and resource-conversion vehicles. Earnings proxy is adjusted EBITDA, corrected for nonrecurring items, restructuring charges and impairments, and reduced by the capex needed to maintain the asset base. EBITDAR adds back operating leases. A valuation multiple is a present-value factor for a growing annuity over 25 periods (the displayed formula is garbled in the OCR). Comparable public-company and deal multiples set upper bounds. Adds resource-conversion values: segment sales, below-market leases assignable in Chapter 11, and NOLs subject to Section 382. Closes with the Chapter 7 liquidation analysis required by the best-interests test.

**Findings.**

- EBITDA minus the capex needed to maintain the business is a better earnings measure than EBITDA. Distressed firms often defer capex, so reported capex can understate the need.
- A multiple equals the present value of a growing one-dollar annuity. The authors use 25 periods because longer horizons barely change multiples at the low growth rates a distress investor would assume.
- Comparable multiples are upper bounds for a troubled company. Home Products International's valuation used 4.63x EV/EBITDA, well below healthy peers.
- Below-market leases are assets in Chapter 11 because the debtor can assign them over the landlord's objection. Kmart Holdings later sold four properties to Home Depot for about $59 million.
- NOLs can be carried back two years and forward 20 years. The Section 382(l)(5) exception requires old shareholders and creditors to own at least 50 percent of the reorganized debtor's stock.

**Use for bond signals.** A recovery model can compute enterprise value as a below-peer multiple times EBITDA less maintenance capex, then run the claim waterfall to find the fulcrum class. Lease portfolios and NOLs are hidden assets that can move recoveries for retailers and loss-making issuers.

### Chapter 11. Due Diligence for Distressed Issues

**Question.** Which documents and checks should a distress investor rely on, and how should their authors' biases be read?

**Method.** Reverse-engineers the incentives of document preparers. Corporate attorneys write 10-K and 10-Q narratives with a negative bias. Auditors lean conservative. Bankruptcy filings are adversarial. Lists key 10-K items and footnotes, bankruptcy filings (first-day affidavit, creditor lists, docket, fee applications, stay-relief petitions, DIP proposals, plans), regulatory filings of regulated subsidiaries, trading information and industry sources. Ends with a checklist that includes structural subordination, substantive consolidation, events of default and equitable-and-ratable clauses.

**Findings.**

- Holding-company debt over highly regulated subsidiaries that give no guarantees is structurally subordinated. Dividends are the main cash source and regulators can block them.
- Initial Chapter 11 filings list the 20 largest unsecured and five largest secured creditors of record. Record holders are usually indenture trustees, not beneficial owners.
- Court documents are on PACER at 8 cents a page, capped at $16 per document over 200 pages.
- Form 10-K lacks consolidating statements, but disclosures usually let the analyst estimate the parent's stand-alone position and the financials of guarantor subsidiaries.

**Use for bond signals.** Text features from filings can flag guarantee structures, structural subordination, events of default and equitable-and-ratable clauses. Monthly operating reports in Chapter 11 give a cash-burn series for each debtor.

### Chapter 12. Distress Investing Risks

**Question.** What specific risks threaten a claim buyer's recovery after a Chapter 11 filing?

**Method.** Defines risk types (investment, credit, operational, credit rating, inflation, market, reorganization). Groups distress risks into four categories. (1) Alteration of priorities: equitable subordination (Mobile Steel test, assignment versus sale under Enron v. Springfield), substantive and deemed consolidation (Auto-Train, Augie/Restivo and Owens Corning tests), fraudulent-conveyance attacks on upstream and cross-stream guarantees, critical-vendor payments and lien-perfection defects. (2) Collateral and enterprise valuation. (3) Reorganization risks. (4) Other risks: classification, cramdown, blocking positions and uncontrolled professional costs.

**Findings.**

- Equitable subordination needs inequitable conduct, injury to other creditors and consistency with the code. A disability attached to a seller travels with an assigned claim but not with a sold claim.
- Junior classes push for high enterprise values to stay in the money. Senior classes prefer lower values. Issuing more senior securities or cash under a plan tends to reduce feasibility.
- Because votes count only claims actually voting, a holder of slightly more than one-third in amount of a class holds a blocking position.
- Kmart's critical-vendor order paid 2,330 suppliers about $300 million, financed by a super-priority DIP facility, at the expense of other unsecured creditors.
- Time is the enemy of unsecured creditors. In small cases the reorganization should finish within the exclusivity period or little value will remain for them.

**Use for bond signals.** Claim-level risk flags can be built for upstream guarantees (fraudulent-conveyance risk), insider or lender-control history (equitable subordination) and multi-entity structures (consolidation risk). A holder with more than one-third of a class signals blocking power that can shift recoveries.

### Chapter 13. Form of Consideration versus Amount of Consideration

**Question.** Why does the form of plan consideration matter as much as its amount?

**Method.** Maps constituency preferences. Banks and insurers want cash or covenanted cash-pay senior paper for regulatory and liability-matching reasons. Trade creditors, landlords and employees want a feasible debtor. Public noncontrol bondholders want a market-out at the highest price. Creditors seeking control want equity. Junior holders want a high reorganization value. Relates these preferences to plan feasibility.

**Findings.**

- Banks and insurers usually have at least 90 percent of assets offset by cash-consuming liabilities, so they demand cash or cash-pay instruments.
- A larger reorganization value lets junior classes participate and lets banks receive more cash-pay debt. It also burdens the reorganized firm with more required payments and lowers feasibility.
- Creditor-led changes of control include Eddie Lampert at Kmart, Third Avenue at Home Products International, a Sam Zell entity at ACL, and Danielson Holding's acquisition of Mission Insurance (later Covanta).
- For passive equity recipients, post-reorganization market prices matter most. For control investors the aim is long-term value creation.

**Use for bond signals.** The share of plan recovery paid in cash or new debt versus equity, and post-emergence leverage, are candidate predictors of re-default for reorganized issuers. They are useful for screening bonds issued by recently emerged firms.

Concepts: [[concepts/zombie-firms|Zombie Firms and Zombification]] · [[concepts/leverage-ratios|Leverage Ratios]] · [[concepts/reorganization-value|Reorganization Value]]

### Chapter 14. Brief Case Studies of Distressed Securities, 2008-2009

**Question.** How do the four distress businesses (performing loans, small cases, large cases and capital infusions) work in the 2008-2009 market?

**Method.** Third Avenue Value Fund's late-2008 performing-loan holdings with yields and the managers' probabilities of staying performing. A price-accretion table for a 6 percent ten-year bullet at a constant 25 percent yield. A definition of small cases as those where operating losses plus fees exceed the cash saved by not servicing unsecured debt. Third Avenue's playbook of buying performing loans, controlling at least half of an issue and running a prepack on default. Typical prepack terms, large-case dynamics and 2008 capital infusions including TARP terms.

**Findings.**

- Moody's projected the global speculative-grade issuer default rate to rise to 4.3 percent by end-2008 and 10.4 percent a year later. Many performing loans then yielded 25 to 54 percent to maturity.
- The managers put the chance of staying performing at 90-95 percent for Forest City and MBIA and 70-80 percent for GMAC.
- After the 2008 meltdown, Third Avenue raised its required yield to maturity on potential small-case performing loans from 15 percent to 25 percent. It seeks at least 50 percent of an issue so nonmoney indenture changes need its consent.
- A typical prepack gives 90 to 95 percent of new common stock to former bondholders and 5 to 10 percent to junior holders. Management gets options on 6 to 12 percent struck at reorganization value per share.
- A 6 percent ten-year bullet bought at a 25 percent yield pulls to par if the yield stays constant. The authors note quoted yields assume reinvestment at the same rate, which is unrealistic.

**Use for bond signals.** Probability of staying performing times promised yield gives an expected-return screen for distressed high yield. The constant-yield accretion path benchmarks pull-to-par for deep-discount performing bonds. Holder concentration above 50 percent of an issue signals who controls amendments and any later prepack.

### Chapter 15. A Small Case: Home Products International

**Question.** How did a small prepackaged Chapter 11 move control of Home Products International to its majority noteholder?

**Data.** Company filings, Third Avenue shareholder letters and the plan disclosure materials for Home Products International, 1992-2007.

**Method.** Case history: debt-financed acquisitions, margin erosion and customer concentration, a contest for control of the equity, and Third Avenue's purchase of over half of the 9 5/8 percent senior subordinated notes. Then an indenture amendment for a consent fee, a missed coupon and a prepackaged plan. Plan details cover classes, the debt-for-equity split, exit financing and convertible PIK notes, a comparable-company valuation and a hypothetical Chapter 7 liquidation for the best-interests test.

**Findings.**

- Sales to the three largest customers rose to 74 percent of the total by 2002. The company could not pass on resin and steel cost increases.
- Third Avenue bought the notes at about a 21 percent yield to maturity and 16.8 percent current yield, and reached 50 percent of principal, giving it control of the likely fulcrum class.
- Under the plan the notes were extinguished. Noteholders received 95 percent of the new common stock and old equity 5 percent. Only two of seven classes were impaired.
- Enterprise value used EBITDA multiples of 4.5x to 4.75x on a 2007 EBITDA forecast of $10,476,000, giving a midpoint of $48,454,000 against $44 million of exit debt.
- In the hypothetical liquidation unsecured claims would recover about 1.5 percent, so the plan met the best-interests test. The case took 90 days from filing to consummation.

**Use for bond signals.** Customer concentration and failure to pass on input costs are early distress features for small high-yield issuers. The case shows a subordinated fulcrum class converting into about 95 percent of equity when enterprise value barely exceeds exit debt.

### Chapter 16. A Large Reorganization Case: Kmart Corporation

**Question.** What did the Kmart Chapter 11 show about leases, vendors, KERPs, guarantees, blocking positions, DIP financing and investor returns?

**Data.** Kmart disclosure statement, 10-K reports, fee applications from PACER, and Third Avenue Value Fund claim purchases, 2002-2005.

**Method.** Case analysis of Section 365 lease decisions and designation-rights sales, the critical-vendor order, the KERP, fraudulent-transfer and subsidiary-guarantee challenges settled through deemed consolidation, professional costs, blocking positions and claims trading (Rule 3001(e)(2), big boy letters, holder data from Schedule D, 13F and Bloomberg), DIP financing under Section 364(c), the plan, and an approximate reconstruction of Third Avenue's returns.

**Findings.**

- Kmart owned 133 of its 2,114 stores and leased 1,981, many at below-market rents. It sold designation rights on 56 closed-store leaseholds for $46 million.
- The KERP contemplated almost $300 million for 9,705 employees, against $25 million for 395 at WorldCom. Professionals billed about $10 million a month.
- ESL and Third Avenue held 55.3 percent of the note class, and ESL held 35.6 percent of the lender class. That gave them blocking positions in both potential fulcrum classes.
- Reorganization value was $2.25 billion to $3.0 billion, about six times pro forma EBITDA. Prepetition lenders got cash equal to 40 percent of claims, noteholders recovered 14.4 percent in stock, and trade and lease-rejection claims 9.7 percent.
- Third Avenue's cost basis fell from about 57 to 52.51 percent of claim. Buying plan shares at $10 cut its cost per share to $26.24. Shares later traded at $100, roughly a 56 percent annual IRR.

**Use for bond signals.** Claim prices fell steadily after filing while cash burn rose, so monthly operating reports can feed a post-filing timing signal. The gap between bank (40 percent cash) and note (14.4 percent) recoveries shows how guarantees and settlements split nominally similar unsecured claims. Accumulation of blocking stakes can be tracked from 13F, Schedule D and holder data.

### Chapter 17. An Ideal Restructuring System

**Question.** What should a good-enough restructuring system achieve, and what reforms do the authors propose?

**Method.** States dual goals: debtor feasibility, and net present value for creditors under strict absolute priority. Notes the two can conflict. Lists three US strengths, seven goals for an ideal system and three reform areas. The reforms are to end estate funding of other constituencies' professionals except for substantial contributions to feasibility, to curb management entrenchment, and to encourage feasibility and market-outs.

**Findings.**

- Senior creditors taking cash or covenanted senior paper often reduces the debtor's feasibility, so the dual goals conflict.
- Chapter 11 works as a market test. If a debtor cannot get super-priority DIP or trade credit from experienced lenders in normal times, it probably should go out of business.
- Proposed governance reforms include a shadow trustee picked by the creditor group with the largest stake, a model charter, and a new board election one year after reorganization. Groups with 20 percent or more of votes would get proxy costs financed.
- The authors favour low reorganization values and see fixing the companies as the main unsolved problem. They expect repeat filings ('Chapter 22') to grow.

**Use for bond signals.** Issuers that emerge with high reorganization values and heavy cash-pay debt are candidates for repeat filings. Emergence leverage relative to plan value is a feature for forecasting defaults among post-reorganization bonds.

## Questions Raised

- Can the authors' probability that a bond stays performing be estimated systematically from fundamentals and price, and does price times that probability predict returns?
- Do deep-discount bonds that stay performing deliver returns close to their quoted yield, given the authors' point that yield to maturity overstates realized return?
- Does the finding that case duration tracks the number of law firms retained still hold on post-2005 BAPCPA data?
- How well do the stated hurdles (25 percent yield for performing loans, IRR above 30 percent for reorganizations) perform as screens in a systematic high-yield backtest?
- Can the fulcrum security be identified ex ante from public capital-structure data and an EBITDA multiple valuation?
- Do reorganized issuers that emerge with high reorganization values and cash-pay debt re-default more often (the 'Chapter 22' risk)?

## Intended Audience

Distress investors, value investors and control investors, plus others involved in corporate restructurings such as private equity, lawyers and policy makers. The book assumes some finance background but explains Chapter 11 law from the ground up.
