# All That Glitters

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** December 17, 2010 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2010-12-17-all-that-glitters.pdf)

## Summary

Marks takes on gold after it had roughly doubled in two years following the financial crisis. He first states the gold bug's case and the sceptic's case as polar opposites, then argues that gold is like religion: you either believe or you don't. His core analytical point is that assets producing cash flow can be valued, while non-income-producing assets such as gold, oil, homes and collectibles are worth only what buyers will pay. Price must matter, but gold has no quantifiable value to compare against. He then turns the argument on fiat currencies, which also have value only because people agree they do, and describes how a weak dollar, quantitative easing, fiscal indiscipline in Washington and Chinese buying were pushing demand toward gold by a process of elimination. He ends agnostic: gold has worked for centuries and may deserve a 'just in case' diversifying position, but the price question remains.

## Key Ideas

1. Cash-flow-producing assets can be valued with P/Es, yields and cap rates. Non-income-producing assets have no analytical value, only what buyers will pay.
2. No asset is a good or bad idea without reference to its price, and gold has no quantifiable value against which to judge its price.
3. Gold works as a store of value solely because people agree it does, much like collectible plates in Condon's novel.
4. Fiat currencies such as the post-1971 dollar also rest only on belief, so scepticism about gold should extend to currencies.
5. Keynes's beauty contest applies: the question is whether people will keep imputing value to gold, and whether others will bet that they will.
6. Gold acts as a barometer of inflation fears and doubts about currencies. A rising dollar price of gold may really reflect a weakening dollar.
7. Gold's popularity as a reserve asset comes largely by elimination, as the dollar, euro, sterling and yen each have problems and the renminbi is not freely exchangeable.
8. Marks's 'agnostic' position: he would not bet against gold and might hold some for diversification, but questions whether the current price is the right entry point.

## Market Context

December 2010: gold near $1,400 an ounce after doubling in about two years, the Fed running a $600 billion round of quantitative easing (QE2), ultra-low interest rates, European austerity, a failed G20 accord on global imbalances, the US tax-cut compromise, and the deficit commission's proposals failing to get a supermajority. Investors Marks met worldwide felt the range of possible outcomes was wider than ever.

## Notable Claims

- Gold peaked at $850 in 1980 and gained only 2% a year since then.
- Gold was $250 in mid-1999, implying 16% a year for a decade-plus.
- Oil at $35 in December 2008 versus $147 in July 2007 shows qualitative arguments cannot set a price.
- China imported 209.7 metric tons of gold in the first 10 months of 2010, a fivefold increase (WSJ via Xinhua).
- The December 2010 tax compromise had an estimated cost of $858 billion over ten years.

## Quotes

> My view is simple and starts with the observation that gold is a lot like religion.

> In fact, that’s true of all non-income-producing assets: they’re only worth what buyers will pay for them.

> I’ve said it many times: no asset can be considered a good idea (or a bad idea) without reference to its price.
