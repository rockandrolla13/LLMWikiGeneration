# Will It Work?

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** March 5, 2009 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2009-03-05-will-it-work.pdf)

## Summary

Prompted by his son Andrew asking whether Treasury Secretary Geithner was doing the right things, Marks argues that economics is not an exact science and that there is no 'golden tool' for fixing the 2008-09 crisis. Every policy option involves conflicting goals: stimulus vs inflation, deficit spending vs foreign Treasury demand, bank recapitalisation vs rewarding mistakes, foreclosure relief vs moral hazard, auto rescues vs rationalisation. He critiques the stimulus bill's bonus limits at rescued banks, weighs the pros and cons of bank nationalisation, and endorses a friend's idea of separating government-protected deposit banking from risky investing, in the spirit of Glass-Steagall. He describes how politics, re-election incentives and partisan voting shape policy, and the rhetorical bind of officials who must both dramatise and calm. His bottom line: the economy must delever, capital destruction must be dealt with through restructurings, and confidence must be restored. Investors should rely on intrinsic value, company survival and their own staying power, and accept giving up some upside.

## Key Ideas

1. Economics is not an exact science; its main ingredient is psychology, so no single policy tool can be relied on to fix the economy.
2. Policy responses involve conflicting goals and compromises; some will work, some will fail, and some will help one facet while aggravating another.
3. Marks reaffirms membership of the 'I don't know' school and rejects confident forecasts of recovery timing based on past recessions.
4. In the crisis, investors can fall back only on intrinsic value, company survival and their own staying power.
5. Bonus caps at bailed-out banks may backfire by driving away talent, encouraging early repayment of federal capital, and weakening lending.
6. Separating government-backed deposit-taking from high-leverage risky investing (as Glass-Steagall did until its 1999 repeal) can prevent 'lemon socialism'.
7. Recovery requires deleveraging, dealing with capital destruction (bankruptcies, foreclosures, restructurings that move assets to new owners) and restoring confidence.
8. Assuring survival in bad times is inconsistent with maximising returns in good times; Oaktree willingly cedes the part of the return distribution between 'solid' and 'maximum'.

## Market Context

March 2009, near the depth of the global financial crisis and recession. The new Obama administration and Treasury Secretary Geithner were rolling out stimulus and bank-rescue measures. Live debates included bank nationalisation, buying toxic assets, suspending mark-to-market accounting, mortgage relief, auto-industry bailouts, derivatives regulation and bonus limits at TARP-assisted banks. Consumer confidence was weak, and deleveraging and capital destruction were under way.

## Notable Claims

- The stimulus law bars TARP-recipient firms from paying top earners bonuses above one-third of total pay (WSJ, Feb 14).
- Citi paid a $7 billion premium for protection on $301 billion of mortgage securities, taking the first $30 billion and 10% beyond.
- Only three of 217 Republican votes in Congress supported the stimulus package.
- Glass-Steagall's powers ended in 1999 with the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act.
- The 'I know' school consensus expected recovery to start in Q1 2010.

## Quotes

> The main ingredient in economics is psychology, and the workings of psychology clearly can’t be fully known, controlled or fixed.

> That’s why crises will recur and history will continue to rhyme.

> This time is no different.
