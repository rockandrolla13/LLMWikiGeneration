# Growing the Pie

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** April 1, 2019 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/growing-the-pie.pdf)

## Summary

A political-economy memo, written just after Oaktree's partnership with Brookfield, that continues Marks's January 2019 worry about left-wing populism and anti-capitalist sentiment. Borrowing Ray Dalio's line that populists of the right don't know how to divide the pie while populists of the left don't know how to grow it, he argues that capitalism created the biggest pie, at the price of the unequal sharing Churchill called its 'inherent vice'. He uses Nicholas Lardy's 1986 account of China's post-1978 agricultural decollectivisation to show that incentives raised output and incomes sharply but also increased inequality and weakened public services. His main case is New York's loss of Amazon's HQ2: he argues the $3 billion in incentives were contingent future tax credits, not money that could be spent elsewhere, so rejecting the deal forfeited jobs and roughly $24 billion of net taxes. He also criticises the proposed pied-à-terre tax. He concludes that business must work to reverse stagnant, unequal incomes so that the capitalist system survives, without shrinking the pie.

## Key Ideas

1. Populists of the right struggle to divide the pie; populists of the left struggle to grow it (Dalio).
2. Capitalism's gains derive from letting the more able and hard-working earn bigger slices; inequality is its 'inherent vice', and equal sharing of miseries is socialism's 'inherent virtue' (Churchill).
3. China's post-1978 shift to household farming and market incentives sped up grain output growth to about 5% a year and nearly tripled farm income, while raising inequality and weakening rural services.
4. The Amazon HQ2 incentives were contingent rebates of future taxes, not a pot of cash; rejecting them gave up jobs and net tax revenue.
5. Resentment-based policies such as the pied-à-terre tax may depress related markets and employment rather than help.
6. Responsible capitalism: business should act to arrest stagnant and unequal incomes in order to preserve the system.

## Market Context

April 2019, ahead of the 2020 US election cycle, with populism on the left and right and proposals for wealth taxes, higher top income-tax brackets and a New York pied-à-terre tax. Amazon withdrew from its Long Island City HQ2 plan on February 14, 2019. Oaktree had just announced its partnership with Brookfield Asset Management.

## Notable Claims

- The Queens HQ2 deal involved about 25,000 Amazon jobs, $27bn of projected incremental taxes over 25 years and $3bn returned to Amazon.
- No Amazon meant no $3bn paid out and no $24bn of net taxes received.
- Chinese grain output rose from 305 to 407 million tons, almost 5% a year, versus 2.1% in 1957-78 (quoting Lardy).
- Chinese per capita farm income rose from 134 to 355 yuan, 1978-84 (quoting Lardy).
- 238 entities bid for HQ2.

## Quotes

> As a rule, populists of the right (who are usually capitalists) don’t know how to divide the pie well, while populists of the left (who are usually socialists) don’t know how to grow the pie.

— Ray Dalio and Bridgewater, quoted by Marks

> In other words, you can’t have it all.

> Capitalism is the most dependable route to prosperity. And it has to be responsible capitalism.
