# Warning Flags

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** May 12, 2010 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2010-05-12-warning-flags.pdf)

## Summary

Marks frames investing around two risks, losing money and missing opportunity, and argues that investors obsess over the wrong one at the extremes. Before mid-2007 nobody feared loss, and he calls that shortage of risk aversion the main cause of the crisis. In late 2008 nobody would take risk even at 19% high yield bond yields. He then assembles press clippings from spring 2010 showing a return of covenant-lite loans, PIK toggle notes, dividend recaps, IPOs, CLOs, mega-buyouts and a low VIX. He explains the fast rebound by the brevity of the crisis, near-zero rates and Fed asset purchases. He uses the Greek debt crisis, which broke while he was writing, to show that the danger is the surprise nobody listed, and urges more caution as prices rise relative to intrinsic value.

## Key Ideas

1. Investors face twin risks, losing money and missing opportunity, and cannot avoid both; at extremes they usually guard against the wrong one.
2. Risk aversion is essential for markets to work; its absence before mid-2007 was the main cause of the crisis.
3. In Q4 2008 no return was high enough to induce buying, so the cheapest assets of the cycle went to the few with dry powder and nerve.
4. Spring 2010 clippings on covenant-lite loans, PIK toggles, dividend recaps, CLO revival and mega-LBOs signal that fear of missed opportunity is back.
5. Near-zero policy rates and Fed purchases of mortgage securities pushed investors into riskier assets and sped the recovery of risk tolerance.
6. The most dangerous risk is 'something else', the surprise nobody anticipated, as Greece showed.
7. The higher prices are relative to intrinsic value, the more one should allow for the unknown and demand a margin for error.

## Market Context

Written April to May 2010, about 18 months after the Lehman bankruptcy. High yield had rallied from December 2008, the Fed had just ended its $1.25 trillion mortgage-buying program, short rates were near zero, stocks were back to pre-Lehman levels, and the VIX was at its lowest since July 2007. The Greek debt crisis burst into full bloom the week before the memo went out.

## Notable Claims

- Yields at the 2008 trough failed to attract buyers.
- Debt-funded dividend payouts surged in early 2010.
- Record monthly high yield issuance in March 2010.
- Size of the Fed's mortgage-buying program.
- Marks names inadequate risk aversion as the crisis's main cause.

## Quotes

> Risk aversion is absolutely essential in order for markets to function properly.

> “We’re not expecting any surprises” is one of my favorite oxymorons.

> The higher prices are relative to intrinsic value, the more we should allow for the unknown.
