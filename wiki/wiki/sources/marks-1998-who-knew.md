# Who Knew?

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** January 8, 1998 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/1998-01-08-who-knew.pdf)

## Summary

A short memo written as the Asian currency crisis spread. Marks notes that his September 1997 memo had warned the next shock would come from something no one anticipated, and the Asian meltdown arrived the following month. He argues the only safe forecasts are that something significant will happen and that nobody knows what. He lays out the three stages of a bull market, says cycle timing works only for the contrarian, and asks whether investors pricing US stocks near all-time highs are too optimistic. He lists unanswered questions about Asia, deflation and slowing earnings, and concludes that this is not a buying opportunity and that the stock market's best days are behind it for a while.

## Key Ideas

1. The only reliable predictions are that something significant will eventually happen and that no one knows what it will be.
2. Marks will say 'never' to the idea that things go right forever: hopes always get dashed and unpleasant surprises always arise.
3. Every bull market has three stages: a few far-sighted people see improvement, most agree it is underway, then everyone believes things will improve forever.
4. Successful cycle timing requires acting as a contrarian: buy near the bottom, let optimism fade as prices rise, hold few exposed positions at the top.
5. Being optimistic when no one else is can be highly profitable; being optimistic when everyone else is can be very costly.
6. US indices sat near highs despite large unresolved uncertainties (Asian contagion, deflation, earnings disappointments), a combination Marks calls dangerous.
7. Oaktree tries to know where it stands, buy avidly only when fear is high, and use strategies that prepare for lurking negatives.

## Market Context

Written in early January 1998, a few months into the Asian financial crisis. Asian currencies and economies were in free-fall, yet US equity indices were near all-time highs, helped by a flight to quality. Newspapers reported earnings disappointments, restructurings and layoffs, and Greenspan had mentioned deflation risk. The DJIA stood at 7,802 on the memo date.

## Notable Claims

- The Asian meltdown came the month after his September 1997 memo warned of an unanticipated shock.
- Marks judged that the market was not offering a buying opportunity.
- US equity indices had not fallen since September despite the Asian crisis.
- Dow Jones level on the memo date.
- Greenspan had raised the possibility of deflation.

## Quotes

> it's most prudent to be optimistic when no one else is, and it can be highly profitable. But it can be dangerous to be optimistic when everyone else is, and very costly.

> Things will never go right forever.

> Prices near highs and optimism in bloom -- that's a dangerous combination, especially with perceived risk on the rise.
