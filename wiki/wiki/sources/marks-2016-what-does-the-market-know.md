# What Does the Market Know?

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** January 19, 2016 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/what-does-the-market-know.pdf)

## Summary

Written during the January 2016 sell-off triggered by worries about China and falling oil, this memo answers a Bloomberg anchor's question, 'does the market's decline worry you?' Marks argues that 'the market' is just its participants, so its price reflects only their average insight, while their emotions compound into herd behaviour. Active investors who try to buy low must believe the market is sometimes wrong, so it makes no sense to take instruction from it. He illustrates with 2008: the short-selling and CDS feedback loop that drove Morgan Stanley down 82% before it recovered to $33, and senior loans that fell 29% under forced CLO selling and then rose 45% in 2009. Price declines are reflective, not predictive. Selling simply because prices are falling has nothing to do with value, and selling at the lows converts a temporary fluctuation into a permanent loss, which he calls the cardinal sin.

## Key Ideas

1. In the short run the market is a voting machine and a barometer of sentiment, not a fundamental analyst (Graham).
2. There is no 'market' apart from its participants. Price reflects their average insight, and only their emotion is synergistic.
3. Active investing presumes the market is sometimes wrong, so following its signals contradicts the reason for being active.
4. 2008 feedback loops (unrestricted short selling plus CDS spreads) pushed bank stocks far below any reasonable link to intrinsic value.
5. Senior loans fell more than high yield bonds in 2008 (-29% vs -25%) because they were the main home of leverage and forced selling, then rebounded 45% in 2009.
6. Price declines are reflective, not predictive. Following average opinion cannot produce above-average results (second-level thinking).
7. Selling something cheap because you fear a further fall creates 'two-decision' or 'three-decision' problems, and most people forget to buy back.
8. Converting a short-term fluctuation into a permanent loss by selling at the bottom is the cardinal sin of investing.

## Market Context

Mid-January 2016: stocks and oil were falling sharply in the first trading weeks of the year on fears about China's economy and possible spillover to the US. Investors asked Marks whether a 2008-scale crisis was coming. The New York Times reported growing concern that the sell-off signalled a US slowdown, though officials at a lunch Marks attended did not expect a recession that year.

## Notable Claims

- Morgan Stanley fell 82% to under $10 in 2008 and was $33 a year later.
- Senior loan index fell 29% in 2008, more than the 25% fall in high yield.
- Senior loans rose 45% in 2009, leaving a buy-and-hold investor up 3% over two years.
- Non-distressed senior loans had rarely traded below 95 before falling into the 60s.
- Morgan Stanley secured a $9 billion convertible equity injection from Mitsubishi UFJ.

## Quotes

> My bottom line on this subject is that the market price merely reflects the average insight of the market participants.

> There’s only one intelligent form of investing: figure out what something's worth and see if you can buy it at or below that price

> It’s not that it’s always wrong; it’s that there’s no reason to presume it’s right.
