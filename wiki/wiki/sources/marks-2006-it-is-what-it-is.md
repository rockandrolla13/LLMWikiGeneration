# It Is What It Is

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** March 27, 2006 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2006-03-27-it-is-what-it-is.pdf)

## Summary

Marks reads 'it is what it is' as accepting today's givens without accepting the end result, and ties it to the Japanese idea of mujo, the inevitability of change. Cycles are the most dependable thing in investing. Nobody can say how far a trend will go or when it will turn, but an investor can and must know where we stand in the cycle. A 'visitor from Mars' who simply describes current conditions (strong returns, no worry, high multiples and leverage, sold-out hedge fund and private equity conferences) has made no forecast yet gives a clear signal to be cautious. Marks judges 2006 a 'market of no fear' with skinny risk premiums in high yield, real estate and buyouts, driven by a glut of capital chasing alternatives because mainstream assets promise only about 5-7%. Drawing on a lunch with Charlie Munger and Buffett's Gotrocks parable, he argues that the alternative-investment management business is itself inefficient, with bargaining power and fees tilted toward managers.

## Key Ideas

1. Accept the givens and decide well 'given the givens'; mujo means cycles will rise and fall beyond our control, so we must recognise, accept, cope and respond.
2. We may never know where we're going, but we'd better know where we are: judge the economic cycle, recent market performance and, most important, how others are behaving.
3. Observing present conditions requires no forecast; the 'inner Martian' sees complacency, high multiples and easy capital and infers caution.
4. When high returns are not on offer, do not invest as if they are, and do not reach into riskier assets for return when the risk premium is skimpy.
5. High realised returns must not be mistaken for great opportunities to invest more; strong past returns have probably borrowed from the future.
6. A two-column 'Poor Man's Guide to Market Assessment' checklist lets an investor place the market as hot or cold, and lists the mistakes available in each state.
7. A 'bubble expectation' that a field guarantees instant wealth signals an inefficiency: managers or their clients must be making a mistake; in 2006 fee bargaining power sat with alternative managers.
8. Buffett's Gotrocks parable: shuffling assets among owners through 'Helpers' cannot raise total wealth but does transfer wealth to the Helpers through fees.

## Market Context

March 2006, mid-credit-boom. Risk premiums were thin across high yield, real estate and buyouts, buyout multiples and leverage were high, stock buybacks and dividend recaps were adding leverage, and capital was flooding into hedge funds and private equity. Marks had already warned in 'Risk and Return Today' (November 2004).

## Notable Claims

- Mainstream assets offered roughly 5% (high grade bonds) and 5-7% (US stocks) expected returns, pushing capital into alternatives.
- Leading private equity managers could raise funds of $10-15 billion.
- High yield spreads were near historic lows.
- Pension funds and endowments need 8%-or-better returns, tilting fee bargaining power to alternative managers.
- Buffett said he had moved from buying dollars for fifty cents to hunting for dollars at eighty cents.

## Quotes

> We may never know where we’re going, but we’d better have a good idea where we are.

> My bottom line, as they might say in the self-help books: Listen to your inner Martian.

> High past returns don’t imply high future returns; more likely, they’ve borrowed from the future.
