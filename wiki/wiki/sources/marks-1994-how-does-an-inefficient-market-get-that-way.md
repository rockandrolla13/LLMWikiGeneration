# How Does an Inefficient Market Get That Way?

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** July 15, 1994 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/1994-07-15-how-does-an-inefficient-market-get-that-way.pdf)

## Summary

A short client memo arguing that the high yield bond market stayed inefficient a decade after Marks expected it to become efficient. He contrasts efficient markets, where risk alone earns extra return, with inefficient ones, where skill can earn superior risk-adjusted returns. His favoured cause of inefficiency is investor bias: when enough large holders refuse to own an asset at any price, its price can fall below fair value. He uses a Pensions & Investments article about SEI's recommendation of high yield allocations to show that plan sponsors still shunned junk bonds because of the 1989-90 collapse and the Milken/Drexel association. He concludes that investors who look in the rear-view mirror create bargains in assets that performed badly or carry negative bias.

## Key Ideas

1. In an efficient market extra return comes only from extra risk; in an inefficient one, skill can produce consistently superior risk-adjusted returns.
2. Possible causes of inefficiency include obscurity, poor information flow, weak infrastructure and no trade reporting, but Marks's favourite cause is non-objective, biased investors.
3. If enough owners want or must sell and will not buy at any price, the price can fall below fair value and bargains appear; universal enthusiasm does the reverse.
4. High yield bonds still offered a yield premium over Treasuries far larger than expected credit losses, which Marks reads as an excessive risk premium.
5. Plan sponsors' reluctance traced to headline risk: the 1989-90 collapse and the association with Michael Milken and Drexel Burnham Lambert.
6. An asset class that collapsed in the past is more likely to be cheap than one riding a crest of popularity, so rear-view-mirror investing creates opportunity.

## Market Context

Mid-1994, roughly five years after the 1989-90 junk bond collapse and the fall of Drexel Burnham Lambert. Pension consultants (SEI, Callan) were beginning to recommend high yield allocations, but many plan sponsors remained unwilling or barred by investment-grade-only guidelines.

## Notable Claims

- High yield bonds offered 350-400 bp over Treasuries against 50-150 bp a year of credit losses.
- Marks expected the market to become efficient within five years of 1984, but it had not after ten.
- SEI estimated a sponsor could add about 20 bp of return without more risk via a 10% high yield allocation.
- SEI recommended pension plans put 10% to 30% of fixed income in high yield.

## Quotes

> The asset class that collapsed in the past is likely to be cheap, not to be riding a crest of popularity and thus heading for a fall.

> But too many investors drive looking in the rear-view mirror.

> We'd rather buy what has performed badly or is the subject of negative bias and thus is cheap.
