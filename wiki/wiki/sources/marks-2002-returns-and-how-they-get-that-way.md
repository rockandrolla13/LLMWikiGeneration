# Returns and How They Get That Way

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** November 11, 2002 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2002-11-11-returns-and-how-they-get-that-way.pdf)

## Summary

Marks asks where equity returns actually come from after a bubble in which investors assumed prices simply rise. He decomposes price change into earnings growth plus change in the P/E multiple, argues that multiples were still high in 2002 and cannot be counted on to expand, and concludes that long-run returns should track mid-single-digit profit growth. He then turns to active management: mainstream markets are efficient, so dependable outperformance requires inefficient markets and managers with genuine alpha, analysed through the y = alpha + beta x framework. The final third reviews Nassim Taleb's 'Fooled by Randomness', stressing that luck can masquerade as skill, that decisions cannot be judged by outcomes, and that long records of consistent, loss-avoiding performance matter more than occasional brilliance.

## Key Ideas

1. Appreciation needs an underlying process: asset values or cash flows coming to be seen as worth more than the last price paid, not hope that new buyers will pay more.
2. Price change = change in earnings + change in P/E. With P/Es still high, long-run equity returns should be roughly mid-single-digit profit growth.
3. The most dependable superior return comes from buying below intrinsic value and letting price move to fair value, which needs neither uncertain value growth nor irrational overvaluation.
4. Bargains appear in inefficient markets: things not widely known, not understood, or seen as risky, complex, unfashionable, controversial or unseemly. Even these niches fade once crowded.
5. Use y = alpha + beta x to separate what the market provided from what the manager added. A manager who moves 2x the benchmark has leverage, not skill.
6. Alpha managers come from the 'I don't know' school: contrarian, defensive, micro-focused, building records on high batting averages and few losers. They will look terrible in euphoric periods.
7. Following Taleb, judge records against 'alternative histories'. The correctness of a decision cannot be judged from its outcome, and luck often looks like skill.
8. Long records of modest but consistent outperformance and loss avoidance tell more than records lifted by occasional flashes of greatness.

## Market Context

Late 2002, near the end of the post-2000 bear market. Marks notes that even after significant price declines, P/E ratios were still above levels seen at many previous market tops. Index funds had grown over the prior thirty years, and Taleb's 'Fooled by Randomness' was circulating among hedge fund managers.

## Notable Claims

- Buffett's actual phrasing on profits and stock appreciation.
- Marks expects trendline equity appreciation in the mid-single digits.
- It takes 64 years to be statistically sure a manager is skilled, per Marks's Chicago teachers.
- Buffett's coin-flip contest: 215 survivors after 20 days out of 225 million.
- No asset class earns high risk-adjusted returns by birthright.

## Quotes

> The correctness of a decision can't be judged from the outcome.

> The fact that something's improbable doesn't mean it won't happen. And the fact that something happened doesn't mean it wasn't improbable.

> "there are probabilities, and then there are outcomes."
