# Knowledge of the Future

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** April 14, 2020 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/knowledge-of-the-future.pdf)

## Summary

Written in the first weeks of the COVID-19 crisis, this memo borrows epidemiologist Marc Lipsitch's split between facts, informed extrapolation and speculation, and argues that in investing there are almost no facts about the future. Since the US faced the worst pandemic since 1918, the worst contraction since the Depression, a record oil collapse and the largest-ever policy intervention at once, past patterns could not be safely extrapolated. Marks walks through the stages needed to control infections and the trade-off between saving lives and reopening the economy, and predicts a gradual, stop-start reopening. He then questions the Fed's 'limitless' response: buying high yield ETFs and downgraded bonds, widening TALF to CMBS and CLOs, and SEC relief for BDC leverage tests. He argues this creates moral hazard and suppresses the healthy fear of loss that markets need. He notes the S&P 500 had already risen 23% from its March 23 low.

## Key Ideas

1. There are few if any facts about the future; most forecasting is extrapolation or guesswork dressed in formal terms.
2. Extrapolating past patterns is useful only when there is good reason to think they apply; unprecedented events break that link.
3. People hold firm conclusions about an unknowable future, often reflecting their biases (Dunning on 'confident idiots').
4. There is no algorithm for trading lives against economic activity, and reopening is likely to be gradual with setbacks.
5. The 2020 Fed went far past its traditional limits by buying sub-investment-grade debt and expanding TALF collateral.
6. Protecting leveraged investors from losses creates moral hazard: markets work best when participants fear loss.
7. Investors must allow for unlikely and unforeseeable events, like the six-foot man crossing a stream five feet deep on average.

## Market Context

Mid-April 2020, during the COVID-19 lockdowns. Fed funds had been cut to near zero, the Fed and Treasury had announced trillions in stimulus including purchases of investment grade and some high yield credit, oil prices had collapsed, unemployment claims were at records, and the S&P 500 had rallied strongly from its March 23 low.

## Notable Claims

- Fed provided $2.3 trillion in programs (CNBC via Bulletin Intelligence).
- Rate cuts totaling 150 bp took fed funds to near zero.
- S&P 500 up 23% from the March 23 bottom.
- US deaths at 23,000 and claims at 10x the record.
- Markets need fear of loss.

## Quotes

> These days everyone has the same data regarding the present and the same ignorance regarding the future.

> But blind faith in the relevance of past patterns makes no more sense than completely ignoring them.

> I see no reason why financiers should be bailed out simply because the event they’re being harmed by was unpredictable.
