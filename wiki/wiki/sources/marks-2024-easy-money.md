# Easy Money

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** January 9, 2024 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/easy-money.pdf)

## Summary

Prompted by Edward Chancellor's book The Price of Time, Marks extends his 'Sea Change' thesis by cataloguing ten ways that declining and ultra-low interest rates distort behaviour: they stimulate the economy, erase perceived opportunity costs, lift asset prices, push 'handcuff volunteers' into risk and Hayekian 'malinvestment', make weak deals easy to finance, encourage fragile leverage, tempt borrowing short to lend long, create expectations that rates will stay low, transfer wealth from savers to borrowers, and lay the groundwork for the next crisis. He illustrates each with history from Chancellor (South Sea Bubble, Overend Gurney, 1880s Argentina, the 1920s) and recent cases (Argentina's 100-year bond, zombie companies, Theranos, FTX, Signa). He restates the credit cycle as an open-or-shut window, argues today's 5.25-5.50% fed funds rate is normal, not high, and guesses rates will average 2-4% (3.0-3.5% fed funds) rather than 0-2%, which calls for a different asset allocation than in 2009-21.

## Key Ideas

1. Low rates act like an airport moving walkway: easy times flatter businesspeople and investors into confusing brains with a bull market.
2. Cutting the risk-free rate shifts the yield curve and capital market line down and can flatten risk and liquidity premia below what compensates for risk.
3. Ultra-low safe returns create 'handcuff volunteers' and malinvestment; 'the worst of loans are made at the best of times'.
4. Cheap leverage, like ketchup, makes unattractive investments look edible but only amplifies results; PE capital structures did not anticipate a 400-500 bp rise.
5. Low rates subsidise borrowers at savers' expense and widen wealth inequality.
6. Cycles are best seen as excesses and corrections, each step causing the next: rate cuts, lower prospective returns, more risk-taking, losses, fear and tight money, then rate cuts again.
7. Natural rates set by supply and demand allocate capital best; the Fed should stimulate in recessions, cool overheating, and otherwise leave rates alone.
8. Current consensus looks like 'Goldilocks thinking', which raises expectations and the room for disappointment.

## Market Context

January 2024: after the Fed's 2022-23 hiking cycle left fed funds at 5.25-5.50%, markets anticipated a soft landing and multiple 2024 rate cuts (the dot plot showed three); Silicon Valley Bank had failed in March 2023, private equity's outlook had dimmed, Signa had entered insolvency, and Charlie Munger had just died (November 28, 2023).

## Notable Claims

- Argentina's 100-year bonds yielded 7.85% versus 2.77% on 30-year Treasurys.
- Restructured Argentine 100-year bonds recovered roughly 54.5 cents on the dollar.
- The fed funds rate averaged 8.2% in 1969.
- Marks estimates fed funds averaged ~0.5% from 2009 to 2021.
- Marks guesses fed funds will average 3.0-3.5% over the next 5-10 years.

## Quotes

> In short, these were easy times, fueled by easy money.

> "Excesses and corrections" is a much more useful way to think about cycles than "ups and downs."

> Maybe we have a new version of Lord Acton's law: easy money corrupts, and really easy money corrupts absolutely.

— Charlie Munger, from a note to Marks in 2001
