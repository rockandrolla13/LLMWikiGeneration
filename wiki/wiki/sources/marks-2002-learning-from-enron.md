# Learning From Enron

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** March 14, 2002 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2002-03-14-learning-from-enron.pdf)

## Summary

A long post-mortem on Enron's collapse, framed by Ferdinand Pecora's 1939 account of 1920s banking abuses to show that history repeats. Marks walks through the three transaction types Enron misused (off-balance-sheet partnerships, mark-to-market profits from long-dated contracts valued with its own models, and prepaid swaps that were loans in disguise). He argues the problem was people using accounting as a weapon, helped by rule-based rather than principle-based standards. He then assigns responsibility across management culture, option-driven pay, auditors (Arthur Andersen), directors, sell-side analysts and finally shareholders, who bought what they could not understand. He lists proposed reforms without endorsing them and closes with the lesson that numbers with integrity need people with integrity, and that investors need skepticism and a margin for error.

## Key Ideas

1. Enron used legitimate tools (special-purpose entities, mark-to-market accounting, swaps) for purposes they were not designed for, and accounted for them to mislead.
2. Rule-based accounting standards became a roadmap for duplicity; Marks calls for standards set and enforced as principles.
3. Corruption is what happens when exigency meets moral weakness; top management sets the tone and must stress means, not just ends.
4. Stock options focus managers on short-term stock price, give a skewed interest in the company, and carry no income-statement cost, which encourages artful accounting.
5. Auditors and independent directors are essential but fallible, since both rely mostly on information from management.
6. Sell-side analysts gave little warning: most still rated Enron a buy weeks after the SEC probe, while its debt priced in likely default.
7. Responsibility ultimately falls on equity providers who held stock they could not understand while insiders sold in plain sight.
8. Disclosure means presenting facts so their significance can be discerned, not burying them in indecipherable footnotes.

## Market Context

Written in March 2002, months after Enron's late-2001 collapse, with the post-bubble environment still unfolding. Tyco and other companies with accounting issues had seen their stocks collapse, and investors had swung from excessive faith in management in the late 1990s to deep skepticism. Congress was holding hearings and a long list of accounting, auditing, banking and governance reforms was under discussion.

## Notable Claims

- Off-balance-sheet partnerships required outside equity of at least 3% to avoid consolidation.
- Andersen's Enron fees in 2000.
- Most analysts still rated Enron a buy in November 2001.
- Fastow and Kopper profited from the partnerships.
- Oaktree's high yield portfolios hit an unusual cluster of accounting frauds.

## Quotes

> In order to get numbers with integrity, you need people with integrity.

> Accounting is like any other tool; the results will depend on whose hands it's in.
