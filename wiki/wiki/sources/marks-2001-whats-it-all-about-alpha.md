# What's It All About, Alpha?

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** July 11, 2001 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2001-07-11-whats-it-all-about-alpha.pdf)

## Summary

Marks sets out his own working definitions of market efficiency, inefficiency, alpha, beta, risk and correlation, drawing on his Wharton practice training and Chicago theory training. He accepts that mainstream markets are efficient in the sense of speedy, not right: prices reflect the consensus quickly, but the consensus can be badly wrong, as Yahoo's fall from $237 to $11 shows. Inefficient markets do not hand out high returns. They supply mispricings that let skilled investors win at the expense of others, so manager selection matters most there. Alpha is differential skill, knowing what others do not, while beta is symmetric responsiveness to the market. He rejects volatility as the definition of risk, defining it instead as the chance of losing clients' money, which cannot be quantified. Theory should inform decisions but not dominate them.

## Key Ideas

1. Efficient means 'speedy', not 'right': prices reflect the consensus quickly, but the consensus can be wrong, and beating it requires a non-consensus view that is also more correct.
2. Inefficiency is structural and long-lived, not a synonym for 'cheap'; it shows up as often-wrong prices, out-of-line risk-adjusted returns and persistent outperformance by some.
3. Inefficient markets give the raw material of mispricings, so for every winner there is a loser; the dispersion of manager returns is far wider in venture capital and LBOs than in mainstream stocks and bonds.
4. Alpha is differential skill, knowing something others do not; beta is relative volatility, and leverage-driven beta is symmetric and adds nothing unless decisions are right.
5. Diversification and the demand for liquidity are tacit admissions that investors cannot see the future and that alpha is scarce.
6. Risk is the chance of permanent loss, which varies by asset class and investor and resists quantification; volatility was adopted because it is absolute and measurable.
7. Tracking error can be too low; an active manager with near-zero tracking error is not doing what clients hire it for.
8. Theory should inform but not dominate: James Lorie's advice was to index the core and manage the periphery hard.

## Market Context

July 2001, after the dot-com bust (Yahoo had fallen to $11 by April 2001). Marks had written 'Irrational Exuberance' (May 2000) and 'Safety First' (April 2001). Clients were paying growing attention to risk-adjusted returns and tracking error.

## Notable Claims

- Yahoo fell from $237 to $11 between January 2000 and April 2001, so the market was wrong at least once.
- Peter Vermilye held that only the top 10% of analysts add value.
- Oaktree defines risk as the chance of losing clients' money, not volatility.
- In 1970 colleagues expected 12% a year from stocks on a simplistic extrapolation of the 10% historic return.
- Quoting Swensen's data, the interquartile range of ten-year manager returns was 1.2% in US fixed income versus 21.2% in venture capital (table values).

## Quotes

> When I say efficient, I mean it in the sense of "speedy," not "right."

> Alpha isn't knowing something, it's knowing something others don't know.

> It's a concept, a notion, a worry . . . but not a number.
