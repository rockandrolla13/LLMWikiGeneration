# Political Reality Meets Economic Reality

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** January 30, 2019 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/political-reality-meets-economic-reality.pdf)

## Summary

Building on his 2016 memos 'Economic Reality' and 'Political Reality', Marks examines what happens when political promises collide with economic tradeoffs, using two cases. First, the Trump tariffs on China, steel, aluminum and possibly autos: he does not oppose them outright but shows how they are paid largely by domestic consumers, hurt downstream manufacturers, invite retaliation, may push production abroad, and cost far more per job saved than they deliver. Second, and more worrying to him, rising anti-capitalist populism on the left: Sanders, Ocasio-Cortez, Warren's Accountable Capitalism Act and wealth tax, Booker's Worker Dividend Act and a 70% top rate. He argues these proposals would undermine ownership and incentives, defends capitalism as 'the worst economic system . . . except for all the rest', while supporting regulation, safety nets and some higher taxes on top earners. The core lesson is that complex problems have no simple solutions and every economic action has second-order consequences.

## Key Ideas

1. Economics is the study of choice under constraints. Political reality promises everything without tradeoffs or consequences.
2. A bilateral trade deficit is not a loss: both buyer and seller can win, as with paying a barber.
3. Tariffs are mostly paid by domestic consumers and have knock-on effects: higher input costs for downstream manufacturers, offshoring, retaliation and greater uncertainty.
4. Protecting jobs by tariff is costly: the 2011 tire tariff cost about $1 million per job for jobs paying about $40,000.
5. Rising left-wing populism targets capitalism itself, fed by slower growth, globalisation, automation and inequality ('the tide is no longer rising').
6. Mandating worker directors, stakeholder duties or worker payouts tied to buybacks erodes ownership and the incentive to start companies.
7. Capitalism is imperfect but has delivered the most aggregate gains. It should be regulated, with safety nets, not dismantled.
8. Politics mostly redistributes costs and benefits rather than increasing them, so simple political economic prescriptions must not be swallowed whole.

## Market Context

January 2019: an escalating US-China trade war (2018 tariffs of 25% on steel and 10% on aluminum, threatened auto tariffs, IMF warnings about global output), and the start of the 2020 presidential primary cycle with progressive Democrats gaining influence after the 2018 midterms. Warren announced her wealth tax proposal on January 24, 2019.

## Notable Claims

- US trade deficit with China was $335 billion in 2017.
- Obama-era tire tariffs cost about $1 million per job preserved (NYT).
- The US produces only 13% of the raw aluminum it uses.
- Top 5% of taxpayers paid 60% of income taxes in 2015.
- A 2016 Harvard IOP poll found 51% of 18-29 year-olds rejected capitalism.

## Quotes

> In the same way, I’m convinced that capitalism is the worst economic system . . . except for all the rest.

> Thus we shouldn’t take actions – like imposing tariffs – just because they offer potential benefits, without considering their costs.

> Since politics is largely about how costs and benefits are distributed – rather than about increasing aggregate benefits – politicians’ simplistic economic prescriptions mustn’t be swallowed whole.
