# The Pendulum in International Affairs

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** March 23, 2022 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/the-pendulum-in-intl-affairs.pdf)

## Summary

Written a month after Russia invaded Ukraine, this memo applies Marks's pendulum metaphor beyond markets to geopolitics and globalisation. Prompted by a Brookfield board discussion, he links two seemingly separate issues: Europe's heavy dependence on Russian energy, which made the most effective sanction (an energy embargo) too costly to impose, and decades of US offshoring of manufacturing, especially semiconductors, now exposed by pandemic supply-chain disruptions and chip shortages. He traces how cheap foreign labour, shrinking transport and communication costs and a long period of relative peace swung the pendulum toward globalisation and interdependence, delivering low inflation (durables prices fell almost 40% over 1995-2020) but hollowing out US manufacturing jobs. Both cases show reliance on others for essential goods, chosen for being cheapest, easiest or greenest while security was ignored. He expects the pendulum to swing back toward local, more secure sourcing, creating investment opportunities in the transition.

## Key Ideas

1. The pendulum is the best metaphor for anything driven by psychology, because opinion spends little time at the happy medium -- this applies to geopolitics, not just markets.
2. Europe's energy dependence on Russia (one-third of oil, 45% of imported gas, nearly half of coal) made the strongest sanction the hardest to use.
3. Germany's retreat from nuclear power and domestic gas production, driven by climate goals, ignored the security dimension of energy policy.
4. Offshoring lowered costs and US inflation (PCE 1.8% a year 1995-2020, durables prices down almost 40%) but cost millions of US manufacturing jobs.
5. Semiconductor production shifted from the US and Europe (over 80% in 1990) to Taiwan and South Korea (US/Europe ~20% by 2020), creating a national-security vulnerability.
6. Globalisation's swing was fed by a shrinking world, relative peace after the Cold War, and short memories.
7. Economics (and geopolitics) is the science of choice: few options are all positive; negatives show up only when the tide goes out.
8. The pendulum is swinging toward the safest and surest supply, which may create investment opportunities in onshoring.

## Market Context

March 2022: one month into Russia's invasion of Ukraine, Western sanctions exempting energy, Europe facing energy supply risk, US inflation blamed partly on pandemic-era supply-chain disruptions, a global chip shortage (22-week lead times, 7.7 million fewer cars in 2021), and companies announcing US semiconductor foundries.

## Notable Claims

- Russia supplies about one-third of Europe's oil, 45% of its imported gas and nearly half its coal.
- Russia's top four (energy) exports were $223 billion, 55% of its $407 billion total exports in 2019.
- Durables prices fell almost 40% over 1995-2020 while overall PCE inflation was 1.8% a year.
- US and Europe share of semiconductor production fell from over 80% in 1990 to about 20% in 2020.
- Germany cut nuclear's share of electricity from 30% to 11% by 2020 (Shellenberger, quoted).

## Quotes

> Choosing to count on a hostile neighbor for essential goods is like building a bank vault and contracting with the mob to supply it with guards.

> This absence of perfect, permanent solutions is characteristic of pendulums – it’s why they swing.

> In complex fields like economics and geopolitics, there are few easy decisions – just choices, many of them very difficult.
