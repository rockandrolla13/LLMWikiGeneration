# Dare to Be Great II

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** April 8, 2014 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2014-04-08-dare-to-be-great-ii.pdf)

## Summary

Marks revisits his 2006 memo 'Dare to Be Great' on how institutions can pursue superior results. The real question is not whether you dare to be great but whether you dare to be different, to be wrong, and above all to look wrong. Outperformance requires a portfolio unlike others', which means 'uncomfortably idiosyncratic' positions (Swensen) and accepting the risk of being different and worse. Almost every tactic is a two-edged sword; only skill (alpha) is not, and the goal is asymmetry, not zero losses. Institutional agents face asymmetric career payoffs that push them to a rule of never buying so much that failure would look bad, which leads to over-diversification. Because being right often takes time, investors also need the staying power to look wrong for a while.

## Key Ideas

1. You cannot take the same actions as everyone else and expect to outperform; the first prerequisite for top-decile results is a portfolio different from others'.
2. 2x2 matrix: conventional behaviour gives average results, good or bad; only unconventional behaviour combined with superior judgment gives above-average results.
3. Most great investments begin in discomfort; distressed debt has been cheap for Oaktree precisely because no one reveres a distressed company.
4. Every tactic (timing, aggressiveness, concentration, leverage) is symmetric; only alpha is not. The goal is asymmetry, not avoiding all losses.
5. Insisting on zero losses guarantees against gains too: Oaktree's high yield default rate averaged about a third of the universe's, but zero defaults would mean taking too little risk.
6. Agents' career payoffs are asymmetric, so many institutions follow the rule 'never buy so much that if it fails we look bad', which by definition caps upside and leads to over-diversification.
7. Looking right is harder than being right: correct decisions may fail through randomness, and timing is unpredictable ('being too far ahead of your time is indistinguishable from being wrong').
8. Everything important in investing is counterintuitive; unconventional behaviour is the only road to superior results, but it requires temperament and circumstances that tolerate looking wrong.

## Market Context

April 2014, a period the memo does not describe in market terms. It references a big West Coast bond manager then in the news (unnamed) causing headline-risk concerns for asset owners, per Pensions & Investments of March 17.

## Notable Claims

- Oaktree's high yield default rate has averaged one-third of the universe rate.
- Marks and Bruce Karsh organised their first distressed debt fund in 1988.
- A sovereign wealth fund allocated close to a billion dollars to Oaktree over 15 years, only a few tenths of a percent of its assets.
- Greenspan's 'irrational exuberance' warning in December 1996 preceded more than three more years of gains.

## Quotes

> Most great investments begin in discomfort.

> This is really the bottom-line: not whether you dare to be different or to be wrong, but whether you dare to look wrong.

> I’m convinced that everything that’s important in investing is counterintuitive, and everything that’s obvious is wrong.
