# The New Paradigm

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** October 19, 2006 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2006-10-19-the-new-paradigm.pdf)

## Summary

Marks argues that a new paradigm had taken hold in investing, and not for the better. After the 2000-02 equity losses, institutions expected only 5-7% from stocks, so pension funds and endowments needing 8% poured money into small alternative markets. That stretch for return wiped out risk aversion, lowered demanded risk premiums, reduced skepticism and let quantity replace quality. He examines buyouts (high prices, heavy competition, IRRs boosted by dividend recaps), real estate (lending driven by appraisers and over-100% financing), structured credit (CDOs and CLOs built by structurers and rating agencies rather than credit analysts), and hedge funds writing cat insurance, credit default swaps and second-lien loans. He blames a fee model that lets managers get rich on asset size, and concludes that it is time for caution and risk control.

## Key Ideas

1. Low expected equity returns pushed institutions into alternatives, and the stretch for return overcame their innate caution.
2. Large inflows into small markets created a virtuous cycle of appreciation and easy refinancing that made risk look low.
3. Buyout funds face high entry multiples and heavy competition, so they lean on financial engineering and leverage rather than buying cheap.
4. Leverage raises both the chance of getting into a jam and the damage the jam does.
5. In real estate and structured credit, the credit spigot is controlled by appraisers, structurers and rating agencies who have no capital at risk; financial engineering is displacing credit analysis.
6. Hedge funds writing CDS and catastrophe insurance book premiums now and face defaults later, so crowded selling drives premiums below the risk.
7. The new manager paradigm: raise a lot of money, earn acceptable returns, avoid risks that would preclude an encore, and invest fast to raise the next fund.
8. When risk aversion is at cyclical lows, risk premiums need not be generous, so the prescription is caution and risk control.

## Market Context

Written in October 2006 during the pre-crisis credit boom. Credit markets were sanguine, structured credit (CDOs, CBOs, CLOs) was proliferating, buyout funds were raising record funds (KKR at $15 billion), lenders financed dividend recaps and real estate loans above 100% of cost, and hedge funds were moving into CDS writing, catastrophe insurance, movies and second-lien loans.

## Notable Claims

- Expected US stock returns had fallen to 5-7%.
- Relative size of alternative and mainstream markets per JPMorgan.
- S&P 500 P/E versus the start of the LBO era.
- Over a third of full-cycle CLOs lost money for equity holders.
- Management fees alone can make a large buyout fund's managers rich.

## Quotes

> “Financial engineering is the science of structuring cash flows; credit analysis is the art of getting paid.”

> But, simply put, when you increase leverage, the probability of getting into a jam increases and the consequences of that jam worsen.

> Too many people trying to dine at the buffet simultaneously can lead to a disorderly process and skimpy portions.
