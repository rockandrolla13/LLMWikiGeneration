# bubble.com

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** January 2, 2000 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2000-01-02-bubble.pdf)

## Summary

Writing at the very start of 2000, Marks compares the technology, Internet and telecom boom with the 1720 South Sea Bubble as told in Edward Chancellor's 'Devil Take the Hindmost'. He accepts that the technologies will change the world but argues that changing the world is different from making money, citing RCA after 1929 and the zero cumulative profit of airlines. He examines venture capital inflows, the IPO 'mania-within-a-mania', free-product business models, weak barriers to entry and extreme valuations such as Yahoo!, Akamai and VA Linux. His lessons: genuine positives can still lose money if you overpay, overpricing can persist, a top comes when the last holdout buys, and eventually valuation has to matter.

## Key Ideas

1. Speculative manias repeat: South Sea Bubble features (greater fool buying, circular valuation, everyone quitting work to speculate) reappeared in the dot-com era.
2. Being right that an industry will change the world does not make its stocks good investments; RCA and airlines are cautionary examples.
3. No investment is so good that the wrong balance of supply and demand cannot ruin it; too much money for too few ideas means overpaying.
4. The big, low-risk profits come when results have been poor, capital is scarce and everyone says 'no way'.
5. Dot-com profitability is doubtful: free products give zero marginal revenue, barriers to entry are low, and incumbents will fight back.
6. In the valuation vacuum a 'lottery ticket mentality' and the fear of missing out drive buying.
7. 'Prices are too high' does not mean 'the next move is down', but eventually valuation has to matter.

## Market Context

Written January 2, 2000, at the height of the technology, Internet and telecom boom. Nasdaq p/e was reported above 170, 1999 IPOs averaged about 160% above issue price, VA Linux rose 698% on its first day, and MBA students and buyout professionals were leaving for dot-coms and venture capital. Marks admits his recent caution on the stock market had been wrong.

## Notable Claims

- Newton lost £20,000 in the South Sea Bubble.
- RCA fell from $114 to $2½ after the Great Crash.
- Airline industry cumulative profit through 1992 was zero.
- Venture capital commitments tripled from $7.8 billion (1994) to $26.1 billion (1998).
- Yahoo! worth more than GM and Ford with a p/e above 1,000.

## Quotes

> Eventually, though, valuation has to matter.

> while the market is usually driven by fear and greed, sometimes the strongest motivator is the fear of missing out.

> it's one thing to innovate and change the world and another thing entirely to make money.
