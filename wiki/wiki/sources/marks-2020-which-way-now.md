# Which Way Now?

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** March 31, 2020 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/which-way-now.pdf)

## Summary

Written at the end of March 2020, after the fastest market meltdown in history and an equally violent rebound, this memo catalogues the optimistic and pessimistic cases for the Covid-19 crisis. The positive case assumes the virus is controlled within about three months, a V-shaped recovery, government 'life support' during an economic 'coma', healthier banks than in 2008, and ample dry powder. Marks admits to being more worrier than dreamer and gives a longer negative case. It covers poor US testing and quarantine, a record economic contraction (3.3 million jobless claims in a week), the conflict between isolation and reopening, and fixed costs against vanishing revenues. It adds highly leveraged companies and leveraged investment vehicles facing defaults, haircuts and forced selling without bailouts, and an oil collapse to $19. He describes the CARES Act and Fed programs as stabilisation rather than stimulus. He asks whether replacing incomes is enough when the economy must still produce goods, and notes that Modern Monetary Theory has arrived de facto, with implications for the dollar and inflation. He judges assets fairly priced for the optimistic case but not for worsening news, expects declines, and says the most important thing is to be ready to take advantage of them.

## Key Ideas

1. Markets swung from panic to FOMO within two weeks, showing how people treat long-term phenomena as if they can change in the short run.
2. With unprecedented uncertainty and no valid historical analogies, investors should lay out optimistic and pessimistic scenarios rather than assume one outcome.
3. The bull case rests on quick virus control, a V-shaped recovery, government life support during the economic 'coma', healthier banks and abundant dry powder.
4. The bear case: weak US public-health response, record contraction, an unresolved trade-off between isolation and reopening, and fixed costs exceeding collapsing revenues.
5. Corporate leverage and leveraged investment entities built in the low-rate era increase vulnerability: rising defaults, lender haircuts, margin calls and forced selling, with bailouts unlikely for non-bank vehicles.
6. Government can replace lost wages and revenues but cannot replace lost output; prolonged inactivity may impair productive capacity, like a shut-in oil reservoir.
7. Massive deficits and Fed money creation mean Modern Monetary Theory has arrived de facto, raising questions about the dollar's reserve status and inflation.
8. Assets looked priced for the optimistic case; more important than adding defensiveness is being ready to take advantage of declines.

## Market Context

End of March 2020, early in the Covid-19 pandemic. US cases were rising fast with limited testing, and lockdowns were shutting businesses. The S&P 500 had just had its fastest-ever crash and then its best three days since the 1930s. Investment grade issuance hit records after Fed and Treasury intervention, the CARES Act had passed, oil had collapsed amid a Saudi-Russia price war, and a deep recession was expected.

## Notable Claims

- The S&P 500 fell 33.9% from February 19 to March 23, 2020, then gained 17.5% in three days.
- 49 companies issued $107 billion of IG bonds in one week, a record.
- 3.3 million new unemployment claims in one week versus a prior record of 695,000.
- Oil fell from $61 at year-end to $19.
- The CARES Act provided roughly $2 trillion of rescue and support.

## Quotes

> I always say we have to be aware of and open about our biases. I admit to mine: I’m more of a worrier than a dreamer.

> So replacing lost wages and revenues will not be enough for long: the economy has to produce goods and services.

> But the most important thing is to be ready to respond to and take advantage of declines.
