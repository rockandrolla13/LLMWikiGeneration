# On Regulation

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** March 2, 2011 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2011-03-02-on-regulation.pdf)

## Summary

Marks argues that attitudes to financial regulation swing like a pendulum because neither free markets nor regulation works perfectly. He traces the swing from the lightly regulated 1920s, through the 1930s laws (Securities Act, Securities Exchange Act, Glass-Steagall), to the deregulation that began in the late 1970s and culminated in the repeal of Glass-Steagall, the exempting of derivatives and higher investment-bank leverage before the 2007-08 crisis. Free markets need regulation because self-interest leads to excessive risk, but rules are reactive, easy to evade and written by regulators who cannot foresee the next problem. He suggests that compromises between the two can be most dangerous, citing Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, rating agencies granted official standing, and moral hazard at too-big-to-fail banks. He finds post-crisis US regulation surprisingly mild, and shows how the Volcker Rule, executive hedging of stock grants and Barclays' reclassification of its US unit illustrate a 'balloon' effect where self-interest pops out elsewhere. He concludes the pendulum will keep swinging.

## Key Ideas

1. Regulatory attitudes follow a pendulum: crashes bring regulation, calm brings deregulation, then risk-taking and misdeeds return.
2. Free markets allocate resources well on average, but self-interest and miscalculation create intolerable fluctuations that regulation must modulate.
3. Rules are reactive and specific, so they are circumvented; principle-based regulation needs judgment and foresight that regulators rarely have or are allowed.
4. Hybrids of free markets and regulation can be the most dangerous: Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac took vast risk under a tacit federal guarantee.
5. Putting rating agencies in charge of investing standards gave commercial firms official power and turned triple-A into a meaningless label.
6. Moral hazard at too-big-to-fail institutions means a free-market system may work 95% of the time but carry terrible risk in the remainder.
7. Regulation acts like a balloon: pushing in one place (Volcker Rule, capital rules) makes self-interested behaviour pop out in another.
8. Attitudes to regulation are largely inherited from upbringing and circumstances, not reached through analysis.

## Market Context

March 2011, about 3½ years after the financial crisis surfaced in July 2007. The Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission had just released its report, the Dodd-Frank reform law with its Volcker Rule had passed the year before, and Democrats controlled the White House and Congress. Marks had just finished his book The Most Important Thing, due in late April.

## Notable Claims

- The deregulation list preceding the 2007-08 crisis.
- The 1929 crash led to unemployment of 25%.
- Moral hazard undermines a free-market mechanism in the tail.
- Highly regulated banks suffered most while unregulated hedge funds were relatively unscathed.
- Barclays deregistered its US subsidiary as a bank holding company to escape Federal Reserve capital requirements.

## Quotes

> Bottom line: the financial system can’t be entrusted to untrammeled free markets.

> The conclusion is inescapable: responsibility for the safety of the financial system can’t be delegated to regulators.

> Regulations are limited in their scope and effect, and like a balloon, when you push in one place, self-interested behavior pops out in another.
