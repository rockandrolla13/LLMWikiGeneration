# A Case in Point

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** June 6, 2005 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2005-06-06-a-case-in-point.pdf)

## Summary

A follow-up to 'There They Go Again' that uses convertible arbitrage as a case study in repeated investor mistakes. Marks explains how convert arb works (buy the convertible, short a hedge-ratio share of the stock), credits Ed Thorp with turning it from art to science, and shows its strong 1995-2003 record on the CSFB/Tremont index. He then draws four rules from its 2004-05 collapse: more money chasing a strategy lowers its returns; nothing is a sure thing, as the GM trade showed when Kerkorian's bid lifted the stock and S&P's downgrade cut the bonds a day apart; crowding into the same trades raises costs of entry and exit; and a virtuous cycle of inflows and rising prices turns vicious when withdrawals force selling. He notes convertibles were becoming cheap again and that Oaktree was considering adding to them.

## Key Ideas

1. Convertible arbitrage buys a convertible and shorts less than the full conversion share count, using a hedge ratio that depends on estimated stock volatility.
2. Rule one: the return available from a strategy depends on how much money pursues it; more money means lower returns.
3. Rule two: widespread belief that something cannot miss is the 'kiss of death' for its profitability.
4. The 2005 GM trade lost on both legs: Kerkorian's May 4 bid raised the shorted stock, and S&P's next-day downgrade lowered the bonds held long.
5. Rule three: quantitative funds screening the same universes pile into the same trades and face illiquidity together when unwinding.
6. Many hedge fund strategies are 'piggybacking' on underlying markets; when arbitrageurs outnumber long-only buyers, they change the prices they exploit.
7. Rule four: a virtuous cycle of inflows and rising prices turns vicious as withdrawals force selling and deepen losses.
8. Loss of confidence creates bargains: Marks says convertibles were getting cheap again by mid-2005.

## Market Context

June 2005. Convertible arbitrage, a hedge fund staple of the prior decade, had just suffered its first run of four (then five) consecutive down months as capital fled. GM and GMAC had been downgraded, and short rates had risen more than 200 basis points while intermediate and long rates fell.

## Notable Claims

- The CSFB/Tremont convertible arbitrage index returned 12.8% a year over 1995-2003 with one down year.
- Arbitrageurs were thought to account for up to 80% of convertible buying in 2004.
- The index fell to 2.0% in 2004 and -5.8% in the first four months of 2005.
- Kerkorian's bid moved GM stock from about $28 to $32.
- Convert arb withdrawals were $1.7bn in Q4 2004 and $1.8bn in Q1 2005.

## Quotes

> More simply put: everything else being equal, more money means lower returns.

> If there’s a “kiss of death” in the investment world, it’s widespread belief that something can’t miss.

> Excessive confidence sets the stage for disappointment, and the loss of confidence creates bargains.
