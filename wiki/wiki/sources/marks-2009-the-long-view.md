# The Long View

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** January 9, 2009 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2009-01-09-the-long-view.pdf)

## Summary

In the depths of the 2008 crisis Marks admits that, although he read the short-term 2003-07 cycle correctly, he missed that it sat at the top of a much longer cycle. He argues there are cycles in the long-term trend itself, not just short cycles around it, and that the post-World War II decades were the positive phase of such a cycle. That phase was driven by an 'era of increasing willingness' -- investors growing ever more willing to trust stocks, forgo liquidity, accept complexity and take risk at shrinking premiums -- and by rising 'expansiveness', his word for leverage across consumers, homeowners, corporations, financial institutions, governments and investors. He traces the changing status of equities, parallels with the 1920s (uptick rule, Glass-Steagall, bucket shops), Madoff as a classic top-of-cycle swindle, and warns that the recent decades were the best of times, not a norm to which markets will return.

## Key Ideas

1. Two concepts investors must master: value and cycles; values themselves move with the economic cycle.
2. There are cycles in the long-term trend, not just short-term cycles around it; 2003-07 was different in kind, not just degree.
3. The 'era of increasing willingness': decades of rising trust, reduced caution and acceptance of more risk at smaller risk premiums.
4. 'Expansiveness' (leverage) grew at every level of the economy, often through products 'with the leverage inside' rather than explicit borrowing.
5. Leverage is 'money of the mind': it depends on the lender's mood, and corporate debt is rolled over rather than repaid, so solvency depends on credit staying open.
6. Equities are structurally inferior to debt; their higher expected return is compensation, so their attractiveness depends on price.
7. Practices move from exotic to mainstream to overdone: what the wise man does in the beginning, the fool does in the end.
8. Superior investors make more in good times than they give back in bad; high tide hides who is swimming without a bathing suit.

## Market Context

January 2009, after the Lehman collapse and the 2008 credit crisis: S&P 500 down 38.5% in 2008, disappearance of all major US non-bank investment banks, about $1 trillion of bank write-offs, $7.8 trillion committed to US recovery programmes, the Madoff fraud exposed, closed capital markets (no sub-investment-grade bond issued in November 2008), record equity fund outflows and rising unemployment.

## Notable Claims

- Consumer credit grew 260-fold from 1947 to 2008, from 4.2% to 17.9% of GDP.
- Bank indebtedness rose from 21% of GDP in 1980 to 116% in 2007 (quoting Vanity Fair).
- Federal debt grew from $1 trillion in 1980 to $11 trillion.
- The S&P 500 fell 38.5% in 2008, the biggest drop since 1931.
- From 1975 to 1999 the S&P 500 never lost more than 7.5% in a year; 16 of 25 years exceeded 15%.

## Quotes

> In my opinion, there are two key concepts that investors must master: value and cycles

> leverage is purely a function of the lender’s mood.

> Buoyant, decades-long up-trends and their explosive endings are the inevitable results of the tendency of human nature to go to extremes.
