# Volatility Decomposition and Estimation in Time-Changed Price Models

## Summary

The paper asks whether splitting spot volatility, estimated in a time-changed (transaction-time) price model, into tick-time volatility (variance per trade) and trading intensity (rate of trade arrivals) gives more insight and a better estimator than the classical clock-time diffusion approach. It models trades as a point process, with returns driven by a Brownian motion sampled at trade arrival times, plus additive microstructure noise on observed prices.

Using pre-averaging (from Podolskij and Vetter, extended by Jacod et al.) adapted to the transaction-time setting, and a kernel estimator for the point-process intensity, the authors build separate estimators for tick-time volatility and trading intensity, then multiply them to obtain an alternative clock-time volatility estimator. They compare this construction against the standard pre-averaging clock-time estimator on NASDAQ transaction data for four stocks (MSFT, GM, HON, NKE) traded on April 1, 2014, and derive asymptotic properties of both estimators using an infill-asymptotic (time-rescaling) argument.

Empirically, the tick-time volatility curve is usually much smoother than both the clock-time volatility curve and the trading-intensity curve, especially for the more liquid stocks, so most of the fluctuation in clock-time volatility traces back to fluctuations in trading intensity, including the well-known intraday U-shape. Because microstructure noise only contaminates the smoother tick-time volatility curve and not the intensity curve, a larger averaging window can be used, giving the decomposition-based estimator a faster rate of convergence than the classical pre-averaging estimator in several smoothness regimes, sometimes even beating the known minimax lower bound for the standard diffusion model.

What is new is the volatility decomposition itself as an estimation device: rather than estimating clock-time volatility directly, the paper shows it factors exactly into tick-time volatility times intensity in the transaction-time model, both curves are separately identifiable from data, and this factorization can be exploited both for interpretation (attributing volatility changes to company news versus general trading-intensity shifts) and for improved estimation under microstructure noise.

## Clock and Sampling

**Time change: a stochastic clock used as a modelling device.**

See [[concepts/stochastic-time-change|Stochastic Time Change]].

Observations arrive at transaction (tick) times generated by a point process with intensity lambda_t; log-price increments between consecutive trades are driven by a Brownian motion subordinated to this point process, plus additive i.i.d. microstructure noise on the observed price. Clock-time spot volatility is shown to equal tick-time volatility (variance per trade) multiplied by trading intensity (trades per unit calendar time), and both curves are estimated locally via kernel/pre-averaging smoothers rather than through a single fixed prediction horizon.

## Data

- **Asset class:** Equities
- **Instruments:** MSFT (Microsoft), GM (General Motors), HON (Honeywell), NKE (Nike); a simulation study also reuses transaction arrival times from CSCO
- **Venue:** NASDAQ stock exchange
- **Period:** One trading day, April 1, 2014 (09:30-16:00), for the empirical illustration; underlying data supplied by QuantQuote TickView
- **Granularity:** Individual transactions (tick-by-tick), roughly 4,000 to 30,000 transactions per stock per day; each trading day is treated as spanning 23,400 seconds

## Features and Measures

- **tick-time volatility.** The instantaneous variance of price changes measured per transaction rather than per unit of calendar time; estimated with a pre-averaging technique adapted to the transaction-time model to remain robust to microstructure noise.
- **trading intensity.** The instantaneous rate of transaction arrivals, modelled as the intensity of a point process and estimated with a kernel smoother.
- **decomposition-based clock-time volatility estimator.** The product of the tick-time volatility estimate and the trading-intensity estimate, proposed as an alternative to directly estimating clock-time volatility with a classical pre-averaging estimator.

## Method

The authors work in a time-changed Brownian motion model where log prices move only at transaction times, with tick-time volatility and intensity treated as (in the base case) deterministic curves, and additive i.i.d. noise contaminating observed prices. They estimate intensity with a kernel estimator and tick-time volatility with a pre-averaging estimator (blocks of averaged returns, then a local sum of squares), and multiply the two to form the clock-time estimator; asymptotic normality and rates of convergence are derived using an infill-asymptotics approach that rescales time as the sample span grows.

Performance is judged by asymptotic rate of convergence and asymptotic variance, compared case by case (according to the smoothness/Holder exponents of the tick-time volatility and intensity curves) against the classical pre-averaging clock-time volatility estimator from the standard diffusion model; a numerical illustration on the four NASDAQ stocks and a simulation study (constant versus oscillating true tick-time volatility) supplement the theory.

## Results

- In the four-stock NASDAQ illustration (MSFT, GM, HON, NKE on April 1, 2014), the tick-time volatility curve is generally smoother than the clock-time volatility curve and the trading-intensity curve.
- For the more liquid stocks MSFT and GM, most of the fluctuation seen in clock-time volatility over the day traces to fluctuation in trading intensity rather than tick-time volatility.
- The intraday U-shape typically seen in clock-time volatility is mainly a feature of trading intensity, not tick-time volatility, in this sample.
- Because microstructure noise contaminates only the smoother tick-time volatility curve, the decomposition-based estimator can use a larger effective averaging window, giving it a faster rate of convergence than the classical pre-averaging clock-time estimator in several of the smoothness cases the authors examine.
- In another subset of smoothness cases, both estimators share the same convergence rate, but the decomposition-based estimator has smaller asymptotic variance when trading intensity is below 1, and larger variance otherwise.
- In a further subset of cases, the comparison between the two estimators is left unresolved because the bias of the classical pre-averaging estimator cannot be derived explicitly there.
- A simulation using real transaction arrival times from CSCO on April 1, 2014, with an imposed constant or oscillating tick-time volatility, supports that the pre-averaging tick-time estimator tracks the true (smoother) curve well.

## Limitations

- Formal asymptotic results are derived only for the case of deterministic tick-time volatility and intensity curves; the more general stochastic-parameter case is left largely unresolved.
- The empirical illustration covers a single trading day (April 1, 2014) and four NASDAQ stocks.
- The authors state that a data-adaptive way to choose the smoothing parameters (bandwidths and block sizes) remains to be solved.
- Reader note: no out-of-sample, multi-day, or cross-market validation of the empirical U-shape and smoothness findings is reported.
