# What's Your Game Plan?

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** September 5, 2003 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2003-09-05-whats-your-game-plan.pdf)

## Summary

Marks uses a string of sports metaphors to explain Oaktree's defensive philosophy. Most investment careers end not for lack of winners but because of too many losers, so Oaktree aims for batting average rather than home runs: 'if we avoid the losers, the winners will take care of themselves.' He credits Charles Ellis's 1975 'The Loser's Game', built on Simon Ramo's tennis analysis, as a source of this loss-avoidance view, while differing from Ellis's conclusion in favour of passive investing. Investing is like soccer rather than American football: no whistle tells you when to switch from offense to defense, so an investor must pick a lineup and stick with it. Oaktree chooses to keep up in good times and outperform in bad times. He also stresses 'playing within yourself' and taking what the market gives, and ends with Buffett's point that investors never have to swing at a pitch.

## Key Ideas

1. Investment careers end because managers strike out too often, not because they hit too few home runs; concentration, overtrading and positioning for hoped-for outcomes cause the strikeouts.
2. Loss avoidance as foundation: Oaktree diversifies, limits fundamental risk, seeks downside protection and emphasises senior securities.
3. Ellis's 'loser's game' framing (from Ramo's amateur tennis) applies to investing, but Oaktree rejects Ellis's passive conclusion because it believes some markets are inefficient.
4. Investing is like soccer: there is no whistle to switch between offense and defense, so investors must commit to an aggressive, defensive or balanced approach in advance.
5. Oaktree's defensive aim is to keep up (or lag slightly) in good times and outperform in bad times, giving above-average full-cycle results with below-average volatility.
6. Longevity and consistency (Aaron, Ripken, Sampras) beat peak seasons; 'his worst moments were almost as good as his best' describes a terrific money-management career.
7. Play within yourself and take what the market gives: after a 64% first-year IRR in a distressed fund, forcing a repeat would only give back profits.
8. Buffett's 'no called strikes' point: investors can wait patiently for the pitch they like.

## Market Context

September 2003, after the 2000-02 bear market and a period of very high distressed-debt returns (Oaktree's newest distressed fund had a 64% first-year net IRR). Marks frames the question as what to do when opportunities are limited after exceptional gains.

## Notable Claims

- Oaktree's newest distressed debt fund made a 64% net IRR in its first year.
- None of Oaktree's prominent early distressed-debt competitors remained a leader.
- Oaktree's motto is that avoiding losers lets winners take care of themselves.
- Ellis's 'The Loser's Game' dates from 1975.

## Quotes

> “The key to investment success isn’t hitting home runs; it’s avoiding strikeouts and inning-ending double plays.”

> We, too, try to win by not losing.

> If it’s not there, hoping won’t make it so.
