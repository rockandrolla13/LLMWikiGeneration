# What’s Going on in Private Credit?

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** April 9, 2026 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/oaktreecaplibraries/memos/whats-going-on-in-private-credit.pdf)

## Summary

Marks traces the evolution of credit from the acceptance of non-investment-grade debt in the 1970s through LBOs, syndicated loans, CLOs, subprime RMBS, post-GFC direct lending and, most recently, the sale of direct lending vehicles to individual and retirement investors. He lays out the normal pattern of investment bubbles (newness, a grain of truth, early success, envy, overpaying, disillusionment) and argues direct lending followed much of it, with roughly $2 trillion lent in 15 years and many untested managers lowering standards. The tide began to go out in 2025-26: First Brands and Tricolor bankruptcies, redemption limits in non-traded BDCs, NAV doubts, and in February 2026 a sharp repricing of heavily represented software debt after AI coding advances (including Anthropic releases) raised disruption fears. He draws lessons from Sorkin's book on 1929 (unsuitable sales, heavy leverage, liquidity mismatch), describes Oaktree's limited direct lending and software exposure, and explains how the end of ultra-low rates ('Sea Change') is squeezing private equity and thus the loans that financed it. He expects direct lending may need a full credit cycle but ultimately to be fine.

## Key Ideas

1. Credit innovation over five decades (HY bonds, LBOs, syndicated loans, securitisation, CLOs, direct lending, retail vehicles) transformed investing; private credit and direct lending are not synonyms -- direct lending is a subset.
2. Bubble anatomy: newness, a grain of truth, rewarding early entry, envy, possibility turned into certainty, neglect of the safe price, latecomers lowering standards, then disillusionment.
3. Low mark-to-market volatility in private loans gave high Sharpe ratios, but risk is not volatility; direct loans carry no less credit risk than liquid credit.
4. The limiting factor in credit is lenders' willingness to supply capital, not borrowers' appetite for it.
5. Software debt is heavily represented in direct lending (20-30%) versus HY bonds (4-5%), often at ~20x EBITDA purchase multiples; AI coding advances triggered a sentiment- and flow-driven repricing in early 2026.
6. 1929 lesson: unsuitable sales to the public, heavy leverage and illiquid assets financed with short-term loans can ruin investors; the parallel is retail access to levered, semi-liquid direct lending vehicles.
7. The 40-year, ~2,000bp fall in rates gave private equity a double tailwind; the 2022 rate rise squeezed coverage ratios, exits, distributions and returns, which will feed through to buyout lenders.
8. Keep a healthy balance between belief and doubt; discipline in hot markets costs AUM but positions a manager for better opportunities after the correction.

## Market Context

April 2026: private credit under scrutiny after the mid-2025 First Brands and Tricolor bankruptcies (with fraud concerns), redemption limits in non-traded BDCs and widening discounts on traded BDCs, and a February 2026 selloff in software debt after AI coding tools (an Anthropic coding model in November 2025 and 11 plug-ins in late January 2026) raised disruption fears; private equity still squeezed by the post-2022 rate rise; a war in the Middle East stoking recession fears.

## Notable Claims

- About $2 trillion of direct loans were made in the last 15 years; all private credit was about $150 billion 20 years ago.
- Software debt is roughly 4-5% of HY bonds, 10-15% of broadly syndicated loans and 20-30% of direct lending.
- Direct lending is about 20% of Oaktree's performing credit and under 15% of total AUM; Oaktree has just over $10bn in public direct lending vehicles vs $40-50bn+ for leaders.
- MSCI estimated US private equity funds returned 5.8% annualised from 2022 to Q3 2025 vs 11.6% for the S&P 500 (quoted via Claude).
- Marks's bank loan rate was 22¼% in 1980 versus 2¼% borrowing in 2020.

## Quotes

> In my experience, the limiting factor in the credit markets is never borrowers’ appetite for capital, but rather lenders’ willingness to supply it.

> True believers make the most money in manias, and skeptics lose the least when they crash.

> I insist strenuously that risk and volatility aren’t the same thing.
