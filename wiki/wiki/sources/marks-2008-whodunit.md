# Whodunit

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** February 20, 2008 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2008-02-20-whodunit.pdf)

## Summary

Written during the subprime crisis and credit crunch, this memo assigns blame along the chain that built subprime mortgage CDOs. Wall Street demand for high-yielding collateral drove mortgage brokers to prize volume over credit; appraisers, mortgage insurers, rating agencies and monoline insurers lent false respectability; investment bankers and CDO managers engaged in 'ratings arbitrage'; and borrowers took deals too good to be true. Marks also faults siloed risk management and VaR, quants who extrapolate past patterns and miss tail events, and Greenspan's Fed for keeping rates too low and creating moral hazard. Ultimately he places responsibility on two groups: middlemen paid for actions they would not remain responsible for, and buyers who believed return was available without proportionate risk and lacked skepticism.

## Key Ideas

1. Originate-to-distribute incentives meant no party wanted conservative underwriting or appraisals; brokers were paid for volume, not loan performance.
2. 'Ratings arbitrage': turning low-rated collateral into mostly high-rated CDO debt without adding value signals that ratings were broken.
3. Rating agencies' worst failure was rating securities whose risk (the eventual default rate) was unknowable; monoline insurers wrote protection far beyond their capital.
4. Separate risk management functions second-guess experts with less knowledge; VaR failed in Q3 2007, and Goldman's judgment overrode its VaR to good effect.
5. Quant models extrapolate past norms and are useless in the tails, where 'everything interesting' happens (Ric Kayne).
6. Fed policy that lets advances run and limits declines creates moral hazard and larger future excesses.
7. Excess return requires market mistakes; when return looks generous for the risk, suspect hidden risk. Skepticism is indispensable and most investment failures are preceded by a lack of it.
8. Short-termism made firms 'keep dancing' (Chuck Prince); losing market share in a frothy market would have been the right call.

## Market Context

February 2008: subprime crisis, credit crunch and possible recession. CDO investors had lost over $100 billion, monoline insurers' triple-A ratings were threatened, and the Fed under Bernanke had made a 3/4-point cut on January 21 followed by another 1/2 point a week later.

## Notable Claims

- Appraisers helped inflate mortgage values by $135 billion in 2006 (Wachter estimate).
- S&P placed 69% of 2006 triple-A subprime bonds on negative watch.
- MBIA's Q4 mortgage losses ($714m) compared with $920m of muni losses over 36 years.
- ACA wrote $69 billion of credit protection on $425 million of capital.
- VaR's predicted maximum trading loss was exceeded on a quarter of trading days in Q3 2007.

## Quotes

> There’s something fundamentally wrong when there’s no party to a transaction who wants the appraisal to be conservative.

> “99% of financial history has taken place within two standard deviations,” he says, “but everything interesting has taken place outside of two standard deviations.”

> “People can’t be counted on to do the right thing,” she said, “when they don’t have anything at risk.”
