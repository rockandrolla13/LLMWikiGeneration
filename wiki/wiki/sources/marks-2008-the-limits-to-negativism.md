# The Limits to Negativism

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** October 15, 2008 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2008-10-15-the-limits-to-negativism.pdf)

## Summary

Written days after the post-Lehman panic peaked, this memo argues that skepticism is not the same as pessimism. In 2005-2007 no positive story was too good to be believed. In September-October 2008 no negative scenario was too dire, and investors failed to distinguish what might happen from how likely it was. Marks calls the panic the greatest he had seen and says most price declines came from forced selling and psychology, not from weakness in the underlying assets. He expects governments to rescue every key player after the Lehman mistake, but warns that printed money must still be lent on, and that a glut of currency raises the long-run risk of inflation, illustrated with Weimar Germany. He predicts a less leveraged, more regulated financial sector, declares the bear market at its third stage, and says Oaktree was buying aggressively and averaging down.

## Key Ideas

1. Skepticism and pessimism are not synonyms: skepticism calls for pessimism when optimism is excessive, and for optimism when pessimism is excessive.
2. In a crisis people stop separating what might happen from the probability that it will happen.
3. Most of the late-2008 declines came from a technical spiral (mark-to-market, capital inadequacy, margin calls, forced selling), not from deterioration in the assets themselves.
4. When the negative story is priced in, the positive story that few believe holds the greater potential for profit.
5. After Lehman, governments would rescue every key player and could print without limit, but credit ('money of the mind') only restarts when lenders choose to lend.
6. Printing money to repay nominal debts risks currency debasement and inflation (Weimar Germany), which argues for caution on low-yielding long Treasurys.
7. In the third stage of a bear market everyone agrees things can only get worse, so the greater long-term risk lies in not investing.
8. A formula for collapsing markets: estimate intrinsic value, buy when price falls below it, average down, and be right about the value.

## Market Context

Mid-October 2008, weeks after Lehman Brothers failed. The commercial paper market froze, money market funds broke the buck, Morgan Stanley was under pressure, and governments were committing hundreds of billions to trillions to rescue financial institutions, including government equity stakes in banks. Markets rallied sharply on Monday October 13 as if the crisis were over.

## Notable Claims

- Marks called it the greatest panic he had ever seen.
- Letting Lehman fail is now widely seen as a big mistake.
- The German mark went from 60 to 8,000 per dollar between early 1921 and end-1922.
- Marks judged the bear market to be in its third stage.
- The financial sector of the future will be less leveraged and more regulated.

## Quotes

> no scenario was too negative to be credible, and any scenario incorporating an element of optimism was dismissed as Pollyannaish.

> Skepticism calls for pessimism when optimism is excessive. But it also calls for optimism when pessimism is excessive

> When others conduct their affairs with excessive negativism, it’s worth being positive.
