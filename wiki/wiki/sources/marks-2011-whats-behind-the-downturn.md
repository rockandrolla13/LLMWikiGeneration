# What's Behind the Downturn?

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** September 7, 2011 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2011-09-07-whats-behind-the-downturn.pdf)

## Summary

Written after the violent market swings of August 2011, the memo argues that the sell-off came from a confluence of already-known worries rather than new facts. Markets cope with problems one at a time but lose their composure when several hit together, as in the credit crises of 1990, 2002 and 2008. Marks walks through the US debt-ceiling standoff and S&P's downgrade of US debt to AA+ (after which Treasury yields paradoxically fell below 2%), the European sovereign debt problem as unpayable debts in the periphery and questionable receivables in the core, the risk of a double-dip recession, and the disappointment of over-idealised emerging markets. He concludes that the swing was more in psychology than in fundamentals. Because high yield spreads had widened sharply while fund investors redeemed, he would now be a better buyer, in moderation. Longer term, he sees a distribution of possible futures shifted to the left of the late twentieth century's.

## Key Ideas

1. Markets handle problems one at a time, but a confluence of simultaneous negatives makes them chaotic; simultaneity rather than individual force drove the crises of 1990, 2002 and 2008.
2. Investors flip-flop between seeing only positives and only negatives; the task is to look past market behaviour to underlying realities.
3. The S&P downgrade of US debt caused a flight into Treasurys, the opposite of the near-unanimous expectation, which shows how little investors know about how markets work.
4. Debtors worldwide borrowed more than they could repay on demand; the question is whether loans are renewed, who pays them off, or how else they are discharged. In Europe the strong nations' problem is questionable receivables.
5. Marks is not an economist and holds no strong double-dip view; Oaktree assumes a sluggish rather than V-shaped recovery, which argues against moving out on the risk curve.
6. Simple 'sure thing' stories (the emerging-market miracle, China) get priced for perfection, so even favourable developments can produce losses.
7. Nobody waves a banner when assets get cheap enough; with high yield spreads up sharply and fund redemptions heavy, he would buy more, in moderation.
8. The distribution of possible futures is shifted left versus the unusually lucky late twentieth century; cheapness provides only limited margin of safety.

## Market Context

Early September 2011, after the US debt-ceiling standoff, S&P's August 5 downgrade of US long-term debt from AAA to AA+, the escalating euro-zone peripheral debt crisis and worries about European banks, weak Q2 US growth with downward revisions, and slowing emerging markets. Markets had been risk-on in May ('How Quickly They Forget').

## Notable Claims

- The Dow moved 400+ points four days in a row for the first time.
- US high yield yield-to-worst and spread widened from 6.8%/526 bp to 8.3%/719 bp in three months.
- European high yield yield and spread went from 7.7%/545 bp to 10.0%/840 bp.
- US unfunded future obligations estimated at $64-99 trillion versus $14.3 trillion national debt.
- After the downgrade, the 10-year Treasury yield fell below 2%.

## Quotes

> Market crises are chaotic, not orderly, and the multiplicity and simultaneity of contributing causes play a big part in making them so.

> So, as is often the case, the swing we’ve had is more in psychology than in fundamentals.

> Nobody waves a banner when assets have gotten cheap enough
