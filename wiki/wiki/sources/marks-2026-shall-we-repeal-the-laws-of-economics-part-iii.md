# Shall We Repeal the Laws of Economics – Part III

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** September 22, 2026 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/shall-we-repeal-the-laws-of-economics-part-iii.pdf)

## Summary

The third memo in a series criticising government attempts to override economic laws. With the 30-year Treasury yield above 5.3% in August 2026, the Treasury expanded long-dated buybacks to push long rates down. Marks calls this cosmetic: the effect is likely temporary, it ignores the root causes (inflation above target, fiscal indiscipline with a ~6% of GDP deficit in prosperity, heavy capital demand from deficits and the AI buildout), and its impact is mainly psychological. He sees no realistic US default and expects the dollar to remain the main reserve currency, but warns of debasement and of a chronic, growing interest cost. The fix is behavioural: fiscal responsibility, higher revenue as a share of GDP, spending growth below GDP growth, and productivity gains. He advises against selling US stocks to escape what is a fiscal and currency problem, not a corporate one.

## Key Ideas

1. Economies are natural systems; interventions to override market outcomes often have unintended second-order effects.
2. Treasury buybacks to suppress long yields are cosmetic and temporary, like a water jet holding a ball up only while it runs.
3. Rising long rates reflect fundamentals: sticky inflation, large deficits in prosperity, a shortening debt maturity profile, and huge capital demand including AI data centres.
4. The US has a 'golden credit card' from reserve-currency status, but misuses it; Keynes advocated deficits in slowdowns and repayment in recoveries.
5. A US default is improbable since debt is in its own currency; the risk is debasement and a rising interest 'invoice' rather than an acute crisis.
6. The goal should be to address what pushes rates up, not to push rates down (ice pack on a fever).
7. Solutions: accept debt won't shrink, adopt budget discipline, raise revenue (especially top marginal rates, fewer preferences), keep spending growth below GDP growth, and raise productivity (including via AI).
8. Selling US stocks doesn't escape a dollar risk; moving to non-dollar assets brings other risks, so diversification away from the dollar should be modest.

## Market Context

September 2026. Long-term US yields had risen (30-year above 5.3% in August), federal debt reached about $40 trillion, inflation was elevated partly due to oil prices tied to a war with Iran, and the Fed had just raised its benchmark rate. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent expanded long-bond buybacks; markets were underwhelmed, with yields rising after the September 9 operation. McKinsey estimated over $5 trillion of AI data centre spending through 2030.

## Notable Claims

- The 30-year Treasury yield closed above 5.3% on August 17, a 19-year high.
- Treasury raised its maximum long-dated buyback from $2bn to $4bn per operation (later $6bn).
- PCE inflation was 3.7% in July 2026 versus a 2% target.
- Deficit near 6% of GDP with 4% unemployment; net interest over $1 trillion, more than defense.
- The dollar was in 89% of FX transactions in 2025 and 57% of allocated reserves in Q1 2026.

## Quotes

> The goal shouldn’t be to get interest rates down. It should be to respond to the factors pushing rates up.

> The issue at hand isn’t a matter of conjecture . . . just math.

> This isn’t an investment problem. It’s a political problem, but it poses a problem for investors.
