# Risk Revisited Again

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** June 8, 2015 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2015-06-08-risk-revisited-again.pdf)

## Summary

An expanded version of 'Risk Revisited' (September 2014), with the new passages in italics, meant to hold everything Marks knows about risk. He rejects volatility as 'the' definition of risk: what investors fear is permanent loss, which cannot be quantified before or even after the fact, any more than the probability of rain. Drawing on Peter Bernstein, Keynes, Leibniz, Minsky and Chesterton, he argues that the future is a probability distribution, not a fixed outcome; that risk means more things can happen than will happen; that knowing probabilities does not tell you the outcome; and that only one of many possibilities will occur. He redraws the risk-return line as widening distributions, catalogues 24 forms of risk, illustrates them with Oaktree's 'ten percent solution' credit strategies, and gives six over-arching observations: risk is counterintuitive, risk aversion keeps markets safe, risk is hidden, multi-faceted, should not be left to risk managers, and must be managed constantly. He ends that in 2015's low-rate world investors have entered the zone of imprudence, so caution deserves extra weight.

## Key Ideas

1. Risk is the possibility of permanent loss, not volatility; volatility is used because it is quantifiable ('machinable').
2. Risk can't be measured even after the fact: a doubled investment may have been safe or a lucky dart throw.
3. The future is a probability distribution; risk means more things can happen than will happen (Dimson), and even though many things can happen, only one will.
4. Riskier investments must appear to offer higher returns but are not certain to deliver them; higher risk widens the outcome distribution and worsens the bad tail.
5. Two complementary risks: losing money and missing opportunities; excessive fear of missing out creates 'FOMO risk'.
6. The riskiest thing is overpaying; a low price provides a margin of safety, and valuation risk is within the investor's control.
7. Risk is counterintuitive: as prices fall assets become less risky, and widespread belief that there's no risk is the riskiest condition.
8. Risk management belongs to every investment participant, not to separate quants relying on VaR and normal-distribution models.

## Market Context

Mid-2015, about six years after the crisis: ultra-low rates (10-year Treasury about 2½%) pushed money into riskier assets, retail flows lifted prices, capital-market standards and covenants were weakening, and fear of missing out had replaced fear of loss; Marks judged fundamentals fine and prices full but not bubbly.

## Notable Claims

- The S&P 500 fell 57% in the global financial crisis.
- Record cold caused U.S. GDP to fall 2.9% in Q1 2014, confounding forecasters.
- Nifty Fifty investors lost 80-90% between 1968 and 1973.
- Oaktree's 'ten percent solution' is five credit strategies targeting ~10% returns.
- The memo enumerates 24 forms of risk.

## Quotes

> The probability of loss is no more measurable than the probability of rain.

> In brief, if riskier investments could be counted on to produce higher returns, they wouldn't be riskier.

> Simply put, risk is low when risk aversion and risk consciousness are high, and high when they're low.
