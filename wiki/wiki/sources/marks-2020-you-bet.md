# You Bet!

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** January 13, 2020 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/you-bet.pdf)

## Summary

Inspired by rereading Annie Duke's 'Thinking in Bets', Marks draws parallels between gambling and investing. He starts from C. Jackson Grayson's lesson that you cannot judge decision quality from outcomes, recounts his lifelong small-stakes game playing, and classifies games by hidden information, luck and skill: chess, backgammon, roulette, poker and blackjack. Active investing in less efficient markets involves all three, so it is most like poker and blackjack; index investing involves none. The core lesson is that success comes not from picking the favourite but from assessing the proposition, i.e. whether the odds (the price) are fair. The Nifty Fifty were winning companies but poor investments, while 'junk' bonds were good investments because the price was too favourable. Andrew Marks adds parallels such as game selection, circle of competence, not playing every hand, bet sizing, and avoiding ruin.

## Key Ideas

1. You can't tell the quality of a decision from the outcome; good decisions can fail and bad ones succeed, especially over few trials.
2. Outcomes depend on decision quality and luck; good decision-makers estimate how unsure they are rather than seeking certainty.
3. Games differ by hidden information, luck and skill; active investing in less efficient markets involves all three, like poker and blackjack, while index investing involves none.
4. Under the efficient market hypothesis investing is like roulette; markets vary in efficiency, so distinguish 'alpha' markets from 'beta' markets.
5. Success comes from identifying superior propositions (mispricings), not picking favourites: success comes from buying things well, not buying good things.
6. Backgammon doubling shows it can be right to back an inferior position if the payoff justifies it (accept a double above 25% win probability).
7. Bet big when you have a big edge and small otherwise; avoid the risk of ruin; you need not play every hand.
8. In the long run skill overcomes bad luck, but in the short run luck can overwhelm skill and the two can look the same.

## Market Context

January 2020, just before the COVID-19 crash; the memo is philosophical and does not discuss current market conditions. It notes the long-run growth of passive investing as active managers failed to beat indices after fees.

## Notable Claims

- About 4% by dollar amount of high yield bonds defaulted each year on average, yet returns were good because prices were favourable.
- Buying the Nifty Fifty in May 1968 and holding five years lost almost all your money.
- A backgammon player behind should accept a double whenever his chance of winning exceeds 25%.
- A great poker player with a sizable edge will still be losing over 40% of the time after eight hours (Duke).
- Annie Duke had over $4 million of tournament winnings.

## Quotes

> Success in investing doesn’t come from buying good things, but from buying things well, and it’s essential to know the difference.

> It’s not a matter of what you buy, but what you pay for it.

> Why pay someone to play for you in a game where there’s no such thing as skill?
