# It's All Good

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** July 16, 2007 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2007-07-16-its-all-good.pdf)

## Summary

Marks argues that the cycle of 2003-2007 was the strongest, broadest and most heated upswing he had seen, because almost every asset class and country rose together and investors assumed it would continue. He restates his pendulum model of investor psychology and the polar opposites it swings between, warns that 'it's different this time' is a prerequisite for extremes, and stresses that even ideal fundamentals can become overpriced. Most of the memo attacks the period's financial innovation: structured products (CDOs, CLOs, CPDOs), 'selling onward' that separated loan origination from ownership and created moral hazard, covenant-lite and toggle debt, and mega-buyouts built on cheap leverage and optionality for the general partners. He predicts rising defaults and a large distressed LBO opportunity, lists what might end the upswing, and notes the first cracks: subprime delinquencies, two failed Bear Stearns funds, and pulled low-grade debt deals.

## Key Ideas

1. The economic, business, credit and market cycles are linked, and the credit and market cycles swing far more than fundamentals because of investor psychology.
2. The market pendulum swings between euphoria and depression, greed and fear, risk tolerance and risk aversion, and credence and skepticism. It rarely rests at the 'happy medium'.
3. 'It's different this time' is not just a symptom of an extreme but a prerequisite for one. Even ideal fundamentals can become overpriced.
4. Financial innovation is cyclical: 'the mother of invention isn't necessity, its salability'. Inventions originate in up markets but are tested in down markets.
5. Slicing and dicing (structuring) and selling onward do not remove risk. They create moral hazard and invite participants to add the risk back, as with subprime mortgage brokers.
6. 'The worst of loans are made in the best of times': with high yield defaults at a 25-year low, lenders accepted CCC issuance, dividend recaps, covenant-lite and PIK toggle bonds.
7. Equity is a company's margin of safety. Toggle and covenant-lite terms may postpone defaults but can make them worse when they come.
8. Mega-private equity relied on cheap debt, high fees and optionality that favoured the GPs. Marks expects a large distressed LBO debt opportunity a few years out.

## Market Context

July 2007, at the peak of the pre-crisis credit boom. Nearly every asset class and country was rising together, risk premiums were the skimpiest Marks had seen, private equity was doing record buyouts with covenant-lite and PIK toggle debt, and CDOs and other structured products proliferated. First-quarter 2007 subprime delinquencies had spread in the second quarter to CDOs and hedge funds, including two Bear Stearns funds, and new low-grade debt issues were being repriced, postponed or pulled.

## Notable Claims

- High yield default rate at a 25-year low.
- Derivatives market valued at about $500 trillion (WSJ, July 8).
- No country among The Economist's 42 grew GDP by less than Switzerland's 2.2%.
- Late-1980s LBOs like RJR borrowed 95% of the price, versus rarely much more than 80% in 2007.
- Postponed defaults may destroy more value: year three with 20% gone versus year five with 60% gone.

## Quotes

> In the financial world, the mother of invention isn’t necessity, its salability.

> Inventions originate in up markets, but they’re tested in down markets.

> There is no level of fundamentals that can’t become overpriced.
