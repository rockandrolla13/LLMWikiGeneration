# The Route to Performance

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** October 12, 1990 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/1990-10-12-the-route-to-performance.pdf)

## Summary

Marks's earliest memo, written as an addendum to a third-quarter client letter, argues that superior long-run results come from being a little better than average every year and protecting capital in bad years, not from stringing together top-decile years through bold bets. He contrasts a money manager who defended a huge shortfall versus the S&P 500 as being 'too early', and a convertible fund whose 1987 outperformance was largely undone in the next half year, with a Midwest pension plan whose equities never ranked below the 47th or (until 1990) above the 27th percentile yet sat in the fourth percentile over fourteen years. He ties this to how his group runs convertibles, high yield bonds and distressed debt: avoid losers and the winners take care of themselves.

## Key Ideas

1. Bold over- and under-weightings expose a manager to being far worse than the norm as easily as far better.
2. Alternating far-above-average and far-below-average years tends to produce a long-term record of volatility and mediocrity.
3. Consistently modest outperformance compounds into a top long-term ranking (the pension plan example: never below the 47th percentile, fourth percentile over 14 years).
4. An absence of disasters is the best foundation for above-average long-term performance, including in opportunistic niches.
5. In high yield bonds, relative performance should come from avoiding credit losses rather than reaching for higher, more uncertain yields.
6. In distressed debt, buy only where the cost price is covered by asset values; in convertibles, demand downside resistance alongside upside participation.
7. Accept that in some years risk-takers will beat a defensive approach.

## Market Context

Written in October 1990 as an addendum to a third-quarter client letter, prompted by a late-September Wall Street Journal article about a well-known money manager lagging the S&P 500 and a conversation with a pension plan director. The memo itself does not describe the macro backdrop beyond this.

## Notable Claims

- A prominent firm's equity results trailed the S&P 500 by 1,840 bp over twelve months through August 1990.
- A convertible fund beat the average convertible fund by more than 1,600 bp in 1987.
- A Midwest pension plan reached the fourth percentile over 14 years without any extreme single year.
- Avoiding losers lets winners take care of themselves.

## Quotes

> If you want to be in the top 5% of money managers, you have to be willing to be in the bottom 5%, too.

> the best foundation for above-average long term performance is an absence of disasters.

> if you can avoid losers (and losing years), the winners will take care of themselves.
