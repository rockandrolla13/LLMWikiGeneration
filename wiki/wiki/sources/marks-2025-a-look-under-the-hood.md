# A Look Under the Hood

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** October 28, 2025 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/a-look-under-the-hood.pdf)

## Summary

Marks recounts sitting in on a board meeting of an unnamed US state pension fund where its unnamed consultant reported on a board-member survey, and gives his reactions. The consultant framed risk with a two-by-two matrix of ability versus willingness to bear risk (capitalizing, defensive, protective, naive). The board had above-average ability but moderate willingness, which Marks likens to his advice to Penn's endowment in 2000 not to chase the 1990s boom. Board members ranked beating peers last among objectives. Marks stresses that success for a defined benefit plan means paying benefits at minimum sponsor cost, and that luck must be separated from skill through 'alternative histories'. He argues long-term investors have no intrinsic reason to fear volatility, as distinct from permanent loss; volatility matters through 'externalities' such as pension contributions or endowment draws. He discusses leverage at 15-20%, 25% illiquids and fees, and concludes that no performance standard is free of deficiencies. Short-term assessment must be relative, and a fair assessment period must span a full cycle of good and bad times.

## Key Ideas

1. Risk posture can be framed by a 2x2 matrix of financial ability to bear risk versus willingness to bear it: capitalizing, defensive, protective, naive (low ability, high willingness).
2. Success for a defined benefit plan is paying benefits at minimum cost to the sponsor; outperforming peers is not the goal, and shared failure is still failure.
3. Meeting the goal does not prove skill; one must consider alternative histories to separate luck from skill.
4. In pure investment terms, long-term investors have no intrinsic reason to worry about volatility as distinct from permanent loss; volatility matters through investor-specific 'externalities'.
5. Because volatility is risky for some holders but not others, the risk lies in the investor's environment rather than in the asset.
6. The actuarial return target matters most long-term but is irrelevant for judging a single year; short-term judgement must be relative to what similarly placed peers achieved, and no standard is free of deficiencies.
7. A fair performance assessment period must cover a full market cycle, including both bull and bear phases, to separate skill from a bias toward aggression or defence.
8. Moderate leverage (15-20%) and some illiquidity (25%) can suit a well-funded plan, but lender pull-back risk and certain fees versus hoped-for performance must be weighed.

## Market Context

October 2025. The memo notes 'the last two bullish years' and a period of about 16 years of mostly good times with only short dips. It mentions AI stocks as an example of a volatile holding. Otherwise the memo focuses on institutional governance rather than current market conditions.

## Notable Claims

- All board members agreed that exposure to risk is necessary to meet objectives.
- A substantial majority were comfortable with leverage at 15-20% of plan assets.
- Buffett would rather earn a lumpy 15% than a smooth 12%.
- Board members ranked the Sharpe ratio last among six performance metrics.
- Marks chaired Penn's investment committee from mid-2000, when Penn ranked very low in endowment per student.

## Quotes

> Success for a defined benefit pension plan means being able to pay benefits and minimize the cost to the plan sponsor. Period.

> So, the answer is that an appropriate performance assessment period has to include both good times and bad.

> If it’s true that an asset’s volatility can bring risk for some investors but not others, then clearly the risk doesn’t lie in the investment, but in something in the investor’s environment.
