# It's All Very Taxing

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** November 16, 2011 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2011-11-16-its-all-very-taxing.pdf)

## Summary

A background piece on US tax policy written while the Congressional supercommittee worked to a November 23, 2011 deadline to cut the deficit by over $1 trillion over a decade. Marks takes aim at the phrase 'fair share', arguing fairness is in the eye of the beholder. He reviews data showing top earners pay most federal taxes but at lower rates than in the past, and questions the fairness of preferential rates on capital gains and dividends, the Buffett Rule, and deductions for mortgage interest, charity and state and local taxes. He sets out the arguments for and against raising taxes, discusses income inequality, the sputtering economy, flat tax and Cain's 9-9-9 plan, and warns of a 'tyranny of the majority' if people stop believing they can get rich. He concludes the choice comes down to scaling back promises, raising taxes or running bigger deficits.

## Key Ideas

1. 'Fair share' has no objective standard; it is usually another way to say 'raise their taxes'. The only undisputed fairness rule is that higher earners pay more.
2. Top earners pay most federal taxes, yet effective rates for the very top have fallen sharply since 1979 and income tax revenue is at a historic low share of GDP.
3. Which income counts as 'virtuous' (capital gains, dividends vs wages and interest) is subjective and shifts with politics.
4. Deductions, loopholes and tax incentives are the same thing named from different viewpoints; each subsidy (mortgage, charity, state/local taxes, muni exemption) penalises someone else.
5. Arguments against higher taxes include 'starve the beast', the elasticity of taxable income, the Laffer curve and Keynesian concerns about a weak economy.
6. Austerity can worsen deficits by slowing growth; durable recovery needs industrial expansion, which needs tax rates and policy stability that let entrepreneurs keep profits.
7. When people without money believe they cannot make money, a democratic majority may vote to take it from those who have it.
8. Entitlement promises leave only three options: scale them back, raise taxes, or let deficits grow.

## Market Context

November 2011. After a contentious summer debt-ceiling fight that led S&P to downgrade long-term US Treasury debt, a Congressional supercommittee faced a November 23 deadline to find $1 trillion-plus in deficit cuts. The early-2011 recovery had cooled, raising fears of a double-dip recession; Greece and other European debtors faced austerity; the 2012 presidential campaign was starting, with populist rhetoric on both sides.

## Notable Claims

- Distribution of federal individual taxes by income percentile.
- About half of Americans pay no federal income tax.
- Effective federal rate for top 0.01% fell from 42.9% in 1979 to 31.5% in 2005 (CBO).
- Buffett Rule would affect 60,000 taxpayers and raise $13 billion over 10 years.
- TPC analysis: 83.8% of filers would see a tax increase under 9-9-9.

## Quotes

> The truth is, fairness is almost entirely in the eye of the beholder, and “get them to pay their fair share” seems like just another way to say “raise their taxes.”

> When confronted with complex problems, people often welcome simple solutions.

> Tax policy consists of deciding who to take from (and how much) and who to give it to.
