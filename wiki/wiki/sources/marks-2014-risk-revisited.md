# Risk Revisited

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** September 3, 2014 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2014-09-03-risk-revisited.pdf)

## Summary

Building on his 2006 memo 'Risk' and his book The Most Important Thing, Marks argues that investment risk is mainly the possibility of permanent loss, not volatility. Such risk cannot be measured beforehand, or even afterwards. Because the future is unknowable, investors should think of it as a probability distribution. He makes four key points: the future is a range of possibilities, 'risk means more things can happen than will happen' (Dimson), knowing probabilities is not knowing outcomes, and although many things can happen, only one will, so intolerable outcomes must be avoided even on high expected-value paths. He redraws the risk/return line as widening distributions and catalogs 24 forms of risk, from credit, illiquidity, leverage and funding risk to model, black swan, career and FOMO risk. He illustrates several with Oaktree's 'ten percent solution' credit strategies. He closes with six observations: risk is counterintuitive, risk aversion keeps markets safe, risk is hidden, risk is multi-faceted, risk management belongs to every investor rather than designated risk managers, and risk control should be constant. Given ultra-low rates and deteriorating standards, he says markets have entered 'the zone of imprudence' and urges moving forward 'but with caution'.

## Key Ideas

1. Volatility is machinable but is not 'the' definition of risk; investors fear permanent loss, which can't be quantified a priori or even after the fact.
2. The future should be treated as a probability distribution; superior investors sense the distribution better and find asymmetries where upside exceeds downside.
3. Riskier investments must appear to offer higher returns, but they widen the range of outcomes and worsen the bad ones; if they reliably paid more they wouldn't be riskier.
4. Expected value isn't enough: some outcomes are intolerable, so avoid paths that risk ruin ('no interest in being a skydiver who's successful 95% of the time').
5. Investors face two opposing risks, losing money and missing opportunities, plus FOMO risk when fear of missing out becomes excessive.
6. Oaktree's 'ten percent solution' credit strategies deliberately bear credit, illiquidity, concentration, leverage and manager risk while avoiding funding risk.
7. The riskiest thing is overpaying; a low price provides a margin of safety, and valuation risk is largely within the investor's control.
8. Risk is counterintuitive and hidden: it is lowest when risk aversion is high, it shows up only when the tide goes out, and models like VaR understated it in the crisis.

## Market Context

September 2014, six years after the crisis. The ten-year Treasury yielded about 2½%, ultra-low rates were pushing money into riskier assets, capital market standards and covenants were deteriorating, and Oaktree was marketing credit strategies targeting roughly 10% returns. Marks judged US fundamentals fine and prices full but not at bubble levels.

## Notable Claims

- S&P 500 fell 57% in the global crisis.
- Record cold caused a 2.9% Q1 2014 GDP decline.
- Nifty Fifty investors lost 80-90%.
- Enhanced Income Fund II uses 3-to-1 leverage.
- The memo enumerates 24 forms of risk.

## Quotes

> The probability of loss is no more measurable than the probability of rain.

> In brief, if riskier investments could be counted on to produce higher returns, they wouldn’t be riskier.

> At bottom, the riskiest thing is overpaying for an asset (regardless of its quality), and the best way to reduce risk is by paying a price that’s irrationally low (ditto).
