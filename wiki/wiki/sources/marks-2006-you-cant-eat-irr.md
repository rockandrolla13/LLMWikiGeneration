# You Can’t Eat IRR

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** July 12, 2006 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2006-07-12-you-cant-eat-irr.pdf)

## Summary

Marks examines how to measure the performance of private-equity-style funds that call, invest and return capital over time. He explains why the dollar-weighted internal rate of return (IRR) beats the time-weighted compound return, using Oaktree's Special Credits Fund I, where one small final sale doubled the time-weighted return but barely moved IRR. He then shows that IRR ignores how much committed capital was put to work and how quickly, and he proposes times-capital-returned (TCR, measured on committed capital) as a complement, while warning that TCR can reward simply holding a low-return fund longer. Using Opportunities Fund IVb in 2002, he shows one eight-month period giving returns from -0.5% to 61.4% depending on the measure. He criticizes annualizing short-term results and argues that dividend recaps inflate buyout IRRs through financial engineering rather than value creation. His conclusion is that no single number captures fund performance, and risk taken must also be weighed.

## Key Ideas

1. IRR is dollar-weighted and so is a better yardstick than time-weighted return for funds whose capital expands and contracts; time-weighted returns can be distorted by big percentage gains on small capital.
2. IRR measures returns on capital employed but ignores undrawn commitments and how promptly a manager puts money to work; two funds can share an IRR yet differ tenfold in dollar gains.
3. Times-capital-returned on committed capital captures deployment, while 'multiple of cost' on contributed capital shares IRR's blind spot.
4. TCR is also imperfect: holding a low-return fund longer can match a high-return fund's TCR, so the time value of capital matters.
5. Annualizing part-year returns (as IRR must) misleads, as the 1970s buy/write example shows.
6. Dividend recaps raise IRR by shrinking invested equity but add investor wealth only if the cash is reinvested profitably; $68bn of such borrowing since April 2003 signals imprudent capital markets.
7. Fund evaluation must weigh IRR, TCR, deployment speed, distribution timing, what LPs did with uncalled or returned capital, risk and leverage, and the quality of marks.

## Market Context

Mid-2006, during a buoyant credit market with 'an excess of enthusiasm and shortage of caution'. Buyout funds were reporting very high short-term IRRs, helped by dividend recaps financed with high yield bonds and bank loans, while times-capital-returned ratios stayed low.

## Notable Claims

- One small final asset sale doubled Special Credits Fund I's time-weighted return.
- The same sale moved IRR only slightly.
- Opportunities Fund IVb's eight-month IRR versus time-weighted return.
- Dividend recap and buyback debt volume.
- Friend's secondary fund-of-funds earned only 7.3% a year on commitment despite a 27.1% IRR.

## Quotes

> A high internal rate of return does not in and of itself put money in one’s pocket.

> Dividend recaps increase fund investors’ wealth only if the amounts dividended out can be reinvested profitably.

> Reliance on a single figure can’t possibly provide the answer – not even IRR.
