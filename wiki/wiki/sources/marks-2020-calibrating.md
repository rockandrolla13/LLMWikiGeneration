# Calibrating

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** April 6, 2020 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/calibrating.pdf)

## Summary

Written in early April 2020 after four March memos on the coronavirus crisis, this memo explains how to adjust portfolio stance when 'everyone has the same data regarding the present and the same ignorance regarding the future.' Marks summarizes the March memos: buying was reasonable in proportion to how far prices had fallen, an incremental rather than all-in approach was best, and the most consistent message was that buying nothing at the new lows would be a mistake. He argues that a manager's most important job is balancing offense and defense, managing the twin risks of losing money and missing opportunity, and calibrating that balance to the environment. Before the crisis, high prices, low prospective returns and pro-risk behavior justified Oaktree's 'move forward, but with caution'. Now risks are recognized, yields are attractive (high yield ex-energy near 9%) and investors are chastened, so cautious investors can move toward neutral or offense. He calls waiting for the bottom irrational: investors should buy on the way down when sellers are most urgent, as Oaktree did after Lehman in 2008, even though markets may test their lows again.

## Key Ideas

1. In crises most commentary is opinion; optimists and pessimists often weigh the same data points differently because of innate bias.
2. The 2020 environment had a wider-than-usual probability distribution with fatter tails and no reliable historical analogy.
3. The most important portfolio decision is the balance between offense and defense, framed as the twin risks of losing money and missing opportunity.
4. Calibrate that balance to conditions: move toward offense when others are prudent, prices are low and fear is prevalent, and toward defense when the reverse holds.
5. After the March 2020 sell-off, risk was recognized, returns attractive and risk-taking had dried up, so defense no longer needed to be favored.
6. 'The bottom' is only identifiable in retrospect, so waiting for it is irrational; buy on the way down when sellers are most motivated.
7. Buy when price is below intrinsic value and buy more if it cheapens; aim for many good buys rather than a few perfect ones.
8. Bear markets rarely end in a straight line; initial lows are often retested, which is consistent with buying value today.

## Market Context

Early April 2020, during the COVID-19 pandemic. The S&P 500 fell 34% from February 19 to March 23, then rallied sharply after the Fed/Treasury response announced March 24. Oaktree cancelled its client conference, and high yield yields excluding energy had risen to nearly 9%.

## Notable Claims

- High yield ex-energy yield rose from 3½% to nearly 9%.
- Oaktree bought nearly $7bn of distressed debt in Q4 2008.
- S&P 500 low on March 23, 2020.
- Best three-day rally since the 1930s.
- Gavekal: initial lows are usually retested.

## Quotes

> “These days everyone has the same data regarding the present and the same ignorance regarding the future.”

> The investor’s goal should be to make a large number of good buys, not just a few perfect ones.

> Thus, “I’m going to wait for the bottom” is an irrational statement.
