# Risk

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** January 19, 2006 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2006-01-19-risk.pdf)

## Summary

Prompted by a Deloitte article on corporate risk by Rick Funston, Marks devotes a full memo to investment risk. He rejects the textbook claim that riskier investments deliver higher returns: they only have to offer the prospect of higher returns, and he redraws the capital market line with widening return distributions. He rejects volatility as the definition of risk, calling it an academic convenience, and argues that risk is first and foremost the likelihood of losing money. He lists personal risks such as falling short of goals, benchmark and career risk, unconventionality and illiquidity. Risk is subjective, hidden and unquantifiable even in retrospect, because outcomes do not reveal the probabilities that were in play, as Taleb's 'alternative histories' show. Whether concentration, illiquidity or distressed debt is risky depends on how much the investor knows. Risk is multi-dimensional (scenarios, offsets, correlations, domino effects), and models like VaR fail at the extremes. The investor's job is to bear risk intelligently for profit, as life insurers do: risk that is known, analysable, diversifiable and well paid. Risk control is not risk avoidance.

## Key Ideas

1. Riskier investments must offer the prospect of higher returns, not a guarantee; if they reliably delivered higher returns they would not be riskier.
2. Marks redraws the capital market line with probability distributions that widen as risk rises, showing both higher expected return and greater chance of loss.
3. Volatility is an academic convenience for a quantifiable risk proxy; the risk investors actually price is the likelihood of losing money.
4. Other, personal risks exist: falling short of goals, benchmark/underperformance risk, career or agency risk, unconventionality risk, and illiquidity risk.
5. Risk is not measurable even after the fact: what happened was not necessarily likely, and what did not happen was not necessarily improbable (Taleb's alternative histories, 'lucky idiots').
6. Whether concentration, illiquidity or distressed debt is risky depends on the investor's knowledge; Buffett, Munger and Karsh are examples.
7. Risk is multi-dimensional (scenarios, offsets, correlations, domino effects), and models such as VaR are of very limited use at the extremes where it matters.
8. The investor's job is to bear risk intelligently for profit, like a life insurer: risk you are aware of, can analyse, can diversify, and are well paid to bear.

## Market Context

January 2006, a benign period in markets. The memo is conceptual rather than a market call. It references Sarbanes-Oxley era audit committees, Buffett's 1999 underperformance during the tech bubble, and the 1998 emerging-markets meltdown with LTCM and Russia.

## Notable Claims

- Bruce Karsh's distressed debt funds had no losing fund and no negative aggregate year in seventeen years.
- Risk is first and foremost the likelihood of losing money.
- Everything important in financial history happens outside two standard deviations (attributed to Ric Kayne).
- In 1998, emerging-market losses spread through US stocks, high yield funds and private equity.
- Probability estimates other than 0 and 100 can only be assessed over many trials.

## Quotes

> if riskier investments reliably produced higher returns, they wouldn’t be riskier!

> Fault lines run through every portfolio, adding to the complexity of managing risk.

> When you boil it all down, it’s the investor’s job to intelligently bear risk for profit.
