# Random Thoughts on the Identification of Investment Opportunities

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** January 24, 1994 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/1994-01-24-random-thoughts-on-the-identification-of-inv-opportunities.pdf)

## Summary

A short, numbered list of seven principles for finding investment opportunities. Marks argues that no asset class deserves high returns by its nature, that the timing and price of a purchase matter more than the asset itself, and that psychology is the most important investing discipline. Popularity drives future price moves, so the safest and most profitable buys are the unloved ones, and an asset that everyone knows is cheap is a contradiction. He uses the 1990-91 high yield bond market as his example: fear forced high standards and wide spreads, which set up strong returns. The memo closes with Gresham's Law applied to investing: careless buyers push demanding investors out of the market, so demanding investors must accept being inactive at times.

## Key Ideas

1. No group or sector has a birthright to consistently high returns; real estate as an inflation hedge failed when bought at the wrong time.
2. When and at what price you buy matters more than what you buy; the 1974 'nifty fifty' (Xerox, Avon, Merck) show that good companies do not guarantee good results.
3. Psychology outranks accounting and economics: future prices depend on whether an asset gains or loses admirers.
4. Sold-out conferences for an asset class are a danger sign; prefer auctions with one or two buyers.
5. Book the bet no one else will: when everyone fears junk bonds, spreads overcompensate for credit risk.
6. The market runs on fear or greed at any one time, not both; fear imposes credit standards, while fearless buyers raise the careful investor's workload.
7. Gresham's Law in investing: undemanding investors lower underwriting standards and drive demanding investors to the sidelines.

## Market Context

Written in January 1994. The memo looks back on the 1990-91 high yield bond panic, when investors feared junk bonds and only top-quality issuers could borrow. It also references the 1974 collapse of the 'nifty fifty' growth stocks.

## Notable Claims

- High yield bonds in late 1990 were priced to overcompensate for credit risk and became the best performing fixed income sector.
- In early 1991 fear meant only the best high yield bonds could be issued.
- Good companies bought at the wrong price did badly in 1974.
- Price can make any security a good or bad buy.

## Quotes

> There is no security that is so good that it can't be overpriced, or so bad that it can't be underpriced.

> An investment that "everyone" knows to be undervalued is an oxymoron.

> Demanding investors must be willing to be inactive at times.
