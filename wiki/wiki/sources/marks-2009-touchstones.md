# Touchstones

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** November 10, 2009 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2009-11-10-touchstones.pdf)

## Summary

Two years into the financial crisis, Marks pulls together its lessons through a series of quotations and adages. 'Greed is good' only up to a point; nothing is riskier than a widespread belief that there is no risk; Soros's reflexivity shows how belief that mortgages were safe made them unsafe; Greenspan's hands-off view of bubbles abetted them; leverage magnifies but does not improve returns. Using Dimson's 'risk means more things can happen than will happen', he argues for treating the future as a range of outcomes and building in a margin of safety. Prince's 'still dancing', Buffett's 'tide goes out' and Pigou's 'error of pessimism' illustrate herd behaviour at tops and bottoms, and he concludes that countercyclical behaviour (buy low, sell high) matters more than security selection or asset allocation.

## Key Ideas

1. The pendulum swings between trusting free markets and demanding regulation; if markets settle things, we must accept failures, and 'too big to fail' requires restraints on greed.
2. Belief that risk has been banished lets people take risks and bid prices up; worry and risk aversion are essential to a safe system and to adequate risk premiums.
3. Reflexivity: demand for 'safe' mortgage securities weakened lending standards and inflated home prices, producing the first nationwide decline.
4. Bubbles rest on the false idea that something is a good investment regardless of price.
5. Investors who think the future is knowable bet, concentrate and lever; those who do not diversify, hedge and stay high in the capital structure, and they were better placed when the crash came.
6. Margin of safety and return maximisation are incompatible; survival must be achieved every day, not on average (6-foot man, 5-foot-average stream).
7. Investors face two risks, losing money and missing out; when only missing out is feared, behaviour turns risky. 'Too much money chasing too few deals' kills safe returns.
8. Skepticism is not pessimism: it calls for optimism when pessimism is excessive; countercyclical behaviour is the most pivotal factor over a cycle.

## Market Context

November 2009, about two years after the onset of the financial crisis and a year after the post-Lehman panic of late 2008. Markets had rebounded from the lows; Bear Stearns, Merrill Lynch, Citibank, AIG, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac had been rescued rather than allowed to fail, and many leveraged deals from 2003-07 faced debt maturities in the next few years.

## Notable Claims

- Madoff received $7 billion of withdrawal requests he could not meet.
- Greenspan said in 2002 testimony a house price bubble was 'most unlikely'.
- Pre-crisis mortgage products rested on there never having been a nationwide home price decline.
- Late 2008 was one of the greatest buying opportunities Marks had seen.
- Over 2004-08, countercyclical posture mattered more than security selection or asset allocation.

## Quotes

> Nothing’s More Risky Than a Widespread Belief That There’s No Risk

> It’s not possible that something can be a good investment regardless of the price paid.

> I say we never know where we’re going, but we sure as heck ought to know where we are
