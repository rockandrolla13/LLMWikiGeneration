# There They Go Again . . . Again

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** July 26, 2017 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/there-they-go-again-again.pdf)

## Summary

A long cautionary memo, about double the usual length, arguing that 'they' are again taking willing risks in a low-return, high-risk world. Marks acknowledges he is often early with warnings but prefers turning cautious too soon over too late. He describes the environment as high uncertainty, the lowest prospective returns ever, high asset prices and pro-risk behaviour. With his son Andrew, he lists the seeds of a boom: a benign environment, a grain of truth, early success, more money than ideas, willing suspension of disbelief, rejection of valuation norms, pursuit of the new, a virtuous circle and FOMO. He then surveys the evidence. US equities show a Shiller CAPE near 30 and the Buffett yardstick at an all-time high, with the VIX at a record low. Investors anointed the FAANG super-stocks and moved trillions into passive investing and ETFs. Credit shows cov-lite and low-yield single-B bonds (Netflix), emerging markets show Argentina's 100-year bond, and private equity fundraising hit a record. Other signs are SoftBank's levered $93 billion Vision Fund and soaring Bitcoin and Ether, which he calls 'not real'. He concludes the market temperature is elevated, not a bubble, that the easy money has been made, and that Oaktree will 'move forward, but with caution', avoid reaching for return and favour low-risk approaches within each asset class.

## Key Ideas

1. It is better to turn cautious too soon (and underperform for a while) than too late, when it is hard to cut risk and exit.
2. Boom ingredients: benign environment, grain of truth, early success, more money than ideas, willing suspension of disbelief, rejection of valuation norms, pursuit of the new, virtuous circle, and fear of missing out.
3. Risk is highest when people cannot see what might cause a downturn, because they do not price risks they cannot see.
4. Bull markets anoint super-stocks (FAANGs, like the Nifty-Fifty before them) that become priced for perfection; their multiple expansion often reverses hardest in the correction.
5. Passive investing relies on active investors to set fair prices; as passive share grows, value-agnostic forced buying of large-cap winners may prove rotational rather than perpetual, and 'smart beta' rules are discretionary.
6. In credit and emerging-market debt, weak covenants, sub-4% single-B yields and an 8% Argentine century bond indicate inadequate risk premiums; you can't eat relative returns.
7. Digital currencies are a speculative fad with value only in what others will pay; investing means buying when price is attractive relative to intrinsic value.
8. The key strategic decision is whether to be aggressive or defensive; in a low-return, high-risk world, avoid reaching for return and choose low-risk approaches within each asset class.

## Market Context

Mid-2017, more than eight years into the post-GFC bull market. The S&P 500 traded at 25x trailing earnings, the VIX hit a record low, FAANG stocks led, flows shifted strongly to passive and ETFs, and junk-bond covenants were weak (about 60% of June junk issuance had few protections per Moody's). Emerging-market debt yielded below US high yield, private equity fundraising set records, SoftBank raised its Vision Fund, and Bitcoin and Ether surged. The Fed had begun a series of rate increases, and cash returns were near zero.

## Notable Claims

- The Shiller CAPE was almost 30 versus a median of 16, exceeded only in 1929 and 2000.
- The VIX was at the lowest level in its 27-year history.
- Passive covers 37% of equity fund assets; $1.4 trillion flowed into index funds and ETFs over ten years.
- Argentina issued $2.75 billion of 100-year bonds at 8% despite five defaults in the past century.
- Ether appreciated 4,500% in 2017 to date.

## Quotes

> I think it’s better to turn cautious too soon (and thus perhaps underperform for a while) rather than too late, after the downslide has begun, making it hard to trim risk, achieve exits and cut losses.

> The bottom line is that while the prices and prospective returns on many things are justifiable today relative to other things, you can’t eat (or spend) relative returns.

> That’s what happens when there’s too much money and too little fear.
