# Volatility + Leverage = Dynamite

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** December 17, 2008 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2008-12-17-volatility-leverage-dynamite.pdf)

## Summary

Written at the depth of the 2008 crisis, this memo develops a formula Marks first proposed in 1994: combining volatile or risky assets with leverage is explosive. He shows with simple numerical examples how a 30x levered mortgage book is wiped out by 20% defaults with two-thirds recovery, while 2% defaults or 10x leverage would have been survivable. The safe amount of leverage depends on the riskiness and volatility of the assets; utilities and life insurers can carry high leverage, while MBIA should not have insured $75 billion of mortgage paper on $3 billion of capital. Residential mortgages caused the damage because houses lack cash flows that anchor value, and because structured vehicles rated most of their debt triple-A on the assumption that mortgages were uncorrelated. He discusses how rare events lead to relaxed assumptions, quotes Taleb's Russian roulette passage, and argues one cannot plan for the worst case but must survive every moment. He admits some Oaktree evergreen funds were hurt by leverage on bank loans and concludes leverage is a two-edged sword best used in the trough of the cycle.

## Key Ideas

1. The prudent amount of leverage is purely a function of the riskiness and volatility of the assets it finances.
2. Financial institutions failed by combining risky assets they thought were safe (triple-A mortgage paper) with high leverage.
3. Assets without cash flows, like houses, oil or gold, have no anchor for value; they are worth only what someone will pay.
4. Structured mortgage vehicles rated about 90% of their capital above the underlying collateral on the false assumption that mortgages were uncorrelated.
5. A no-documentation 100% mortgage in a soaring market is not a secured loan but an option on future appreciation.
6. Long periods without disaster make risk assumptions look too conservative, tempting relaxation just before risk appears.
7. You cannot plan for the worst case, but you must survive every moment, not just on average (the six-foot man in a five-foot stream).
8. Distinguish permanent leverage from leverage that can be withdrawn; margin-callable leverage adds risk of ruin.

## Market Context

December 2008, after Lehman Brothers' bankruptcy and the bailout debate. Leverage that was too easy to obtain two years earlier had become virtually unavailable, performing bank loans traded in the 60s, high yield was down 30% for the year, and oil had fallen from $147 in mid-2008 to $42.

## Notable Claims

- Goldman Sachs reported in July 2008 that capital providers were underwater at 50 of 52 banks that raised capital.
- MBIA insured $75bn of mortgage paper on $3bn of total capital.
- In a typical CDO, 80% of debt was rated triple-A and 97% investment grade.
- The high yield index fell 30% in 2008 versus a prior worst year of -7%.
- The Case-Shiller index was down 26% from its July 2006 peak.

## Quotes

> It’s not enough to survive in the investment world on average; you have to survive every moment.

> That’s not a secured loan; it’s an option on future appreciation.

> Leverage is neither good nor bad in and of itself.
