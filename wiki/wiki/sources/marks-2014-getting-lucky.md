# Getting Lucky

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** January 16, 2014 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2014-01-16-getting-lucky.pdf)

## Summary

Marks rejects 'you make your own luck' and argues that luck, including Gladwell's 'demographic luck' of being born at the right time and place, plays a large part in success. He lists the lucky breaks in his own career: baby-boom timing, Wharton, Chicago just as modern finance theory arrived, Citibank, being handed the new high yield field in 1978, and his partners. In investing, performance is what happens when events collide with an existing portfolio, so a decision's correctness can't be judged from its outcome. Taking a poker anecdote from Mauboussin ('find weak games'), he argues that the easiest way to win is to stick to inefficient markets. He restates his view of efficiency as prices that are the best estimate but not necessarily right, treats efficiency as a 'rebuttable presumption', and traces Oaktree's success to the once-inefficient high yield and distressed debt markets. With 'efficientization' driven by abundant data, computing power and about 8,000 hedge funds, structural inefficiency is now scarce. Cyclical inefficiency will keep recurring when investors panic.

## Key Ideas

1. Success depends on hard work and on luck; demographic luck (timing and place of birth) explains clusters such as the tech founders born 1953-56.
2. Innate talent is itself luck; Buffett's 'Ovarian Lottery' and Marks's own career show how much lies outside one's control.
3. Performance is what happens when events collide with an existing portfolio; to be right and make money, the view must be correct, the events must occur, and they must occur on schedule.
4. Outcomes don't validate decisions: investors can be right for the wrong reason, wrong for the wrong reason, or too early (indistinguishable from wrong).
5. Find weak games: success has two aspects, skill and the choice of game, and the easiest wins come in inefficient markets.
6. Efficiency should be a rebuttable presumption; no market is perfectly efficient because participants are not objective and unemotional.
7. 'Efficientization' erodes free lunches: data, computing power, the end of moral taboos and ~8,000 hedge funds make structural inefficiency rare.
8. Cyclical inefficiency persists, because markets become absurdly cheap whenever investors turn tail and run.

## Market Context

January 2014, five years into the post-crisis recovery. Marks notes that the absurdly cheap opportunities of five years earlier (2008-09) were mostly gone, and that information and computing were now ubiquitous.

## Notable Claims

- Bruce Karsh's distressed debt record: 23% gross, 17½% net per year since inception, unlevered, over 25 years and $35 billion.
- Karsh earned that return over 25 years while investing $35 billion.
- Sheldon Stone beat his high yield benchmarks risk-adjusted net over 28 years.
- About 8,000 hedge funds exist worldwide.
- Moody's defined a B-rated bond as failing to possess the characteristics of a desirable investment.
