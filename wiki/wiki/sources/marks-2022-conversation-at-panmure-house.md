# Conversation at Panmure House

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** June 23, 2022 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/panmure-house.pdf)

## Summary

An edited transcript of a video interview Marks gave Patrick Schotanus for an Edinburgh Business School symposium on cognitive economics and the Market Mind Hypothesis, held at Panmure House, Adam Smith's last home. Marks argues that neither economies nor markets are mechanical: people have moods, so the pendulum is a mood swing rather than a predictable physical process. He offers his own version of the efficient market hypothesis (prices are as close to right as people can get, so mistakes are hard to exploit), explains cycles as excesses and corrections driven by psychology, and recounts two episodes: the 2007 'race to the bottom' in lending and the October 2008 'limits to negativism' conversation with a client. He also discusses where superior returns can come from, why history rhymes, passive investing as free-riding, and his view that computers can beat most investors but not the best.

## Key Ideas

1. Economics and investing are not mechanical sciences; human emotion makes outcomes unpredictable ('physics would be much harder if electrons had feelings').
2. Marks's version of the EMH: prices are as close to right as investors collectively can get, so few can identify and profit from the mistakes.
3. Markets swing from 'flawless to hopeless' through mass hysteria; S&P 500 annual returns are rarely near the 10½% average.
4. Cycles are excesses and corrections around a trend, driven by swings between optimism and pessimism, greed and fear, risk tolerance and risk aversion.
5. The most valuable single fact about a security would be how much optimism is in its price; prices reflect fundamentals plus how people view them.
6. Skepticism in a panic means saying 'that's too bad to be true'; in October 2008 Oaktree invested heavily as negativism peaked.
7. Superior returns need either a better view of the future or a better reading of qualitative information, including market mood.
8. Passive investing free-rides on active price discovery; the fewer active investors, the more price can diverge from value.

## Market Context

Memo dated June 23, 2022; the interview was shown at the symposium on May 24 (2022). It is reflective rather than a market call. It references the pandemic, Marks's 2018 book 'Mastering the Market Cycle', and the growth of passive, algorithmic and AI-based investing.

## Notable Claims

- CRSP found S&P 500 returned 9.2% a year 1928-62.
- UK banks raised mortgage lending from three-and-a-half to four or five times salary before the GFC.
- A levered senior loan fund offered a potential 26% return; even 13% annual defaults would leave 13%.
- Black Monday fell 22.6% eight days after the 'Why This Market Cycle Isn't Different' article.
- Computers can beat most people but not the best.

## Quotes

> in the real world, things fluctuate between pretty good and not so hot. But in the markets, they go from flawless to hopeless.

> if I could know just one thing about every security I was thinking about buying, it would be how much optimism is in the price.

> active investing is no good; passive investing works better, but only if people keep doing active investing.
