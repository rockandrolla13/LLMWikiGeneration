# Microeconomics 101: Supply, Demand and Convertibles

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** October 8, 1992 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/1992-10-08-microeconomics.pdf)

## Summary

Marks argues that investment success depends on two things, the quality of the asset and the price paid, and that price matters more for his group because they work in convertibles, high yield and distressed debt where assets are not top quality. Price is set by supply and demand, so buying when everyone else is buying can doom an investment, while buying what nobody wants tends to produce bargains. He applies this to convertible mutual funds: heavy inflows in 1985-87 led to overpaying and poor results, and by 1992 assets had shrunk sharply, which he reads as a sign that convertibles are cheap and attractive for contrarians.

## Key Ideas

1. Two factors drive investment outcomes: the intrinsic quality of the asset and the price paid for it.
2. Price outranks absolute quality for Oaktree's group: 'everything is triple-A at the right price'.
3. Excess investor demand pushes prices too high; 1980s real estate and later LBOs failed partly for this reason.
4. Heavy fund inflows are a precursor of poor performance, and low inflows are a precursor of good performance.
5. Convertible fund assets fell from about $5.8 billion in 1987 to about $2.2 billion in 1992, signalling neglect and bargains.
6. Convertibles capture much of equity upside while being cushioned on the downside, which suits a period of high uncertainty.

## Market Context

Written in October 1992. Strong demand, including mutual fund inflows from cash leaving low-yielding short-term investments, was supporting stock prices, while convertible funds attracted little money. Marks notes high uncertainty at the time and refers back to failed 1980s real estate and late-1980s LBOs and the 1987 crash.

## Notable Claims

- Oaktree's view that any asset is a good buy at a low enough price.
- Convertible fund count and assets grew thirteen-fold 1977-1987.
- Convertible fund assets fell 62% from the 1987 level.
- Inflows predict poor performance and vice versa.
- Convertible funds matched the S&P 500 in 1991.

## Quotes

> everything is triple-A at the right price

> buying what no one else will buy at any price almost assures eventual success

> Convertibles are getting no respect and attracting no inflows.
