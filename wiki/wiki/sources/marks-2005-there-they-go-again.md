# There They Go Again

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** May 6, 2005 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2005-05-06-there-they-go-again.pdf)

## Summary

Opening with Galbraith on 'the extreme brevity of the financial memory', Marks catalogues the recurring mistakes that drive bubbles, from 'it's different this time' to 'if it stops working, I'll get out'. He then applies the list to the new darlings of 2005: residential and commercial real estate, hedge funds, private equity mega-funds and crude oil. Home buyers were speculating with floating-rate, low-down-payment mortgages while rents fell relative to mortgage payments, and property cap rates and spreads had narrowed sharply. With the capital market line 'low and flat', he reviews the options open to investors and argues that the one clear error is reaching for return by adding risk when risk premiums are thinnest. Defensive investing will miss the exciting end of bull markets but makes you a long-term survivor.

## Key Ideas

1. Investors repeat the same mistakes in new assets because financial memory is short and greed trumps caution, especially after following the rules has cost money.
2. A list of recurring errors: 'it's different this time', 'it can't miss', simple stories, trees growing to the sky, today's positives lasting, extrapolating past returns, 'always beats the cost of borrowing', ignoring supply and demand, 'higher risk means higher return', 'anything's better than cash', fear of missing out, and 'I'll get out in time'.
3. Good results attract buyers who raise prices, which lowers future returns and raises risk.
4. Real estate in 2005 showed bubble behaviour: speculative home purchases, falling rent-to-mortgage ratios and lower cap rates, with property yields only about 1 point over the 10-year Treasury.
5. Hedge fund and private equity investors were ignoring the price of manager services, with mega-funds targeting their lowest returns ever.
6. Commodities such as oil lack a clear intrinsic value, so prices can swing far more than fundamentals.
7. With a 'low and flat' capital market line, reaching for return means taking risk when others compete to take it, which is exactly the wrong time.
8. Defensive pillars: awareness of history, belief in cycles, skepticism about free lunches, and insistence on low purchase prices that leave room for error.

## Market Context

May 2005: low interest rates and risk premiums, a housing boom funded by easy floating-rate mortgages, record private equity fundraising, a proliferation of hedge funds after the 2000-02 equity bear market, and crude oil in the $50s. Marks's prior memo 'Risk and Return Today' (October 27, 2004) had described the capital market line as low and flat.

## Notable Claims

- Rent fell from 102% to 92% of mortgage payments between 2001 and 2005.
- Average home prices rose 16.4% in 2003-4 while rents were flat (M/PF YieldStar).
- Property cash-on-cash returns had averaged 3.3 points over the 10-year Treasury, versus about 1 point in 2005 (Grant).
- Private equity managers who raised $3-6 billion funds were raising $8-10 billion.
- Oil traded at $51 a barrel, a month after $58.

## Quotes

> statements including the words “always” and “never” are usually a sign of trouble ahead

> You want to take risk when others are fleeing from it, not when they’re competing with you to do so.

> “The market’s not a very accommodating machine; it won’t provide high returns just because you need them.”
