# Now What?

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** January 10, 2008 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2008-01-10-now-what.pdf)

## Summary

Written as the subprime crisis was becoming a full credit crunch, the memo first recaps the 2003-07 boom and then ventures views on what comes next. Low rates after the 2001-02 telecom and accounting scandals pushed investors into leverage, alternatives and financial innovation, while non-bank lending, securitization, tranching and generous ratings (especially on CDOs of subprime mortgages) removed traditional restraints. Marks attributes the bust to the excesses of the boom rather than the subprime spark, and traces the 2007 chain from rising defaults to CDO downgrades, forced selling, SIV losses and bank capital worries. Looking forward, he expects slower growth driven by weaker consumers, scarcer capital and cautious business, notes the limits on what Fed rate cuts can do, and highlights the monoline insurers as a risk to AAA-insured munis. Corporate defaults had not yet risen, but he expects aggressive buyouts and issuer-friendly debt to produce distress later. He judges it the 'second or third inning' and recommends modest, cautious buying, since cash will be king.

## Key Ideas

1. Boom/bust: busts are better attributed to the preceding boom's excesses than to the specific spark (here subprime mortgages).
2. The boom required greed, optimism, credulity and risk tolerance; cycles ensure these eventually give way to fear, scepticism and risk aversion.
3. Borrowing short to invest long or illiquid sets up fire sales: in a crisis you sell what you can, not what you want to sell.
4. The Fed can cut rates but cannot make people borrow, spend or take risk ('pushing on a string'); inflation, dollar reserves and moral hazard limit its freedom.
5. Monoline insurers that guaranteed CDOs threatened the AAA status of insured munis, an 'isolated development' with wide ramifications.
6. Issuer-friendly debt (PIK toggles, covenant-lite) delays defaults but does not prevent them, and financial engineers rebuild risk to fill their 'risk budget'.
7. Fire sales enrich distressed buyers and clear problems faster; Oaktree's 1990 and 2002 distressed funds earned net IRRs in the 30s and 40s from forced sellers.
8. In the early innings, explore and act modestly with a fraction of reserves; 'don't catch a falling knife' can be an excuse for not thinking.

## Market Context

January 2008. Subprime defaults had led to CDO downgrades from July 2007, margin calls, SIV and conduit failures, bank write-downs and capital raises (Citigroup from Abu Dhabi), Northern Rock's rescue, and a credit crunch by mid-November. The Fed had cut rates in September (50 bp) and December. High yield spreads had moved from all-time lows in June 2007 to 'normal' by November; corporate defaults were still near zero.

## Notable Claims

- High yield default rates have averaged 4.2% over 20+ years but were near 25-year lows, and Oaktree's high yield portfolios were in their 47th month without a default.
- Oaktree's distressed debt funds formed in 1990 and 2002 earned net IRRs in the 30s and 40s.
- Monoline insurers guaranteed payments on $2 trillion of municipal bonds with relatively little capital.
- Defaults are believed to rise significantly when growth falls below 2% a year.
- Marks places the crisis in the second or third inning.

## Quotes

> The air always goes out of the balloon a lot faster than it went in.

> In times of crisis, you sell what you can sell, not what you want to sell.

> In the period ahead, cash will be king
