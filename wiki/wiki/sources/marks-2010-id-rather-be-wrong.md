# I'd Rather Be Wrong

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** March 17, 2010 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2010-03-17-id-rather-be-wrong.pdf)

## Summary

A short memo about US political gridlock and its threat to long-term fiscal health, rather than about markets directly. Marks argues that deficits, national debt, healthcare costs, Social Security, Medicare and underfunded public pensions are long-term problems. Fixing them needs short-term pain that politicians focused on re-election will not accept. Drawing on articles by Jackie Calmes in The New York Times and David Wessel in The Wall Street Journal, he describes hardened positions on both sides: no new taxes on one side and no domestic spending cuts on the other. He also points to virtual filibusters that raise the threshold for action to 60 votes. He stresses that the criticism applies to both parties. His proposed remedy is for voters to demand compromise and to elect moderates.

## Key Ideas

1. Long-term fiscal problems get worse the longer they are left, but the pain of fixing them falls on today's politicians while the benefit comes after they leave.
2. Hardened polar positions, such as no-tax pledges against no spending cuts, leave no zone of agreement, like a seller at $20 and a buyer at $18.
3. Public pension underfunding gets hidden by raising assumed returns on assets above already-challenging levels of 8% or more.
4. Gridlock is not a partisan point: the obstructing party will switch when power changes.
5. Virtual filibusters have turned 60 Senate votes into the effective threshold, and supermajorities produce inaction rather than better decisions.
6. Some problems were created by government and only government can solve them, which requires leaders to explain why compromise is needed.

## Market Context

March 2010, after the 2008-09 financial crisis and deep recession. Democrats controlled the White House and both houses of Congress, health-care legislation had stalled, deficits were near $1 trillion a year, baby boomers were starting to retire, and the November 2010 midterms were approaching. A deficit commission co-chaired by Alan Simpson had been formed.

## Notable Claims

- Federal deficits were averaging $1 trillion a year, the largest since WWII relative to GDP (quoted from Calmes).
- CBO projected the deficit rising from 10% of GDP to over 20% by 2020 and over 40% by 2080 (quoted from Wessel).
- Pension funds may raise return assumptions above 8% to mask underfunding.
- Filibusters have made 60 votes the effective Senate threshold.

## Quotes

> This memo will be about one of the inarguably most depressing topics of our time: the seeming inability of governments and politicians to solve – or even tackle – the financial problems we face.

> Let’s tell our elected officials we want solutions, not warfare; compromise, not intransigence.

> Many of today’s problems are government-created, so government will have to solve them.
