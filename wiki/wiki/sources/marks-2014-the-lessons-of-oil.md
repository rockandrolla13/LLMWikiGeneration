# The Lessons of Oil

**Author:** Howard Marks · **Date:** December 18, 2014 · **Type:** memo (article) · **Original:** [oaktreecapital.com](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2014-12-18-the-lessons-of-oil.pdf)

## Summary

Marks uses the fall of more than 40% in oil prices in late 2014 to draw lessons about forecasting and contagion, not to call the right oil price. Investors had spent eighteen months asking when the Fed would raise rates, and they were wrong on that while ignoring oil. He describes "the failure of imagination": forecasts extrapolate from current levels and underestimate how big changes can be. Investors also miss second- and third-order effects, and the list of ramifications runs from oil-exporting countries through airlines, autos and drillers to bank loans. Lower prices also correct themselves by raising demand and cutting supply. He notes that energy had become the largest sector of the high yield bond market, which made contagion into credit likely. He argues that cartels cannot hold prices forever and that it is hard to value an asset that produces no income. He closes by saying the new disarray lets Oaktree drop some caution and become more aggressive for the first time in 3½ years.

## Key Ideas

1. What everyone knows is usually unhelpful or wrong; the herd had the rate outlook wrong and was asking about the wrong variable.
2. Prices allow for the risks people know about; the risks nobody has thought of are the ones that knock markets for a loop.
3. Failure of imagination: forecasts stay close to current levels and underestimate the size of rare changes.
4. Second-order effects and portfolio 'fault lines' spread shocks into unrelated assets through capital withdrawals and the need for liquidity; in panics correlations go to one.
5. Low prices sow the seeds of higher prices by raising demand and cutting supply, which shows that economics is dynamic.
6. Cartels and other anti-market mechanisms cannot hold prices above market levels forever.
7. Assets that produce no income are hard to value, which makes it hard to know whether you have a margin of safety.
8. Easy credit issuance followed by a negative igniter produces the distress cycle; Oaktree's best distressed buying came from three such debt crises.

## Market Context

December 2014. Brent crude had fallen from around $110 six months earlier to about $60, and Saudi Arabia had stopped cutting output to balance the market. The market had been fixated on the timing of Fed rate rises after Bernanke's 2013 taper remarks. Central-bank-suppressed yields had pushed investors out the risk curve, and selling in energy high yield bonds and emerging markets was spreading.

## Notable Claims

- The 10-year Treasury yield fell from about 3% to 2.2% despite a consensus that rates would rise.
- Oil fell more than 40% in a few months on a 93 million barrel-a-day base.
- Oil had been above $100 for 43 consecutive months.
- Energy became the largest sector in the high yield bond market.
- The three debt crises in 26 years of Oaktree's distressed investing gave the best buying opportunities.

## Quotes

> Asset prices are often set to allow for the risks people are aware of. It’s the ones they haven’t thought of that can knock the market for a loop.

> If you think markets are logical and investors are objective and unemotional, you’re in for a lot of surprises.

> The key lesson here may be that cartels and other anti-market mechanisms can’t hold forever.
