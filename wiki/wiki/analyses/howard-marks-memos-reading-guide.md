# Howard Marks Memos Reading Guide

## Overview

This Howard Marks reading guide is the entry point to the 165 Howard Marks memos in the wiki. The memos run from "The Route to Performance" (October 1990) to "A Layman Thinks About AI" (November 2026). The guide does three jobs. It places each memo in its era. It groups the memos into twelve recurring themes. It shows where Marks's views on risk, cycles, forecasting, value and AI shifted over time.

Every statement below comes from the memo source pages and their extraction notes. Each source page holds a summary, key ideas, market context, notable claims and quotes. Read the source page before quoting Marks.

**Coverage caveats.**

- The collection is not the full Oaktree archive. "Taking the Temperature" (2023) cites a March 2012 memo, "Déjà Vu All Over Again", that is not in the collection.
- "Calibrating" (April 2020) refers to four March 2020 memos. The collection holds three.
- The 1990s are thin: 15 memos across ten years. 2008 (10 memos) and 2020 (12 memos) are the densest years.

## Timeline of the Memos by Era

### 1990-1999: TCW years, the founding of Oaktree, the late-1990s boom (15 memos)

Marks wrote the early memos at Trust Company of the West (TCW), before Oaktree. The backdrop was the 1989-90 high yield bond collapse and the fall of Drexel Burnham. The Fed's February 1994 rate rise set off a bond rout. Oaktree opened on April 10, 1995. The decade ends with the Asian crisis, the Long-Term Capital Management (LTCM) rescue and the dot-com bull market.

- [[sources/marks-1990-the-route-to-performance|The Route to Performance]] (1990): his earliest memo. Consistency beats bold bets.
- [[sources/marks-1991-first-quarter-performance|First Quarter Performance]] (1991): first use of the pendulum metaphor.
- [[sources/marks-1993-the-value-of-predictions|The Value of Predictions]] (1993): his case against forecast-based investing.
- [[sources/marks-1994-risk-in-todays-markets|Risk in Today's Markets]] (1994) and [[sources/marks-1994-risk-in-todays-markets-revisited|its follow-up]] (1994): the "food chain" of rising risk-taking, then the 1994 rout.
- [[sources/marks-1995-how-the-game-should-be-played|How the Game Should Be Played]] (1995): the Oaktree game plan, written just after the founding.
- [[sources/marks-1996-will-it-be-different-this-time|Will It Be Different This Time?]] (1996) and [[sources/marks-1998-genius-isnt-enough|Genius Isn't Enough]] (1998, LTCM).

### 2000-2002: The dot-com bust, 9/11 and Enron (14 memos)

The Nasdaq peaked on March 10, 2000. The 2000-02 bear market, the September 11 attacks and the Enron, WorldCom and Arthur Andersen scandals followed.

- [[sources/marks-2000-bubble-com|bubble.com]] (January 2000): the first of the five market calls he lists in 2023.
- [[sources/marks-2000-irrational-exuberance|Irrational Exuberance]] (2000) and [[sources/marks-2000-were-not-in-1999-anymore-toto|We're Not In 1999 Anymore, Toto]] (2000): post-mortems on the bubble.
- [[sources/marks-2001-you-cant-predict-you-can-prepare|You Can't Predict. You Can Prepare.]] (2001): his core treatment of the five cycles.
- [[sources/marks-2002-learning-from-enron|Learning From Enron]] (2002) and [[sources/marks-2002-the-realists-creed|The Realist's Creed]] (2002).

### 2003-2006: Low rates and the pre-crisis credit boom (21 memos)

Rates sat at multi-decade lows. Institutions reached for return through hedge funds, private equity and structured credit. Risk premiums thinned. Marks turned more defensive from late 2004.

- [[sources/marks-2003-the-most-important-thing|The Most Important Thing]] (2003): Oaktree's precepts in one place.
- [[sources/marks-2004-the-happy-medium|The Happy Medium]] (2004) and [[sources/marks-2004-risk-and-return-today|Risk and Return Today]] (2004).
- [[sources/marks-2005-there-they-go-again|There They Go Again]] (2005), [[sources/marks-2006-risk|Risk]] (2006) and [[sources/marks-2006-dare-to-be-great|Dare to Be Great]] (2006).

### 2007-2009: The global financial crisis (20 memos)

Subprime defaults began the 2007 credit crunch. Bear Stearns collapsed in March 2008 and Lehman failed in September 2008. 2008 alone has ten memos, the densest crisis record in the collection.

- [[sources/marks-2007-the-race-to-the-bottom|The Race to the Bottom]] (February 2007) and [[sources/marks-2007-its-all-good|It's All Good]] (July 2007): written near the peak.
- [[sources/marks-2008-nobody-knows|Nobody Knows]] (2008), [[sources/marks-2008-the-limits-to-negativism|The Limits to Negativism]] (2008) and [[sources/marks-2008-volatility-leverage-dynamite|Volatility + Leverage = Dynamite]] (2008).
- [[sources/marks-2009-the-long-view|The Long View]] (2009): his admission that he missed the longer cycle.

### 2010-2015: Recovery, zero rates and the euro crisis (30 memos)

Near-zero rates and quantitative easing pushed investors back into risk. The euro-zone debt crisis, the 2011 US debt-ceiling standoff and S&P's downgrade, and the 2014 oil collapse were the main shocks. Marks says markets entered "the zone of imprudence" by 2014-15.

- [[sources/marks-2010-open-and-shut|Open and Shut]] (2010) on the credit cycle and [[sources/marks-2013-ditto|Ditto]] (2013) on why his themes repeat.
- [[sources/marks-2014-risk-revisited|Risk Revisited]] (2014), [[sources/marks-2015-risk-revisited-again|Risk Revisited Again]] (2015) and [[sources/marks-2015-its-not-easy|It's Not Easy]] (2015).

### 2016-2019: Populism, the late bull market and negative rates (18 memos)

Brexit and the 2016 US election pulled Marks into political economy. The bull market became the longest on record. Passive investing grew, and negative yields spread in Europe and Japan.

- [[sources/marks-2016-economic-reality|Economic Reality]] (2016) and [[sources/marks-2017-there-they-go-again-again|There They Go Again . . . Again]] (2017).
- [[sources/marks-2018-investing-without-people|Investing Without People]] (2018), his first sustained look at AI and machine learning.
- [[sources/marks-2019-this-time-its-different|This Time It's Different]] (2019) and [[sources/marks-2019-mysterious|Mysterious]] (2019) on negative rates.

### 2020-2021: Covid-19 (16 memos)

The S&P 500 fell 34% from February 19 to March 23, 2020. It recovered within five months on Fed and Treasury support. 2020 has twelve memos, many written weeks apart.

- [[sources/marks-2020-nobody-knows-ii|Nobody Knows II]], [[sources/marks-2020-which-way-now|Which Way Now?]], [[sources/marks-2020-calibrating|Calibrating]] and [[sources/marks-2020-uncertainty|Uncertainty]] (all 2020).
- [[sources/marks-2021-something-of-value|Something of Value]] (2021): his rethink of value versus growth.

### 2022-2024: Inflation, rate hikes and the "sea change" (19 memos)

Inflation hit a 40-year high and the Fed hiked fast. Russia invaded Ukraine. Silicon Valley Bank failed in March 2023. Marks argued the 2009-21 low-rate era had ended.

- [[sources/marks-2022-sea-change|Sea Change]] (2022) and [[sources/marks-2023-further-thoughts-on-sea-change|Further Thoughts on Sea Change]] (2023).
- [[sources/marks-2022-the-illusion-of-knowledge|The Illusion of Knowledge]] (2022) and [[sources/marks-2023-taking-the-temperature|Taking the Temperature]] (2023).
- [[sources/marks-2024-easy-money|Easy Money]] (2024) and [[sources/marks-2024-shall-we-repeal-the-laws-of-economics|Shall We Repeal the Laws of Economics?]] (2024).

### 2025-2026: AI, tariffs and private credit (12 memos)

The April 2025 US tariffs, autumn 2025 credit failures such as First Brands and Tricolor, and the AI capital-spending boom dominate.

- [[sources/marks-2025-on-bubble-watch|On Bubble Watch]] (January 2025) and [[sources/marks-2025-is-it-a-bubble|Is It a Bubble?]] (December 2025).
- [[sources/marks-2025-cockroaches-in-the-coal-mine|Cockroaches in the Coal Mine]] (2025) and [[sources/marks-2026-whats-going-on-in-private-credit|What's Going on in Private Credit?]] (2026).
- [[sources/marks-2026-ai-hurtles-ahead|AI Hurtles Ahead]] (2026) and [[sources/marks-2026-a-layman-thinks-about-ai|A Layman Thinks About AI]] (2026).

## Recurring Themes and the Memos That Cover Them Best

### 1. Market cycles and the pendulum of investor psychology

Market cycles and the pendulum of investor psychology are Marks's most frequent subject. The concept page [[concepts/pendulum-of-investor-psychology]] cites 56 memos and [[concepts/market-cycles]] cites 37. His claim is that psychology spends most of its time at the extremes, not at the happy medium. Cycles are chains of events in which each event causes the next. You cannot predict a cycle's timing, but you can know where you stand in it.

- [[sources/marks-1991-first-quarter-performance|First Quarter Performance]] (1991): the pendulum metaphor introduced.
- [[sources/marks-2001-you-cant-predict-you-can-prepare|You Can't Predict. You Can Prepare.]] (2001): five cycles and the general rules of cycles.
- [[sources/marks-2004-the-happy-medium|The Happy Medium]] (2004): the paired swings of greed and fear.
- [[sources/marks-2006-it-is-what-it-is|It Is What It Is]] (2006): know where we stand, via the "visitor from Mars".
- [[sources/marks-2013-ditto|Ditto]] (2013): the cycle in attitudes toward risk.
- [[sources/marks-2023-taking-the-temperature|Taking the Temperature]] (2023): reading the cycle in practice.

### 2. Risk is the chance of permanent loss, not volatility

Marks defines risk as the possibility of permanent loss, not volatility. He calls volatility an academic convenience that people use because it can be measured. He argues risk cannot be measured even after the fact. He treats the future as a probability distribution. He separates risk control from risk avoidance. See [[concepts/risk-vs-volatility]] and [[contradictions/risk-equals-volatility]].

- [[sources/marks-2006-risk|Risk]] (2006): the first full memo on investment risk.
- [[sources/marks-2014-risk-revisited|Risk Revisited]] (2014): four key points and 24 forms of risk.
- [[sources/marks-2015-risk-revisited-again|Risk Revisited Again]] (2015): the expanded version, which he meant to hold everything he knows about risk.
- [[sources/marks-2010-warning-flags|Warning Flags]] (2010): the two risks, losing money and missing opportunity.
- [[sources/marks-2024-the-indispensability-of-risk|The Indispensability of Risk]] (2024): high returns require risk.

### 3. The limits of macro forecasting

Marks argues that a macro forecast only adds value if it is both correct and different from the consensus. He finds such forecasts rare. He contrasts the "I know" school with the "I don't know" school he belongs to. He applies Buffett's test that useful information must be important and knowable. See [[concepts/limits-of-macro-forecasting]] and [[concepts/i-know-vs-i-dont-know-school]].

- [[sources/marks-1993-the-value-of-predictions|The Value of Predictions]] (1993): every objection to forecast-based investing.
- [[sources/marks-1996-the-value-of-predictions-ii|The Value of Predictions II]] (1996): he checks Wall Street Journal economist surveys against outcomes.
- [[sources/marks-2003-whad-ya-know|Whad'Ya Know?]] (2003): a rant against the "I know" school.
- [[sources/marks-2021-thinking-about-macro|Thinking About Macro]] (2021): important but not knowable.
- [[sources/marks-2022-the-illusion-of-knowledge|The Illusion of Knowledge]] (2022): why macro forecasting fails, at both inputs and process.
- [[sources/marks-2025-nobody-knows-yet-again|Nobody Knows (Yet Again)]] (2025): forecasting under the 2025 tariffs.

### 4. Second-level thinking, contrarianism and daring to be different

Second-level thinking asks what the consensus believes and what the price already reflects. Marks says superior results need a view that is both non-consensus and right. He says contrarian investing pays at extremes, which differs from always disagreeing with the consensus. His "Dare to Be Great" 2x2 matrix shows that only unconventional behaviour can produce above-average results. See [[concepts/second-level-thinking]] and [[concepts/contrarian-investing]].

- [[sources/marks-2015-its-not-easy|It's Not Easy]] (2015): his fullest restatement of second-level thinking.
- [[sources/marks-2006-dare-to-be-great|Dare to Be Great]] (2006): the conventional/unconventional matrix.
- [[sources/marks-2014-dare-to-be-great-ii|Dare to Be Great II]] (2014): dare to be different, to be wrong and to look wrong.
- [[sources/marks-2007-everyone-knows|Everyone Knows]] (2007): what "everyone knows" is usually wrong.
- [[sources/marks-2022-i-beg-to-differ|I Beg to Differ]] (2022): a synthesis of the being-different theme.

### 5. Defensive investing: avoid the losers

Oaktree's motto is "If we avoid the losers, the winners will take care of themselves." Marks traces it to his first memo in 1990. Defensive investing means a little better than average every year, with few disasters. See [[concepts/defensive-investing]].

- [[sources/marks-1990-the-route-to-performance|The Route to Performance]] (1990): the pension plan that ranked in the fourth percentile over fourteen years.
- [[sources/marks-1995-how-the-game-should-be-played|How the Game Should Be Played]] (1995): Tom Kite versus Babe Ruth.
- [[sources/marks-2003-whats-your-game-plan|What's Your Game Plan?]] (2003): batting average over home runs.
- [[sources/marks-2005-oaktree-at-ten|Oaktree at Ten]] (2005): the motto as the firm's founding principle.
- [[sources/marks-2023-fewer-losers-or-more-winners|Fewer Losers, or More Winners?]] (2023): where the motto fits and where it does not.

### 6. Price versus value and market efficiency

Marks says success depends on the price paid relative to value, not on the quality of the asset. He accepts that mainstream markets are efficient in the sense of fast, not right. He argues inefficiency comes from investor bias. See [[concepts/price-vs-value]] and [[concepts/efficient-market-hypothesis]].

- [[sources/marks-1992-microeconomics-101-supply-demand-convertibles|Microeconomics 101]] (1992): price set by supply and demand.
- [[sources/marks-1994-how-does-an-inefficient-market-get-that-way|How Does an Inefficient Market Get That Way?]] (1994): bias as the cause of inefficiency.
- [[sources/marks-1997-are-you-an-investor-or-a-speculator|Are You An Investor or a Speculator?]] (1997): unpopular assets at compensating prices.
- [[sources/marks-2001-whats-it-all-about-alpha|What's It All About, Alpha?]] (2001): his working definitions of efficiency, alpha and beta.
- [[sources/marks-2021-something-of-value|Something of Value]] (2021): value versus growth.
- [[sources/marks-2025-the-calculus-of-value|The Calculus of Value]] (2025): where value comes from, applied to US stocks in mid-2025.

### 7. The credit cycle and the race to the bottom

Marks calls the credit cycle the most volatile and influential cycle. The credit window swings from wide open to slammed shut. In a race to the bottom, lenders compete by accepting lower returns and less protection. His "seven worst words" are "too much money chasing too few deals". See [[concepts/credit-cycle]] and [[concepts/race-to-the-bottom]].

- [[sources/marks-2007-the-race-to-the-bottom|The Race to the Bottom]] (2007): money as a commodity.
- [[sources/marks-2010-open-and-shut|Open and Shut]] (2010): the self-reinforcing credit sequence.
- [[sources/marks-2013-the-race-is-on|The Race Is On]] (2013): the race resumes under quantitative easing.
- [[sources/marks-2018-the-seven-worst-words-in-the-world|The Seven Worst Words in the World]] (2018).
- [[sources/marks-2026-whats-going-on-in-private-credit|What's Going on in Private Credit?]] (2026): the credit cycle applied to direct lending.

### 8. Bubbles and "this time is different"

Marks treats a bubble as a state of mind, not a calculation. He describes the three stages of a bull market. Newness removes history's limits on imagination. He argues that the belief "this time is different" is what makes it not different. See [[concepts/asset-bubbles]], [[concepts/this-time-is-different]] and [[concepts/dot-com-bubble]].

- [[sources/marks-1996-will-it-be-different-this-time|Will It Be Different This Time?]] (1996).
- [[sources/marks-2000-bubble-com|bubble.com]] (2000): the tech bubble set against the South Sea Bubble.
- [[sources/marks-2005-there-they-go-again|There They Go Again]] (2005): recurring bubble mistakes applied to 2005.
- [[sources/marks-2019-this-time-its-different|This Time It's Different]] (2019): nine optimistic propositions tested.
- [[sources/marks-2025-on-bubble-watch|On Bubble Watch]] (2025): the Magnificent Seven, 25 years after bubble.com.

### 9. Leverage, illiquidity and blow-ups

Marks's formula is that volatility plus leverage equals dynamite. He first proposed it in 1994 and developed it in December 2008. He uses fund collapses to show how leverage, concentration and illiquidity turn small risks into fatal ones. See [[concepts/leverage-and-volatility]] and [[concepts/liquidity-mismatch]].

- [[sources/marks-1998-genius-isnt-enough|Genius Isn't Enough]] (1998): lessons from Long-Term Capital Management.
- [[sources/marks-2005-a-case-in-point|A Case in Point]] (2005): the convertible arbitrage collapse.
- [[sources/marks-2006-pigweed|Pigweed]] (2006): Amaranth's natural gas losses.
- [[sources/marks-2008-volatility-leverage-dynamite|Volatility + Leverage = Dynamite]] (2008).
- [[sources/marks-2015-liquidity|Liquidity]] (2015): liquidity vanishes when you need it.
- [[sources/marks-2024-the-impact-of-debt|The Impact of Debt]] (2024).

### 10. Crises in real time: 2008 and 2020

The "Nobody Knows" memos show Marks writing during a crisis. In 2008 and in 2020 he says no one knows what comes next. In October 2008 he separates skepticism from pessimism. In April 2020 he summarises his March memos: buying was reasonable in proportion to how far prices had fallen. See [[concepts/global-financial-crisis]] and [[concepts/covid-19-market-crisis-2020]].

- [[sources/marks-2008-nobody-knows|Nobody Knows]] (2008): written days after Lehman failed.
- [[sources/marks-2008-the-limits-to-negativism|The Limits to Negativism]] (2008): skepticism is not pessimism.
- [[sources/marks-2009-the-long-view|The Long View]] (2009): the long cycle he missed.
- [[sources/marks-2020-nobody-knows-ii|Nobody Knows II]] (2020): facts, inferences and guesses about Covid-19.
- [[sources/marks-2020-calibrating|Calibrating]] (2020): adjusting portfolio stance in a panic.
- [[sources/marks-2020-knowledge-of-the-future|Knowledge of the Future]] (2020).

### 11. Interest rates and the sea change toward credit

Marks argues the roughly four-decade fall in interest rates after 1980 drove most investment profits. He says the 2022 rise in rates is a "sea change", not a normal cyclical swing. Lending now offers equity-like contractual returns, so he argues credit could be a large share of portfolios. See [[concepts/sea-change-in-interest-rates]] and [[concepts/debt-vs-ownership]].

- [[sources/marks-2019-mysterious|Mysterious]] (2019): negative interest rates.
- [[sources/marks-2022-sea-change|Sea Change]] (2022): the three sea changes of his career.
- [[sources/marks-2023-further-thoughts-on-sea-change|Further Thoughts on Sea Change]] (2023): credit as perhaps the majority of a portfolio.
- [[sources/marks-2024-easy-money|Easy Money]] (2024): ten ways ultra-low rates distort behaviour.
- [[sources/marks-2024-ruminating-on-asset-allocation|Ruminating on Asset Allocation]] (2024): ownership versus debt.
- [[sources/marks-2025-gimme-credit|Gimme Credit]] (2025): whether tight spreads still cover credit losses.

### 12. Artificial intelligence (AI) and investing without people

Marks's view on AI and machine learning is the main subject of four memos. He discusses AI as a tool that may replace investors, as a possible bubble, and as an undiversified "fault line" through the economy and credit markets. He disclaims technical expertise. See [[concepts/ai-capex-funding]] and [[concepts/ai-disruption-software-business-models]].

- [[sources/marks-2018-investing-without-people|Investing Without People]] (2018): passive, quant and AI investing.
- [[sources/marks-2025-is-it-a-bubble|Is It a Bubble?]] (2025): an AI bubble in company behaviour versus investor behaviour.
- [[sources/marks-2026-ai-hurtles-ahead|AI Hurtles Ahead]] (2026): written after he asked Anthropic's Claude for a tutorial on AI.
- [[sources/marks-2026-a-layman-thinks-about-ai|A Layman Thinks About AI]] (2026): eleven questions about investing in AI.

**A secondary theme: political economy.** From 2016 Marks writes about politics and economic law. Read [[sources/marks-2016-economic-reality|Economic Reality]] (2016), [[sources/marks-2016-political-reality|Political Reality]] (2016), [[sources/marks-2019-political-reality-meets-economic-reality|Political Reality Meets Economic Reality]] (2019) and the "Shall We Repeal the Laws of Economics?" series ([[sources/marks-2024-shall-we-repeal-the-laws-of-economics|2024]], [[sources/marks-2025-more-on-repealing-the-laws-of-economics|2025]], [[sources/marks-2026-shall-we-repeal-the-laws-of-economics-part-iii|2026]]).

## How Marks's Views Evolved

### Risk: from "avoid losers" to "the risk of taking too little risk"

His core definition of risk stays fixed from 2006 to 2015. Risk is the chance of permanent loss, and volatility is only a measurable proxy. The 2014-15 risk memos add a probability-distribution framing, a catalogue of 24 forms of risk, and "FOMO risk". The later memos shift the emphasis. "Fewer Losers, or More Winners?" (2023) says the avoid-losers motto fits bonds but not "aspirational" strategies such as distressed debt, which must find winners. It quotes Sheldon Stone: "If you don't experience any defaults, you're probably not taking enough credit risk." "The Indispensability of Risk" (2024) warns of the risk of taking too little risk.

### Cycles: from short cycles to long cycles and sea changes

The 1991 memo introduces the pendulum. The 2001 memo sets out five cycles inside a stable long-term trend. "The Long View" (January 2009) admits a miss: he read the 2003-07 cycle correctly but missed that it sat at the top of a much longer cycle, an "era of increasing willingness" to take risk. "Sea Change" (2022) goes further. It argues the end of ultra-low rates is not a cyclical swing but a regime change. "Further Thoughts on Sea Change" (2023) cites Templeton's allowance that "this time is different" may be true 20% of the time, and argues this is such a case.

### Forecasting: consistent rejection, with calls only at extremes

His rejection of macro forecasting holds from 1993 to 2025. Yet "Taking the Temperature" (2023) lists five market calls in 50 years: January 2000, late 2004 to mid-2007, late 2008, March 2012 and March 2020. He reconciles the two positions. Calls work only when markets are at extremes, and they come from reading investor psychology, not from macro data. In "Sea Change" (2022) he states an expectation that base rates will average 2-4%, but says Oaktree will not bet on it.

### Value and growth: a late revision

The early memos put price first. "Something of Value" (2021) is the clearest change of mind in the collection. After months of discussion with his son Andrew, a growth investor, Marks concedes three points. Low valuation metrics are not the same as being underpriced. Selling great compounders early is a mistake. He had a mean-reversion bias and an unexamined skepticism about cryptocurrencies.

### AI: from "computers beat most investors" to "a matter of faith"

- 2018: Marks concludes that computers will beat most investors. The best few, with qualitative long-term judgment, should keep a role.
- December 2025: he separates a bubble in company behaviour from one in investor behaviour. He flags growing AI debt and circular deals. He says enthusiasm is clearly high but irrationality cannot yet be known, and he recommends a moderate, selective position. A postscript worries about AI-driven job losses.
- February 2026: after a tutorial from Claude, he calls the technology real and likely underestimated. He adds that this does not make AI assets fairly priced. He says AI will raise the bar for active managers, while humans may keep an edge in novel situations and qualitative judgment.
- November 2026: he says an AI thesis must be right on many variables at once, so the joint probability is low. He calls the question of whether AI capex will earn adequate returns largely a matter of faith.

## Questions This Collection Answers Well

1. What does Marks mean by second-level thinking, and why does it matter?
2. How does Marks define risk, and why does he reject volatility as the measure?
3. What is the pendulum of investor psychology, and where does it sit at a given date?
4. Why does Marks distrust macro forecasts, and how does he square that with his five market calls?
5. What did Marks write during the 2008 crisis, before and after Lehman?
6. What did Marks write during the Covid-19 crash of 2020?
7. What lessons did Marks draw from LTCM, Amaranth, Enron and Silicon Valley Bank?
8. What are the signs of a bubble, and was the dot-com era one?
9. How does the credit cycle work, and what is the race to the bottom?
10. What is the 2022 sea change in interest rates, and why does it favour credit?
11. What is Marks's view on AI as an investment, and on AI as an investor?
12. What does Oaktree's motto "avoid the losers" mean, and where does Marks say it stops working?

**What the collection answers poorly.** The early 1990s are thin: 1991, 1992, 1993, 1995 and 1997 have one memo each. Questions about a specific year in that span may have no memo behind them.

## How to Search the Memos

The wiki search ranks section text only. It does not index page titles, headings or tags. So a question phrased with a concept name may miss the concept page whose body never repeats that name. Three workarounds help:

1. Start from this guide or from a concept page listed above.
2. Restrict a search to the memos with `wiki_query(wiki, "<question>", tags=["howard-marks"])`.
3. Phrase a question with words the memo body uses, such as "permanent loss" instead of "risk definition".
