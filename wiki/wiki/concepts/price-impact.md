# Price Impact

Buyers push prices up and sellers push them down. The whole of the field is disagreement about *why*, and the answer determines what impact costs you should expect to pay.

## Two Readings

**Information.** Traders are on average informed, so prices move to their predictions. This is the efficient-market reading, formalised in the Kyle model, which predicts impact that is **linear in volume and permanent**.

**Mechanical.** Prices move because people trade, whatever their reason. Impact is a statistical reaction of the book to incoming flow, and volatility is mostly generated by trading rather than by news. This is the order-driven view, argued at length by [[sources/maitrier-2026-square-root-impact-framework|Maitrier & Bouchaud]].

The empirical facts sit closer to the second. Metaorder impact is **concave, not linear** — it follows the [[concepts/square-root-law|square-root law]]. It depends on quantity executed and not on execution time. And it **decays** after execution ends. All three contradict Kyle.

## The Scale Problem

Price impact means different things at different scales, and the literature is easier to read once the scales are separated.

| Scale | Object | Shape | Typical evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Child order / book event | OFI against same-interval return | **Linear** | $R^2 \approx 90\%$ contemporaneously |
| Metaorder | Volume executed against peak impact | **Square root** | Broker and exchange ID datasets |
| Aggregate / mesoscopic | Generalized imbalance against return | Linear, with $a$-dependent scaling | Trade-by-trade tape |

The rows are [[concepts/order-flow-imbalance|order flow imbalance]], the [[concepts/metaorder|metaorder]], and the aggregate tape respectively.

These are not competing claims. [[sources/maitrier-2026-square-root-impact-framework|Maitrier & Bouchaud]] show a locally linear aggregate relation emerging from a superposition of overlapping square-root-impact metaorders, which is what reconciles the top and middle rows.

There is a second square root in this literature that is easy to confuse with the first. [[sources/cont-2014-price-impact-order-book-events|Cont, Kukanov & Stoikov (2014)]] derive a concave relation between price change and *traded volume over a fixed clock interval* from their linear OFI model alone: volume grows with the number of events, OFI grows with its square root, so price change against volume looks like a square root with a slope that is random from one interval to the next. They call it a statistical artefact of aggregation and show volume drops out once $|\text{OFI}|$ is controlled for. That is a claim about interval regressions on public data, not about the impact of a metaorder against its own executed size, which is what the square-root law proper describes.

## Contemporaneous versus Predictive

An important and easily-missed distinction, sharpest in [[sources/cont-2023-cross-impact-ofi|Cont, Cucuringu & Zhang]].

**Contemporaneous impact** explains the return over the *same* interval as the flow. It is very well explained — 84–90% out-of-sample $R^2$ with integrated OFI — and it is essentially a decomposition, not a forecast.

**Predictive impact** forecasts the *next* interval. Out-of-sample $R^2$ here is routinely **negative** across the whole literature, even for models that generate strong trading profits. Kelly et al.'s argument — that a systematically mis-scaled but correctly-signed forecast produces negative $R^2$ and positive expected returns — is the standard defence, and every paper in this cluster leans on it.

Never compare an $R^2$ from one column with an $R^2$ from the other.

## Measurement Without Identifiers

Metaorder impact studies need to know which child orders belong to which parent, which almost no dataset provides. [[sources/maitrier-2025-artificial-market-generator|Maitrier, Loeper & Bouchaud (2025)]] show inside a simulated market — where ground truth is known — that **proxy metaorders reconstructed from anonymous trades reproduce the square-root law including its prefactor**, provided the reconstruction preserves exact trade history and samples without replacement. The reconstruction degrades toward linear at small volumes.
