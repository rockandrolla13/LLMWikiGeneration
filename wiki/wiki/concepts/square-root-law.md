# Square-Root Law of Market Impact

The average price impact of a [[concepts/metaorder|metaorder]] of total volume $Q$ scales as the square root of that volume:

$$I(Q) = Y \sigma \sqrt{Q / V_D}$$

where $\sigma$ is volatility, $V_D$ daily volume and $Y$ an $O(1)$ constant. It holds across asset classes, venues and decades, which is why it is often called universal — and it has no consensus explanation.

## Why It Is Not Trivial

It looks like the random walk statement that price moves grow as $\sqrt{T}$, and that reading is wrong for three reasons.

1. $I(Q)$ is an **average price change**, not a standard deviation.
2. It depends on **quantity executed alone**, not on execution time. Trading the same volume twice as slowly does not halve the impact.
3. Impact **decays** after execution ends. It is transient, not permanent.

Each of these contradicts the Kyle model, which predicts linear and permanent impact from information revelation.

## Not the Same as the Interval Price–Volume Square Root

[[sources/cont-2014-price-impact-order-book-events|Cont, Kukanov & Stoikov (2014)]] also report a square root, but of a different object. Regressing ten-second price changes on traded volume over the same interval gives a concave fit, and they show this follows from linear impact in [[concepts/order-flow-imbalance|order flow imbalance]] by a central-limit argument: volume scales with the number of book events and OFI with its square root. The slope of that relation is a fresh random draw every interval, and volume loses all explanatory power once $|\text{OFI}|$ is included. The metaorder law is a statement about average impact against a single parent order's executed size, and neither result explains the other.

## The Reconciliation Problem

Three facts that individually are solid look jointly inconsistent:

- Metaorder impact is **concave and decaying**.
- Order signs have **long memory**, decaying as $\tau^{-\gamma}$ with $0 < \gamma < 1$.
- Prices are **diffusive**, with volatility proportional to the square-root law's amplitude.

Concave decaying impact should make prices mean-revert. The classical resolution is the [[concepts/propagator-model|propagator model]], where long-memory order flow and decaying impact balance to give diffusion.

[[sources/maitrier-2026-square-root-impact-framework|Maitrier & Bouchaud (2025)]] show that with a *transient* square-root impact, the heavy-tailed distribution of metaorder durations is **not sufficient** — it still gives sub-diffusion. Diffusion is restored only when the signs of *different* metaorders are long-range correlated, $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon(t)\varepsilon(t+\tau)] \sim \tau^{-\gamma_\times}$, yielding diffusion at $\gamma_\times \simeq 1 - 2\beta$.

That is a substantive claim, because it reopens a debate widely thought closed. If correlation *between* metaorders is essential, then order splitting alone does not account for the long memory of order flow, and some form of herding — shared signals, market makers unwinding inventory, or traders anticipating each other — is doing part of the work.

## Consequences for Volatility

Because diffusion can be recovered purely from superposed metaorders with transient impact, the framework predicts impact amplitude proportional to volatility as an *output* rather than an assumption. Maitrier & Bouchaud go further: they derive what an exogenous "fundamental" volatility component would do to the correlation between returns and volume-weighted imbalance, and find the observed structure does not support it. Their reading is that a substantial fraction of volatility is mechanically generated by trading.

## Two Refinements

**The double square-root law.** Peak impact follows a square root in executed volume, *and* the instantaneous impact of an individual child order scales as $\sqrt{q}$ — established by Maitrier, Loeper, Kanazawa & Bouchaud on Tokyo Stock Exchange data with real trader identifiers.

**The prefactor is measurable without identifiers.** [[sources/maitrier-2025-artificial-market-generator|Maitrier, Loeper & Bouchaud (2025)]] confirm inside a simulated market that proxy metaorders built from anonymous trades reproduce the law *and* its prefactor $Y$ — which matters, since $Y$ sets actual impact cost and is rarely studied.

## Open

No explanation is settled. The Latent Liquidity Theory captures much of the phenomenology but appears inconsistent with some Tokyo Stock Exchange findings. Maitrier & Bouchaud explicitly decline to derive the law, taking it as empirical input and studying its consequences instead.
